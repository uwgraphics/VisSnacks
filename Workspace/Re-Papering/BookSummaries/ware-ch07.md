# Ware, "Visual and Verbal Narrative" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 7)

**What it is:** This chapter examines how visual and verbal (spoken/signed/written) modes of communication differ in their underlying "logic" and how they combine to convey narrative — the sequence of ideas an audience is meant to follow over time. It builds on the perceptual and pattern-finding material of the book's preceding chapters (3, 5, and 6) and sets up the next chapter's discussion of predictive mental models, using examples ranging from sign language and PowerPoint slides to cinematography, cartoons, and infographic posters.

**AI summary:** Ware opens by dismantling the "picture is worth a thousand words" cliché with a deliberately awkward test case — try drawing "If halibut is more than ten euros a kilo at Good Food, go to the fish market on 5th Street" — to argue the real design question isn't images-versus-words but *when each is most effective and how to combine them*. He uses sign language as a test case for what's "fundamentally visual": sign languages (ASL, BSL, the recently emerged Nicaraguan sign language) are full natural languages invented by deaf people themselves, not translations of spoken language, with their own grammars, a critical period for acquisition (as with spoken language and the *enfants sauvage* cases), and processing in the same Broca's/Wernicke's brain areas used for speech — even though signs must first pass through visual pattern-finding machinery. This shows the visual/verbal divide is not about auditory-vs-visual channels but about **symbolization and logic**.

On symbolization: natural language (spoken or signed) rests on the *principle of arbitrariness* — words and most signs bear no resemblance to their referents (nothing "dog"-like about "dog"). Visual thinking, by contrast, is grounded in *pattern perception*, which is not arbitrary or socially agreed but partly innate, partly learned through interacting with the world. Visual designs are almost always hybrids along this spectrum: emoji-like symbols (♥ ♪ €) are essentially words; realist paintings are almost purely pattern-based; diagrams sit in the middle, mixing arbitrary conventions with meaningful spatial patterns.

On logic: natural language carries "if," "and," "but," "otherwise," "nevertheless" — qualifiers enabling abstract, conditional reasoning (also the basis of programming language syntax). Visual/diagrammatic logic is instead the logic of pattern, object, and space — relations like "inside," "on top of," "connected to," "passes through." Ware illustrates this with the 1980s flowchart-versus-pseudocode history: flowcharts, despite being called a "visual language," were largely words in diamond boxes and turned out to be an expensive, failed detour — "hundreds of thousands of dollars were spent documenting computer programs in this way, only for the results to languish on shelves never to be consulted." Pseudocode, closer to natural-language conditional structure, won out. He reinforces the point with the Edgy Media company-structure example: a diagram instantly reveals hierarchy and answers structural queries ("who does Fan report to?") that the equivalent verbal paragraph obscures.

The chapter then turns to how words and images link moment-to-moment via **deixis** — pointing gestures ("put that there," from Richard Bolt's 1980 MIT Media Lab work) that let ambiguous words like "that" and "there" acquire meaning only in combination with a gesture. This grounds Ware's critique of PowerPoint practice: slides should carry images/diagrams with minimal essential text, narration should come from the speaker, and a visible deictic pointer (laser or simulated) should link speech to image — a discipline he says is "often poorly designed," citing bullet-listing and word-for-word slide/speech overlap as the "worst" failure mode because it causes audiences to read ahead and mentally check out before the speaker arrives. He also notes rooms are typically dimmed for slide visibility at the cost of leaving the speaker's own gestures invisible (contrasted with Al Gore's well-lit delivery in *An Inconvenient Truth*).

A discussion of **mirror neurons** (Rizzolatti's 1990s discovery, triggered accidentally when a monkey's neuron fired both for its own reaching and for watching a grad student lick an ice cream cone) frames empathic, pre-linguistic communication as the substrate beneath narrative — mirror neurons fire in the same pathways used to execute an action, potentially explaining how facial expressions (Ekman's seven basic emotions) transmit felt emotion.

The heart of the chapter is the **cognitive thread**: the sequence of concepts held in visual/verbal working memory plus the links between them, which an effective presentation must capture and roughly align with its audience's cognitive threads. Narrative structure is framed as establishing–elaborating–resolving a problem, at every scale from a single exchange to a whole film. Ware discusses several concrete narrative mechanisms: Q&A patterns (Steven Katz's three-shot cinematographic sequence — two seemingly unrelated shots followed by a resolving third, illustrated with the woman-carrying-parcels/man-collide cliché); visual framing (the camera frame as an attention-control device, illustrated with *The Graduate* storyboards showing how shot sequencing forces the viewer's attention from Benjamin's father to Benjamin to Mrs. Robinson); depth-of-focus as a way to suppress distracting background; FINSTs (Zenon Pylyshyn's term, "fingers of instantiation" — up to four objects the brain can track peripherally while fixating one, illustrated with the playground/crowded-scene example, relevant to cinematographers and game designers managing chaotic scenes); and shot transitions, including the establishing-shot-then-focus-shot convention, which work because perception itself is already discrete/saccadic (frame-by-frame), so the brain is well-suited to jump cuts if they're designed to avoid disorientation.

He extends this to cartoons (action lines as a hybrid arbitrary/motivated symbol showing an object's recent path and implied future position, tied to the brain's constant short-term prediction and "exception" signals when expected motion is violated — citing Jeff Hawkins's *On Intelligence*), mixed-mode web/blog narratives with drill-down links (citing Segel & Heer 2010 on narrative visualization categories), and single-frame narrative diagrams (the Gulf Islands Waterscapes water-conservation poster, where arrow sequences impose a reading order on an otherwise static infographic).

The conclusion ranks narrative "strength" by degree of control over attention: movies and animated cartoons exert the most control (an "inescapable narrative train"); graphic/strip cartoons exert intermediate control (ordered frames but allowing random access); static diagrams, pictures, and photographs convey the weakest, most optional sense of narrative, requiring the viewer to actively discover it.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The visual/verbal distinction isn't about eyes-versus-ears (sign language proves that) — it's about arbitrary, socially-agreed symbols with conditional grammar (words) versus non-arbitrary spatial/relational pattern logic (diagrams: inside, connected-to, on-top-of). Most real designs are hybrids, and the design question is which relationships to express which way.
- The flowchart-versus-pseudocode history is a cautionary tale directly relevant to diagram design: forcing logic that's naturally verbal/conditional ("if," "while") into a visual notation can be strictly worse than just writing it in words, even when the result is technically a "diagram."
- Deixis (linking speech to image via pointing/gaze) is the connective tissue between verbal narration and visual content — this is the theoretical basis for concrete presentation advice: minimal-text slides, narration carried by the speaker, and a visible pointer/highlighter tying words to the exact part of the image being discussed.
- The "cognitive thread" concept gives a vocabulary for narrative design: designing a presentation or visualization means engineering what a viewer attends to, in what order, so their momentary sequence of concepts approximates the one the author intends — versus information-seeking/browsing tasks, where the viewer's thread is self-directed and highly individual.
- FINSTs (max ~4 tracked objects) and depth-of-focus give concrete, numeric constraints for how many simultaneous moving/highlighted elements a visualization or animation can expect a viewer to track, and how to suppress unwanted attention-grabbing in a busy scene.
- Narrative strength is a spectrum designers can choose deliberately: movies/animation (strongest control) > cartoon strips (ordered but random-access) > static diagrams/photos (weakest, viewer-discovered) — useful for calibrating how much "narrative" a given static chart or dashboard can realistically deliver versus needing animation or sequence.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The opening sign-language material (pages 125–127) is more linguistics background than design-actionable and can be skimmed once the core point lands (visual ≠ auditory; the real split is arbitrary-symbol-plus-grammar vs. pattern-logic). The flowchart/pseudocode section and the Edgy Media diagram example are worth reading closely — they're the clearest, most reusable illustrations of visual vs. verbal logic. The PowerPoint/deixis section and mirror-neuron section are short and worth reading in full for direct presentation-design advice. The core of the chapter — cognitive thread, Q&A patterns, visual framing, FINSTs, shot transitions, cartoons, and the narrative-strength conclusion (pages 134–141) — is the essential material for a data-vis course and should be read carefully.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The opening test sentence: "If halibut is more than ten euros a kilo at Good Food, go to the fish market on 5th Street" — used to show conditional logic resists visual expression.
- Pylyshyn's FINST research: people can track a maximum of four moving objects at once ("fingers of instantiation"); under normal (non-lab) conditions, we likely track only one or two objects beyond the one we're focally attending.
- Richard Bolt's 1980 "put that there" — a phrase whose two pointing gestures are required for "that" and "there" to have referents at all; foundational example of deixis.
- Rizzolatti's mirror-neuron discovery: a monkey's motor-cortex neuron, wired to fire when the monkey reached for an object, fired identically when a grad student merely raised an ice cream cone to his own lips — an accidental discovery in the early 1990s.
- Flowcharts as "a very expensive mistake": mandated for large commercial software projects in the 1980s, they cost "hundreds of thousands of dollars" in documentation effort that then went unused, because pseudocode was simply easier to read.
- Katz's three-shot Q&A cinematographic pattern (two ambiguous shots + one resolving shot) and the concrete cliché example of a woman and man walking toward each other who collide, spilling parcels.
- The chapter's closing hierarchy of narrative strength: movies/cartoons (strongest, "inescapable narrative train") > graphic cartoon strips (intermediate, ordered-but-random-access) > diagrams/photos (weakest, viewer must "actively discover" the narrative).

**Caveats:** The chapter draws heavily on cinematography craft manuals (Katz) and older neuroscience (Rizzolatti-era mirror neuron claims, which have since attracted significant skepticism and are not as settled a finding as this text presents them), and much of its causal narrative about mirror neurons and empathy is speculative — this is presented with the era's confidence but should be flagged as one interpretation, not consensus. The Segel & Heer 2010 typology and the PowerPoint critique are already somewhat dated relative to current narrative-visualization scholarship and presentation tooling, though the underlying design principles (minimal on-slide text, deixis, avoiding word-for-word slide/speech overlap) remain sound. The chapter's central dichotomy (arbitrary/grammatical language-logic vs. non-arbitrary pattern-logic) is a clarifying simplification for teaching purposes but, as Ware himself notes, most real artifacts are hybrids — students shouldn't over-read it as a strict binary when classifying real-world diagrams.
