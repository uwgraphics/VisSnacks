# Munzner, "Arrange Tables" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 7)

**What it is:** The chapter that spends the top-ranked channel — spatial position — on table data: a systematic tour of how choices about expressing, separating, ordering, and aligning in space generate the familiar statistical charts (bars, scatterplots, heatmaps, parallel coordinates, pies) as points in one design space.

**AI summary:** The **arrange** design choice "is the most crucial visual encoding choice because the use of space dominates the user's mental model of the dataset" — a direct payoff of chapter 5's channel rankings. Figure 7.1 lays out the design space: **express** values directly with spatial position; **separate, order, and align** regions; choose **axis orientation** (rectilinear, parallel, radial); and choose **layout density** (dense, space-filling). The organizing distinction for tables is **keys versus values**: a key is an independent attribute that indexes items; a value is a dependent cell attribute. Counting keys generates idioms: zero keys → scatterplots (two quantitative values expressed with both position channels); one key → bar/dot/line charts (list alignment); two keys → heatmaps (matrix alignment); many keys → recursive subdivision. Each idiom gets a compact what/why/how/scale analysis table.

The separate/order/align trio deserves attention: separation must be by a *categorical* attribute (expressiveness again — categorical data gets regions, not positions along an ordered axis), while ordering and alignment must use an *ordered* attribute. Small choices carry weight: ordering bars by the data value instead of alphabetically reveals patterns that a name-ordered chart hides (Fig. 7.4); in stacked bars only the bottom segment and total sit on a common scale; line charts must not be used with categorical keys, because connection marks imply trends — Zacks & Tversky's subjects read a gender line chart as "the more male a person is, the taller he/she is" (Fig. 7.9). A line chart's **aspect ratio** matters too: **banking to 45°** picks ratios that put informative slopes near the diagonal, where our angle discrimination is best (the sunspot data of Fig. 7.10 tells two different stories at ratios 4 and 22).

For axis orientation, Munzner contrasts rectilinear layouts (the default, highest precision) with **parallel** layouts — parallel coordinates, which trade the scatterplot's two-attribute limit for dozens of axes, showing correlation as parallel segments (positive) or a single crossing point (negative), at the cost of training time and pairwise-only patterns — and **radial** layouts, where the angle channel is both less accurately perceived and inherently cyclic. This grounds the famous pie-chart analysis: pies encode with angle/area judgements, bars with high-accuracy aligned position (Fig. 7.17); pies' one genuine virtue is showing part–whole relations, but a **normalized stacked bar chart** does that with a more accurate channel. Radial layouts are better only for genuinely periodic data (Fig. 7.19). Finally, **dense** layouts (e.g., the Tarantula software-coverage view, one pixel-line per line of code) and **space-filling** layouts trade legibility of individual items for information density — with the caveat that space-filling designs forfeit **white space**.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- Standard charts aren't a zoo of separate species: bar, scatter, line, heatmap, pie, SPLOM, and parallel coordinates are all systematic combinations of a few arrangement choices applied to keys and values.
- The key/value question ("how many keys does this table have?") is the first thing to ask when choosing an arrangement.
- Ordering regions is a free, high-leverage design choice: order by the data, not the alphabet, unless lookup-by-name is the task.
- Line charts assert trend relationships; use them only when the key is ordered. (The Zacks & Tversky result is the memorable evidence.)
- The pie chart argument in its honest form: it's about angle vs. aligned-position channel accuracy, and there's a legitimate part-to-whole use case with better alternatives.
- Every idiom has scalability limits (bars: dozens–hundreds of levels; scatterplots: hundreds of items; heatmaps: a million items but only 3–11 color bins; parallel coordinates: hundreds of lines) — "how big can this get?" is part of choosing an idiom.

**Caveats:** Vocabulary is idiosyncratic in places: "key/value" is Munzner's database-flavored framing (elsewhere: independent/dependent variables), and her "dot chart" deliberately differs from Wilkinson's "dot plot" (she flags this in a sidebar). The idiom analysis tables presuppose the what–why–how framework from her earlier chapters; an isolated reader may find them cryptic. Scalability numbers assume a circa-2014 1000×1000-pixel display — treat them as orders of magnitude, not specs. The cluster heatmap example's red–green colormap is described as a genomics convention and explicitly flagged as bad for colorblind readers — don't misread the figure as an endorsement. Streamgraphs and Tarantula are somewhat dated system examples, though the design points stand.
