# Ware, "What We Can Easily See" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 2)

**What it is:** This chapter lays out the theory of low-level, pre-attentive visual processing that determines what a viewer can find at a glance versus what requires slow, effortful search — and translates that theory directly into design rules for making graphic symbols "easy to find." It builds on Chapter 1's account of eye movements and executable memories, and sets up Chapter 4's later treatment of color.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the thief-with-a-flashlight metaphor: visual attention moves a narrow spotlight point to point, and most information about the world is only picked up when directly fixated — except for a few things that "shout" loudly enough to be sensed in the periphery. The chapter's driving question is what makes something easy to find, and the answer starts in the neurophysiology of the primary visual cortex (V1) and V2, mapped via single-neuron recording pioneered by Hubel and Wiesel (Nobel Prize, 1981). V1 and V2 are described as tapestries of interlocking regions that process color, form/orientation, motion, and stereoscopic depth in parallel, "far more complex and powerful than anything humans have built to date" — more powerful, Ware says, than weather-forecasting supercomputers. V1/V2 output feeds two downstream systems: the *what* pathway (object identity — "a chicken, a Volkswagen, or Aunt Mabel") and the *where* pathway (spatial location, used to plan eye movements and reaching).

The mechanism that connects low-level features to eye-movement planning is *biased competition*: searching for tomatoes issues an instruction like "all you red-sensitive cells in V1, shout louder," and the loudest-shouting red patch gets the next fixation. This is illustrated with the classic p/q search task — the two highlighted p's pop out instantly while the q's take roughly ten times longer to find, even though readers have thousands of hours of experience with both letterforms, because pop-out depends on low-level feature geometry (the p's mirror-image match), not familiarity.

Anne Triesman's visual search experiments are covered in detail: when a target differs from all distracters by a single low-level feature (color, orientation, size, motion, and to a lesser degree convexity/concavity), response time is flat regardless of distracter count — evidence of parallel, automatic processing. Triesman called this "pre-attentive," a term Ware argues is a misnomer since her subjects were paid to concentrate hard on a known target; he proposes *tunable* instead — properties the eye-movement system can be biased toward, not properties seen without any attention. Inattentional blindness (Mack and Rock) is cited as the flip side: without knowing the target in advance, even fairly blatant things are missed. Pop-out requires sufficient feature contrast (a rule-of-thumb 30-degree orientation difference is given) and depends on background homogeneity — the more the background varies within a channel, the bigger the difference needed to stand out. Crucially, *conjunctions* of two features (e.g., "green AND square") do not pop out — the three green squares example demonstrates that the visual cortex can tune for shape or for color, but not both simultaneously; conjunctive targets require slow, serial, attentive search up the *what* pathway.

Ware extends this into *feature channels* — separate, near-independent processing streams (color, orientation/size, motion) that can each be tuned independently. This lets designers support multiple simultaneous visual queries by mapping different variables to different channels, illustrated with a GDP/population-growth scatterplot that also encodes literacy via shape and urbanization via color, and a town/megastore map example using color, 3D shading/cast shadows, and bold outline shapes as independently tunable channels. He notes hard limits: roughly 8-10 independently searchable symbols is a practical ceiling since each channel offers only about three usable steps (three sizes, three orientations, etc.), and enhancements are asymmetric — increasing size/contrast or adding a part is more effective than the corresponding decrease. Learning does not help complex conjunctive search (the "hidden 6" among digits, ~2 seconds, versus a hardwired-feature dot found ~10x faster; individual faces in a crowd don't pop out even for someone searching for a relative), because expertise operates after fixation, not in directing where the eye goes next.

Motion gets its own section as a uniquely powerful channel, tied to evolutionary predator-detection sensitivity in the visual periphery; Hillstrom and Yantis's finding that *emergence* (not just movement) most powerfully triggers the orienting response is highlighted, along with Ware's own prior work using motion as a "human interrupt" signal, with the caveat that rapid/blinking motion is hard to suppress and is "one of the worst forms of visual pollution" when used gratuitously (Times Square is offered as the extreme case).

The chapter closes on visual search strategy: the *move-and-scan loop*, *eye-movement control loop*, and *pattern-testing loop* (each fixation runs 1-4 tests at ~1/20 second each), a *detection field* that grows with distance from fixation (illustrated by a five-fixation search sequence), a *lateral interparietal*-area memory that inhibits revisiting 4-6 recently fixated locations, and the value of multiscale visual structure (clusters of clusters) for efficient hierarchical search, closing with the idea that designs conforming to learned scan patterns (like reading order) are searched efficiently while those that violate stereotype cause frustration.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Pop-out is real and neurally grounded, but only for single-feature differences on one of a handful of channels (color/hue, lightness, size, elongation, orientation, motion, spatial grouping) — conjunctions of two features do not pop out and force slow serial search.
- To make several kinds of information each independently searchable in one display, map each to a *different* channel (color for one variable, shape/orientation for another, size for a third) rather than stacking distinctions onto a single channel.
- There's a practical ceiling of roughly 8-10 independently searchable symbol types in a static display, because each channel only supports about three discriminable steps.
- Feature differences must be large enough to matter (rule of thumb: ~30 degrees of orientation difference) and "enough" depends on how much the background already varies in that channel — a busy, heterogeneous background raises the bar for standing out.
- Learning/expertise does not rescue conjunctive or complex search — pattern recognition expertise only helps after the eye lands on something, not in guiding the eye there in the first place, so designers can't assume "practiced users" will find things faster in a badly-coded display.
- Motion/blinking is the strongest attention-grabber available and should be used sparingly and deliberately (e.g., a rare, self-terminating signal for a genuinely urgent alert), not as decoration — gratuitous motion is "visual pollution."

**Skim/skip guidance:** The core material — the p/q demo, biased competition, Triesman's pop-out findings and the pre-attentive/tunable distinction, the conjunctive-search examples (three green squares, inverted-T, feature-space diagrams), and the "Lessons for Design" / channels section — is essential and directly usable for lecture. The neuroanatomy detail (V1 layer structure, cortical layer numbers, the "billions of neurons" and parallel-computer analogy, What/Where pathway brain diagrams) is good color but skimmable if time is short; the closing visual-search-strategy section (move-and-scan/eye-movement/pattern-testing loops, detection field, lateral interparietal area) is interesting but more about search behavior generally than about design guidance, and could be trimmed for a design-focused course.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The p/q search demo: finding two highlighted p's among random letters is easy; finding two q's in the same field takes roughly ten times longer and disrupts one's train of thought.
- Hubel and Wiesel's single-neuron probing of V1 won a Nobel Prize in 1981.
- A "hidden 6" among a block of digits takes about 2 seconds to find; by contrast a distinct dot pops out roughly ten times faster, in a single glance — "The features that pop out are hardwired in the brain, not learned."
- Rule of thumb: pop-out from orientation contrast typically needs about a 30-degree difference.
- Practical limit: only about 8-10 symbols can be made independently pop-out searchable in a static display, since each channel offers roughly three usable difference-steps (three sizes, three orientations, three motion frequencies, etc.).
- A pop-out object can be distinguished from its surroundings in less than a tenth of a second, versus eye movements occurring at roughly three per second (and up to several seconds) for non-pop-out search.
- Individual faces in a crowd do not pop out — even a sibling's face requires serial search — but a uniquely colored item (the one yellow jacket) is found in a single fixation.
- Between four and six recently-visited fixation locations are retained (thought to involve the lateral interparietal area) to prevent the eye from re-visiting the same spot.

**Caveats:** Ware is explicit that "pre-attentive" is a misleading term for Triesman's findings — a point worth preserving in any summary rather than repeating the more common textbook shorthand that these effects require "no attention at all." The 8-10-symbol ceiling and the 30-degree rule of thumb are heuristics, not precise thresholds, and shouldn't be over-quoted as hard numbers. The chapter's real-world photographic examples (the crowd/yellow-jacket photo, Times Square) are illustrative rather than data from controlled studies, and the "megastore map" and GDP scatterplot are constructed teaching examples, not published empirical results — treat them as design illustrations rather than evidence. Some cited findings (e.g., convexity/concavity pop-out) are flagged by Ware himself as not yet tied to a known V1 mechanism, so shouldn't be presented as fully settled neuroscience.
