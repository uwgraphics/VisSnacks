# Ware, "Building Mental Models: Why We Present with Visualizations" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 8)

**What it is:** This chapter builds a theory of *why* presentations (talks, slide decks, reports with charts and images) work at all, grounding it in "predictive cognition" — the idea that the brain's fundamental job is prediction, and that memory, planning, and communication are all downstream of that. It then works through a series of extended, mostly first-person examples (a seaweed-ecology research program, assembly diagrams, UV-radiation charts, advertising imagery, maps) to show how different visualization types — photographs, cartoons, animations, charts, maps, narrative diagrams — each contribute something distinct to building an audience's "executable mental model." It functions as the book's theoretical capstone for presentation design, following earlier chapters on gist, memory, and narrative.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the question of why anyone gives a presentation at all: not merely to inform, but ultimately to change behavior, "and it is action that counts." He introduces *predictive cognition* as an "all-encompassing theory about what cognition is for, what memory is for, and what communication is about," citing Andy Clark's 2013 survey as background. The core claim is that brains are fundamentally future-predicting machines — from millisecond-scale motor predictions (a hand movement's simulated consequence, checked against touch-sensor feedback) to day-to-day driving, where "an exception is triggered" only when something violates the predictive model (a car veering out of its lane), pulling the situation into conscious attention.

From this, Ware builds outward to memory. *Episodic memory* is reframed as a constructive process — "scenario construction" — rather than a replay, and predictive cognition holds that predicting the future and remembering the past are "essentially the same process," differing only in starting point. He cites Mahr and Csibra's (2015) argument that remembering the past is a secondary function whose primary evolutionary purpose is to let us *communicate* executable mental models to others via language — a claim Ware extends to human evolution generally (cognitive functions developing over the 50,000–150,000-year hunter-gatherer period to support transmitting survival knowledge) and, in a striking aside, to AI/robotics research, where "predictive planning is not enough" — robots also need to explain their reasoning to handlers.

The *long-term memory* section makes the point that roughly 95% of what we consciously perceive is "not what is 'out there' but what is already in our heads" — illustrated by the classic observation that spouses often fail to notice each other's new hairstyles or shaved beards. Long-term memory isn't a separate storage bin; the same neurons (e.g., in V4, inferotemporal cortex) both process and store. The hippocampus is critical to consolidating new executable memories (illustrated via amnesia patients who cannot recognize that a relative has only briefly left the room), and memory formation requires either an internal cognitive reward (successfully interpreting a bar chart) or an external one (merging smoothly onto a freeway, getting a smile from a colleague). A short section on *priming* (long-term/implicit memory, "perceptual facilitation") explains why designers and artists often "prime" themselves by reviewing relevant images for a day or two before a creative push.

The chapter's practical core is the translation of "know your audience" into: "Know and understand your audience's mental models and how they may be adapted and extended." Concretely this means (1) explaining unfamiliar chart types explicitly, since "the inventors of creative and innovative visualizations frequently think their designs are as transparent and understandable to the audience as they are to themselves" — usually false; (2) preferring conventional forms (maps, bar charts, node-link diagrams) so "the medium should not dominate the message"; (3) pitching content "somewhere below the median level of understanding" while linking to broader issues of interest; and (4) leveraging *animate agents* — citing Goldin-Meadow et al.'s (2008) finding that people naturally comprehend events via an agent-patient-act order, so visualizations that cast data in terms of active agents (predators, invaders, causal actors) are more readily assimilated, since human cognition is built on agent-based, spatially metaphorical foundations (Pinker's *The Stuff of Thought* is footnoted).

The bulk of the chapter is Ware's extended seaweed case study (joint work with ecologist Jenn Dijkstra on invasive-seaweed habitat structure), used to walk through visualization types one by one: photographs (a diver sampling seaweed, plus close-up and microscope images) that "have an incomparable ability to enrich mental models" in ways bare declarative sentences cannot; a cartoon of a hungry fish and a mesh "sieve" to explain the *spatial sieve* concept (how seaweed fronds let small prey through but block larger predators); an animated PowerPoint "spherical space analysis" showing inaccessible refuge volume; and a complex multi-panel scatterplot from a research paper, shown in full but with only two data points highlighted by arrows (~30,000 animals/m² under invasive *Dasysiphonia* vs. ~100/m² under kelp) — deliberately including the "unexplained" surrounding complexity so the audience's mental model includes "that there is much more information and depth of understanding to be gained by reading the research paper."

Other sections: *narrative assembly diagrams*, drawing on Heiser, Phan, Agrawala, Tversky, and Hanrahan's (2004) Stanford study of furniture-assembly instructions, which identified four cognitive design principles — clear sequence of operations, clearly identifiable components (consistent viewpoint, transparency where needed), consistent spatial layout across frames, and illustrated actions/connections (arrows for placement, dotted lines for fasteners) — and found diagrams built on these principles outperformed manufacturer instructions and even outperformed video, because diagrams support both narrative-order reading and free out-of-order lookup, while "navigating through time in a video sequence is much more difficult." A section on combining figurative and non-figurative elements uses Byrne, Angus, and Wiles' (2019) three-panel UV-radiation bar charts (plain bars → bars with weather icons → a ray-traced figure showing a human absorbing direct and cloud-scattered UV), which counterintuitively shows broken cloud producing more UV exposure than clear sky, with lines that "have a figurative interpretation as rays from the sun which interact with clouds, but also have an abstract meaning as one-tenth of a unit of UV-B radiation received by the human recipient."

Ware also treats visualizations as *advertising* — using a "heavily doctored" Hubble Pillars/Jets image to argue that NASA's goal there is to promote a mental model of "important science" to funders and the public, not to convey results to other scientists — "it is often the case that visualization designers think they are being asked to help with the science of a project, whereas, in fact, they are being asked to simply show it off." A Seabed 2030 ocean-mapping globe (showing <20% of oceans mapped as of 2020) exemplifies maps that simultaneously advertise a mission and function as an actionable planning tool ("any dark gray area is in need of mapping"). Finally, *narrative tension and plot development* discusses how nonfiction presentations borrow fiction's plot structure — introduction (partial activation of mental models, hints rather than reveals), middle (elaboration, possibly multiple competing hypotheses), and resolution — illustrated by a TV documentary about the 2004 Sumatra tsunami that built tension around two competing causal theories (fault uplift vs. submarine landslide) before resolving in favor of uplift. Ware notes written papers front-load their conclusions (in the abstract) so there's little room for tension, but slide presentations benefit from delayed resolution, and multi-threaded narratives carry real costs — "switching context can have a substantial cost" — so thread switches must be clearly signaled.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- A presentation's job is to change the audience's *executable mental model*, not just transmit facts — so before choosing a chart type, ask what mental model you want the audience to walk away simulating, not just what data you want to show them.
- Never assume your audience understands a novel visualization the way you do — "inventors of creative and innovative visualizations frequently think their designs are as transparent and understandable to the audience as they are to themselves." When you go beyond conventional maps/bar charts/node-link diagrams, budget time to explain the chart's mechanics, or the medium will dominate the message.
- Photographs, cartoons, and diagrams aren't just illustration — they do different cognitive work than prose or numbers alone (compare the seaweed photo, the predator-sieve cartoon, and the spherical-space-analysis animation, each targeting a different piece of the mental model).
- It's fine — even useful — to show a complex, only-partially-explained chart (like the full multi-panel seaweed scatterplot) if the goal includes signaling that there's more rigor and depth behind the two points you actually discuss.
- Multiframe diagrams beat video for teaching a procedure (assembly instructions) because readers can jump around and re-check any step; video forces a fixed timeline that gets the viewer's mental model "out of sync." This is a concrete, testable design principle (Heiser/Tversky et al.) for any tutorial content.
- Distinguish visualization-for-understanding from visualization-for-advertising (the Hubble image, the Seabed 2030 globe) — both are legitimate design goals but call for different choices, and conflating them is a common failure mode when a designer thinks they've been asked for "the science" but have actually been asked to make something look impressive.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The theoretical spine — predictive cognition, episodic memory as scenario construction, long-term memory, and priming (pp. 143–148) — is worth reading once for the framing but can be skimmed on a re-read; the payoff is the single boxed guideline ("Know and understand your audience's mental models...") and its four implications. The real teaching content is the extended examples from "Analyzing Seaweed" through "Narrative Tension" (pp. 149–161): these are concrete, example-driven, and easy to lift into a course session on presentation/report design. The assembly-diagram principles and the UV figurative/non-figurative panel are the most directly reusable, self-contained sub-sections if time is short.

**Memorable specifics:**

- "It is often the case that visualization designers think they are being asked to help with the science of a project, whereas, in fact, they are being asked to simply show it off" (advertising vs. understanding, re: the doctored Hubble Pillars and Jets image).
- The seaweed abundance numbers: ~30,000 small animals per square meter of seafloor under invasive *Dasysiphonia*, vs. only ~100/m² under kelp — the two points singled out by arrows in an otherwise unexplained complex scatterplot.
- As of early 2020, less than 20% of the world's oceans had been mapped, per the Seabed 2030 globe visualization discussed.
- Perhaps 95% of what we consciously perceive is not what is "out there" but what is already stored in long-term memory — explaining why spouses often don't notice a new hairstyle or a shaved-off beard.
- Heiser, Phan, Agrawala, Tversky, and Hanrahan's four cognitive principles for assembly diagrams: clear operation sequence, clearly identifiable/visible components, consistent spatial layout across frames, and illustrated actions (arrows for placement, dotted lines for fasteners) — diagrams built this way beat both manufacturer instructions and video demonstrations.
- The UV-radiation figure's caption line: "The lines have a figurative interpretation as rays from the sun which interact with clouds, but also have an abstract meaning as one-tenth of a unit of UV-B radiation received by the human recipient" — and the counterintuitive finding that broken cloud can produce more harmful UV exposure than clear sky.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The three-panel UV-radiation figure (plain bar chart → bar chart with weather icons → ray-traced figurative/abstract hybrid diagram; p. 156–157, from Byrne, Angus & Wiles 2019) is an excellent, self-contained teaching example of combining figurative and abstract encoding in one graphic, and of a counterintuitive data finding (broken cloud > clear sky for UV exposure) made intuitive by the visualization.
- The furniture assembly-diagram sequence (p. 155, six numbered frames) is a clean, classroom-ready example of narrative diagram principles — sequence, component visibility, consistent layout, illustrated actions/fasteners — that generalizes well beyond furniture to any step-by-step instructional visualization.
- The predator-fish/spatial-sieve cartoon (p. 152) is a good minimal example of how a simple cartoon can stand in for and explain an abstract structural concept ("spatial sieve") before the technical/quantitative visualization is introduced.
- The four-panel scatterplot with only two arrow-highlighted points (p. 154) is a reusable teaching case for "it's OK to show complexity you don't fully explain" — useful for discussing how much detail to include in a presentation chart versus a paper figure.

**Caveats:** The predictive-cognition framework is presented as settled theory ("a radical new theory... upends many traditional views") but it's one contested position within cognitive science, built heavily on a single citation (Clark 2013) and a couple of supporting papers (Mahr & Csibra 2015; Goldin-Meadow et al. 2008) — a course should flag it as Ware's preferred lens rather than consensus. The evolutionary hunter-gatherer dating (50,000–150,000 years) is speculative and stated without much evidentiary hedging. Much of the middle of the chapter is a first-person case study from Ware's own seaweed-ecology collaboration, which is vivid and pedagogically useful but idiosyncratic — readers shouldn't over-generalize the specific "goals for our presentations" list as a universal checklist. Finally, the chapter's examples are almost all about scientific/technical presentation contexts (academic talks, TV science documentaries, NASA funding); it says relatively little about interactive or exploratory visualization, dashboards, or non-presentation contexts, so it pairs best with other chapters (gist, narrative) rather than standing alone as general vis-design guidance.
