# Ware, "Structuring Two-Dimensional Space" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 3)

**What it is:** This chapter explains how the brain constructs two-dimensional patterns — contours, textures, groupings, and their higher-level semantic uses in design — out of the fragmented low-level features described in Chapter 2. It runs from the psychophysics of depth perception through the neuroscience of contour binding and object recognition (V1→V2→V4→IT) to a practical taxonomy of spatial metaphors and a set of worked examples (maps, line graphs, node-link diagrams, a news web page) analyzed as sets of "visual pattern queries."

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the idea of "2.5D space": the eye samples the up-down and sideways (image-plane) dimensions richly — about a million retinal locations refreshed in under a tenth of a second via eye movements — but gets only one depth (away) value per image-plane point, and getting a new depth sample requires walking to a new vantage point, which can take seconds to minutes. This asymmetry (he suggests "2.05 dimensions" is more accurate than "2.5") is why so much perceptual and design machinery is organized around the image plane rather than depth, and why pattern-finding is "the very essence of visual thinking."

The chapter then works through the neuroscience of pattern formation. Contour binding — combining fragmented V1 edge responses into continuous boundaries — is explained anthropomorphically: an edge-detecting neuron enters a "mutual admiration society" with neighboring neurons tuned to the same orientation and in-line position, exciting them while inhibiting differently-oriented neighbors, so that neurons along a real contour fire in unison while fragments are suppressed. A footnote warns against the "homunculus fallacy" — never say a higher mechanism simply "looks at" lower information; explanation requires an actual mechanism. The lateral occipital cortex (LOC) is identified as the site of a "generalized contour extraction mechanism" that binds silhouettes, color edges, texture boundaries, and motion boundaries into a common contour representation (citing Kourtzi & Kanwisher 2001) — which Ware uses to explain why simple line drawings are so effective: a pencil line looks nothing like a real edge but strongly activates this same generalized mechanism.

Texture regions are covered next: grain size, orientation, contrast, and curve-vs-straight are the primary dimensions that make textures pop apart, mirroring the V1 feature properties from Chapter 2; more complex textures (like T's vs. L's) do not segregate as easily. This leads to a discussion of visual interference ("like interferes with like") — text overlaid on a background sharing its feature granularity is hard to read, while low-contrast or dissimilar-granularity backgrounds interfere less — and of channel-based design for overlapping regions (e.g., a map showing both temperature zones and vegetation zones), where heterogeneous channels (color + texture + outline) let a viewer isolate any one region despite overlap.

The "what pathway" (V1→V2→V4→IT) is presented as a hierarchy of increasingly complex, increasingly experience-dependent, and increasingly spatially-invariant pattern detectors, illustrated by "The What Hierarchy" diagram. V4 encodes mid-complexity patterns (spikes, convexities/concavities, T- and X-junctions, per Pasupathy & Connor 2002) and tolerates moderate stretching/rotation of a pattern. IT neurons respond to highly specific learned categories (faces, letters, cars). Ware draws a universal/idiosyncratic distinction: V1 features are essentially the same across people (with rare exceptions like uncorrected childhood vision problems during the critical period), while V4/IT pattern vocabulary is built through individual experience — the basis of expert perceptual skill (an expert reader chunking whole words, a geologist or lighting designer seeing task-specific patterns). He connects this to the deep-learning breakthrough (LeCun, Bengio, Hinton 2015), noting artificial multilayer networks now show face/pattern-selective layers analogous to V1–V4–IT and had to learn low-level features first before higher levels could build on them.

A key design-relevant construct is the "apprehendable chunk": the amount of pattern that can be read into the brain in a single fixation (~1/10 second) without a sequence of eye movements. For unlearned patterns, this is roughly three feature components; for learned/expert patterns (like whole words), a single chunk can hold far more. Complex patterns require serial processing — "multi-chunk queries" broken into subqueries, each resolved by a separate fixation, with visual working memory (Chapter 6) tracking progress. Ware then covers spatial layout/grouping by proximity (points or shapes clustering at multiple simultaneous scales — a group of small dots is itself perceived as a large shape) and the special status of horizontal/vertical orientation (tied to gravity/posture and to the abundance of rectilinear structure in the built environment; humans detect tiny departures from exact vertical/horizontal far more sensitively than deviations from other angles).

The chapter's design section catalogs the basic pattern-forming devices — connecting contour, enclosing contour, proximity grouping, alignment, common color/texture region, common movement — and maps them to "natural semantics": e.g., a big shape means large quantity, physical attachment means "part of," enclosure means containment, connecting lines mean a relationship (with line thickness/color/texture encoding relationship strength or type). Ware invokes Lakoff's *Metaphors We Live By* to argue these spatial mappings aren't arbitrary design convention but tap into spatial metaphor structures fundamental to human reasoning generally. The chapter closes with a worked set of examples — a road map, an ocean current/temperature map, line graphs (including a dual-axis and an uncertainty-band version), a blurred news website, and two node-link diagrams — each analyzed as a specific set of "visual pattern queries" (e.g., "find an efficient route," "find where the strongest currents are located," "can the network function if node X fails?"), explicitly modeling the critical-analysis exercise: naming the query, describing how the design's patterns support or fail to support it, and diagnosing where the design could be improved.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The core method of the chapter — analyzing a graphic as a specific set of "visual pattern queries" and asking how well the design's patterns support each one — is a directly reusable critique technique for any visualization design course.
- Pattern-forming devices (connecting contour, enclosure, proximity, alignment, common color/texture region, common motion) map onto specific "natural semantics" (relationship, containment, similarity, part-of, magnitude) — a compact vocabulary for teaching how graphic encoding choices carry implicit meaning, independent of any legend.
- The distinction between universal, unlearned pattern perception (V1-level; roughly a 3-feature "apprehendable chunk" per fixation) and expertise-dependent, learned pattern perception (V4/IT) explains why novices and experts read the same chart differently, and why chunking/training changes what counts as "simple" in a display.
- Line drawings work because of a specific mechanism (the generalized contour/LOC binding of luminance, color, texture, and motion boundaries into one contour representation) — a concrete, teachable reason sketches and outline diagrams are so effective, not just a stylistic preference.
- "Like interferes with like": overlaying information using features similar to a background layer (in granularity, color, or feature type) actively degrades legibility; maximizing feature-level distinctiveness between layers is the design fix, directly relevant to annotation, labeling, and layered map/chart design.
- Multi-chunk visual queries force a sequence of fixations tracked in visual working memory — a reminder that complex diagrams impose real cognitive cost per additional chunk, motivating design choices that reduce the number of fixations needed for a task.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The 2.5D-space psychophysics opening (pp. 44–45) and the neuron-level "mutual admiration society" contour-binding mechanics (pp. 47–49) are good-to-read background but can be skimmed for the conclusions (2.5D asymmetry drives image-plane-centric design; contours form via mutual excitation/inhibition among aligned edge detectors) without dwelling on mechanism detail. The essential core for a design course is: the generalized contour concept and why line drawings work (p. 49–50), texture region discrimination and interference (pp. 50–51), the apprehendable chunk and serial/multi-chunk processing (pp. 55–56), the pattern-for-design vocabulary and semantic pattern mappings (pp. 58–60, with the two graphical-code/semantics tables), and the worked pattern-query examples (pp. 61–64), which model the analytical exercise directly. The deep-learning aside (p. 54) and the V4-hierarchy neuroscience citations are supporting color, not load-bearing for design practice.

**Memorable specifics:**

- "It would be more accurate to say that visual space has 2.05 dimensions" — Ware's sharpened version of the standard "2.5D" claim, with the reasoning: ~1 million points sampled richly in up-down/sideways per fixation vs. essentially one depth value per point, refreshed on a ten-to-hundred-times-slower timescale (eye movements vs. walking).
- The homunculus fallacy footnote: "It is never acceptable to simply say that a higher-level mechanism *looks at* the low-level information" — a crisp epistemic rule for evaluating any proposed perceptual/design mechanism.
- For unlearned patterns, the apprehendable chunk size is about three feature components per single eye fixation (~1/10 second read-in time).
- People can detect a line rotated only 3 degrees from vertical, and are "extremely sensitive" to small end-to-end misalignments of straight lines — the empirical basis for the special status of horizontal/vertical alignment in layout.
- The Kourtzi & Kanwisher (2001) finding that LOC neurons respond to object shape "irrespective of how the bounding contours are defined" (luminance, color, texture, or motion boundaries) is the empirical anchor for the "generalized contour" concept.
- Deep-learning aside: Hinton's insight that neural nets must learn low-level patterns before higher levels can be learned is called "one of the major breakthroughs that enabled computer AI to analyze patterns as well or better than humans," directly paralleled to the V1→V4→IT hierarchy.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The edge-detector "mutual admiration society" figures (p. 48, noisy-edge-vs-suppressed-fragments pair) are excellent for teaching contour binding via mutual excitation/inhibition — visually clear even divorced from the neuroscience narrative.
- The generalized-contour figure (p. 49, four different dog renderings — photo, silhouette, textured, line drawing — all converging on the same "generalized contour" node) is a strong, reusable illustration for explaining why line drawings and photos can convey "the same object" to the visual system.
- The texture-discrimination pairs (p. 50–51: orientation, grain size, curve-vs-straight, blur; and the hard-to-discriminate T/L, red-green-circle/rectangle, and oriented-spike examples) are ready-made teaching pairs for a lecture on preattentive texture segregation and its limits.
- The "Pattern for Design" and "Semantic Pattern Mappings" figures/tables (pp. 58–60) are the most directly reusable for a design course: they compactly tabulate graphical code → semantic meaning (small shapes = objects/nodes, connecting contour = relationship, enclosure = containment, size = magnitude, etc.) and could anchor a lecture segment or handout on encoding-to-meaning mappings.
- The worked pattern-query examples (pp. 61–64: road map, ocean current map, line-graph variants, news web page, node-link diagrams) are built explicitly as critique exercises with stated queries and design comments — several are usable near-verbatim as in-class critique prompts.

**Caveats:** This is a proof/draft PDF (uncorrected Elsevier proof, not final pagination or typesetting), so page numbers or minor wording could shift in the published version. The neuroscience — particularly the specific claims about LOC as *the* generalized contour mechanism, V4's mid-level pattern vocabulary, and the "critical period" claim about early pattern learning — is presented with appropriate hedging ("emerging evidence," "presumably") but a reader could easily over-read it as more settled than current vision science actually supports; treat the neural mechanism sections as a useful explanatory model rather than a definitive account. The "3-feature apprehendable chunk" figure is stated as a rough research-based estimate ("research suggest[s]"), not a precise universal constant, and shouldn't be quoted as an exact number in design guidance. The chapter's worked examples explicitly include some deliberately mediocre designs for critique purposes — students should not mistake the road map, temperature-graph, or news-page figures for exemplars of good design; several are flagged in the text itself as improvable.
