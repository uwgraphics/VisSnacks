# Ware, "Visual Objects, Words, and Meaning" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 6)

**What it is:** This chapter traces how the visual system turns retinal patterns into recognized objects and scenes, then meaning, and finally into action — moving from the inferotemporal (IT) cortex through visual and verbal working memory to attention and cognitive control. It sits in the book's arc as the bridge between the low-level pattern-processing machinery covered earlier (V1, V2, V4, lateral occipital cortex) and the higher-level cognitive/design implications the book builds toward, closing with a worked example (receiving a cup of coffee) and a set of design-relevant elaborations: making objects easy to identify, novelty and attention-capture, images as symbols/brands, meaning-emotion links, and imagery/desire.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the 1972 Nick Ut photograph of Phan Thi Kim Phuc to establish that images activate meaning in a fraction of a second — far faster than reading a sentence — which is why images are efficient carriers of certain kinds of information. The chapter then works through the "what" channel: retina→V1→V2→V4→IT, ending at the inferotemporal cortex, an area on each side of the brain (behind the temples) containing subregions specialized for faces (the fusiform gyrus), cars, houses, and other object classes, plus multimodal links to other sensory/language areas. Object and scene recognition is viewpoint-dependent: people are much better at identifying objects and rooms from familiar viewpoints, and as "a rule of thumb, we can rotate an object by about 20 degrees and scale it by a factor of two or three and still identify it rapidly." This works via "generalized views" — a handful of canonical views (full-face, three-quarters, profile) built from V4 patterns tolerant of some distortion, so objects are understood as "patterns of patterns," not matches to an idealized Platonic form.

Ware then presents Irving Biederman's geon theory as a competing/complementary account: the brain may also store 3D structural primitives ("geons" — cones, cylinders, etc.) plus a "structural skeleton" describing how they connect. Evidence for connection-based structure: objects are identified faster when views clearly reveal how parts attach (the teapot/hand silhouette example), and altering an attachment can make an object read as different or "bizarre" (the geon-problem figure with an arm from the stomach). Ware treats geon theory as "probably the most controversial" theory discussed, concluding the brain likely uses both 2D generalized views and higher-level structural analysis depending on object type.

A parallel and faster process is scene "gist": people categorize a scene ("a busy road," "a fast-food restaurant") in under a tenth of a second, as fast as identifying a single object — which is a problem for the traditional view that scenes are identified via their constituent objects. Ware cites Oliva and Torralba's work showing gist comes from global, low-spatial-frequency statistical image properties (e.g., beach scenes have a blue band on top, striated sea in the middle, beige sand with vertical shapes) — recognizable even from heavily blurred/averaged composites with no individual objects visible. Gist also biases object recognition: a blurred automobile on a street and a blurred bottle on a table can be perceptually identical blobs, disambiguated only by scene context (Torralba's priming work).

The chapter then turns to working memory. Visual working memory holds a "nexus" — a temporary binding of active visual patterns to nonvisual stored meaning — for a fraction of a second up to a few seconds, with capacity of roughly one to three objects/chunks at a time. Verbal working memory (Wernicke's and Broca's areas) holds about two seconds of speech, roughly three chunks, in an "echoic loop," and much of what we call "thinking" is internalized speech running in this store — which is why we can't think and listen well simultaneously. The prefrontal cortex governs attentional control and temporary bindings (quoting Miller and Cohen on cognitive control as "active maintenance of patterns of activity in the prefrontal cortex that represent goals and the means to achieve them"). Object selection is described as a "biased competition model": a bottom-up wave activates many candidate patterns in IT cortex in proportion to fit, then a top-down wave — biased by priming and task relevance — determines which one to three win and enter working memory, suppressing the rest. Potter and Levy's 1969 MIT RSVP (Rapid Serial Visual Presentation) experiment is cited: viewers could detect a target object (e.g., a dog) in images flashed at up to ten per second, though they retained almost nothing else from the sequence — establishing an upper bound on object-identification rate, with normal fixations lasting about one-fifth of a second and typically extracting one to three objects.

The "receiving a cup of coffee" example walks through a social scenario — reaching for a coffee mug from an acquaintance — showing simultaneous visual working memory objects (hand, handle, face), links to verbal working memory (a semantic schema of "being offered coffee," a gesture of hospitality) and motor working memory (the reach-and-grasp plan), describing the whole as "a loosely coupled network of temporarily active processes, a kind of dance of meaning."

Design-relevant elaborations follow: (1) objects as "patterns of patterns" means typical/canonical exemplars (a Labrador vs. a Daschund) are recognized faster, and canonical/joint-revealing poses (illustrated with Egyptian low-relief carvings) aid identification; (2) novelty-seeking is an innate drive (even babies show it), and designers/advertisers exploit "gist-object conflict" — mismatched scene and object content — to capture extra cognitive cycles, illustrated by Earthrise (Anders, 1968), Magritte and Dalí surrealism, and a Tim Flach puzzle image; (3) images function as symbols with automatically triggered associations (stop signs, brand logos), and brand value can hinge on this — Coca-Cola's brand is estimated at over ten billion dollars of the company's roughly thirty-three-billion valuation — with the Johnny Cash "Walk the Line" poster analyzed as a layered symbol system (guitar, finger-plate shape, flames, vertical bar); (4) meaning carries emotional valence, and without it "information will not be attended and therefore not processed" — a whale-ship-strike photo is offered as an example of emotive imagery driving action; (5) mental imagery is tied to desire via the "elaborated intrusion theory" (Kavanagh, Andrade, and May, 2005), where imagery gives short-term relief from a psychological deficit but heightens craving afterward, like "scratching an itch," and unrelated cognitive tasks using alternative imagery can break the cycle. The conclusion frames a visual object as "a momentary nexus of meaning" binding external visual features to prior knowledge, estimating "perhaps 95 percent of what we 'see' from the outside world is already in our heads," and closes by debunking the "left-brain/right-brain" creativity myth — creativity emerges from interaction across subsystems, with the frontal lobes as the likely coordinating locus.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Images activate meaning in a fraction of a second — much faster than reading text — which is the core justification for using imagery/icons for time-critical or glanceable information in a display.
- Visual working memory holds only about one to three "chunks" (objects) at a time; this hard limit on simultaneous visual comparison is a real bottleneck that should shape how much a design asks viewers to hold in mind at once.
- Side-by-side placement beats page/screen switching for comparison tasks — Ware states eye movements between co-located items are "at least ten times faster" than navigating between pages, a direct, quotable design argument for layout decisions (small multiples, dashboards).
- Scene "gist" is recognized via global statistical image properties (color/texture layout) in under a tenth of a second, independent of recognizing individual objects — a useful model for why coarse visual structure (color fields, overall layout) registers before any labels or details do.
- Making structural connections between an object's parts visually clear (revealed in silhouette/pose) speeds recognition — directly generalizes to diagrams and icons: keep the "joints"/connections between components visible.
- Novelty-seeking is automatic and involuntary; "gist-object conflict" (surprising elements against a familiar scene) is a legitimate, well-documented technique for capturing attention — useful for understanding both effective explanatory graphics and manipulative advertising, and where the line between them sits.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The neuroanatomy sections (IT cortex, geon theory, gist/statistical scene features, verbal working memory circuitry) are worth reading once for the vocabulary (nexus, biased competition, gist, generalized views, geons) since these terms recur through the book, but the anatomical detail itself is skimmable for a design-focused reader. The most load-bearing material for a vis course is the working-memory capacity discussion and the "receiving a cup of coffee" worked example (pp. 117–118), plus the final "Elaborations and Implications for Design" section (pp. 119 onward: make objects easy to identify, novelty, images as symbols, meaning and emotion, imagery and desire) — that's where the chapter cashes out into actionable design guidance. The advertising/branding and desire/craving material (Coca-Cola valuation, elaborated intrusion theory) is engaging but tangential to core dataviz design and can be skimmed or cut for time.

**Memorable specifics:**

- "As a rule of thumb, we can rotate an object by about 20 degrees and scale it by a factor of two or three and still identify it rapidly."
- Potter and Levy's 1969 MIT RSVP experiment: viewers could detect a specified target object in images flashed at up to ten per second, while remembering almost nothing else from the sequence.
- Normal fixations last about one-fifth of a second, typically picking up one to three objects; comparing items on the same page/screen is "at least ten times faster" via eye movements than switching between separate pages.
- Gist perception happens in under a tenth of a second — as fast as identifying a single object — driven by global scene statistics (e.g., beach scenes: blue sky band, striated sea, beige sand with vertical shapes), not object-by-object recognition.
- Coca-Cola's brand/trademark alone is estimated at over ten billion dollars, out of a roughly thirty-three-billion-dollar total company valuation — illustrating how much value symbolic association alone can carry.
- Closing estimate: "Perhaps 95 percent of what we 'see' from the outside world is already in our heads" — a strong, quotable claim about the primacy of prior knowledge over raw visual input.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The blurred street-scene/blobby-object pair (p. 114) — an automobile and a bottle rendered as visually identical blurred blobs, disambiguated only by scene context — is an excellent, self-explanatory demonstration of gist-driven object disambiguation for a lecture on context effects in perception.
- The Oliva/Torralba statistical-average scene images (pp. 113) — the averaged "person in Mallorca" image and the mediaeval-cityscape/beach-scene composites — vividly show that scene category can be read from pure color/texture statistics with zero identifiable objects, a strong visual for explaining "gist."
- The teapot/hand silhouette pair (p. 111) — clear-connection vs. hidden-connection views of the same objects — is a clean, reusable illustration of the "make joints visible" design principle for icon/diagram design.
- The geon-skeleton line drawings (mug, teapot, dog, human figure, stick figures) on p. 111, especially the "geon problem" figure with an arm emerging from the stomach, effectively conveys how structural connectivity, not just silhouette, drives recognition and how violating it produces a bizarre reading.
- The "receiving a cup of coffee" diagram (p. 118), mapping visual/verbal/motor working memory objects onto one photograph, is a good schematic template for explaining multi-store working-memory binding in an applied, relatable scenario.

**Caveats:** This is an uncorrected publisher proof (visible watermark on every page), so figure numbers/captions and pagination may shift slightly in the final print edition — treat citations to specific page numbers as approximate. Geon theory is explicitly flagged by Ware as "probably the most controversial" account in the chapter and should not be presented to students as settled neuroscience; it's a useful design heuristic (make part-connections visible) independent of whether the specific "geon" primitives are neurally real. The chapter's forays into advertising, branding value, and desire/craving (elaborated intrusion theory) are illustrative asides rather than core vis-design content and lean on a small number of cited studies (Torralba 2003; Oliva & Torralba 2006; Kavanagh, Andrade & May 2005) — readers commonly over-generalize "95 percent already in our heads" or the "biased competition" model into more mechanistic certainty than the text (which flags open questions, e.g. whether single neurons or assemblies implement pattern responses) actually claims.
