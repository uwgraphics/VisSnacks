# Cairo, "The Partnership of Presentation and Exploration" + "Why Visualize: From Information to Wisdom" (*The Functional Art*, Preface + ch. 1)

**What it is:** The book's introduction and opening chapter: Cairo's personal manifesto for why a journalist-designer thinks visualization matters, laying out the book's four themes and a worked example of do-it-yourself visualization as evidence-checking. Optional context reading for this module.

**AI summary:** The preface sets out four themes. First, the brain doesn't just process what the eyes deliver — it creates mental visual images we reason with, so understanding visual cognition makes you a better communicator ("to see and to understand are intertwined processes... We understand because we see"). Second, Cairo rejects the "sharp distinction" some erect between infographics (presentation, storytelling) and information visualization (exploration, analysis): **infographics and visualization exist on a continuum**, and every graphic has both a **presentation** and an **exploration** component. His examples span the range: Stefanie Posavec's *Literary Organism* (a hand-drawn tree of Kerouac's *On the Road*) toward the exploration end, and his own Brazil GDP-vs-inequality connected scatterplot for *Época* magazine, which "text" colleagues successfully read and analyzed. Third, **graphics, charts, and maps aren't just tools to be seen, but to be read and scrutinized** — beauty should come through "exquisite functionality," echoing Shneiderman's "the purpose of visualization is insight, not pictures." Fourth, visualization relates to art the way journalism relates to literature: it borrows tools but never *becomes* art. It is, above all, a **functional art**.

Chapter 1 opens with the book's best set piece: Cairo reads Matt Ridley's *The Rational Optimist*, is intrigued but unsatisfied by its claims about converging world fertility rates (supported by just one aggregate line chart), so he downloads UN fertility data himself and, in "three or four hours" with OpenOffice and Illustrator, builds charts to test the claims. The sequence — an unreadable table, a two-country line chart (Spain vs. Sweden), a hopeless all-countries "spaghetti dish," and a redesign that grays out background lines while highlighting a few countries plus the world average — motivates two core maxims: **"The first and main goal of any graphic and visualization is to be a tool for your eyes and brain to perceive what lies beyond their natural reach"** and **"In information graphics, what you show can be as important as what you hide."** The chapter then builds a conceptual frame: the **DIKW hierarchy** (unstructured information → data → structured information → knowledge → wisdom), with the communicator performing the "second level of encoding" that gives shape to data; Wurman's **information architect** and **information anxiety**; a nesting of information architecture ⊃ **information design** ⊃ visualization/infographics; the distinction between **figurative** and **non-figurative** (conventional) displays; and, crucially, the claim that **visualization should be seen as a technology** — an extension of ourselves and a means to reach goals (via Brian Arthur's technology-singular/plural/general). The payoff: **individual graphics are technologies, devices whose aim is to help an audience complete certain tasks** — which sets up chapter 2.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The presentation↔exploration continuum: don't treat "infographics" and "visualization" as rival tribes; every graphic does some of both.
- Visualization is a *tool for tasks* — a technology, not self-expression. This framing is the seed of the whole task-driven view of vis the course builds on.
- The fertility-data story as a model of vis literacy: cheap public data + basic tools let you check an author's claims yourself, and the design choices (highlighting, graying context) are what make the data legible.
- The DIKW pipeline vocabulary: the designer encodes; the reader decodes into knowledge; visualization exists to reduce the reader's work.
- "What you show can be as important as what you hide" — filtering and emphasis are design, not distortion.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the preface's continuum discussion, the fertility-rate walkthrough (Figures 1.3–1.7), and "Visualization as a Technology." Skim: the exabyte/zettabyte data-deluge tables, the Wurman biography, the Brian Arthur three-senses-of-technology taxonomy (get the punchline, skip the machinery). The DIKW section can be skimmed with the warning Cairo himself footnotes — the model has been criticized as "simplistic and vague."

**Memorable specifics:** Posavec's *Literary Organism*; the Brazil GDP-vs-Gini "presidencies" chart with its headline "When the Brazilian Economy Improves, Inequality Doesn't Drop"; Spain vs. Sweden fertility ("So much for Spanish pride" — Fig. 1.4's caption); the spaghetti chart of 150+ countries vs. the highlighted redesign; the *Público* Madrid plane-crash infographic; Shneiderman's "insight, not pictures"; the Brazilian newsroom's "Vamos fazer uma arte!" ("Let's make a piece of art!") as the wrong mindset; "an insatiable, childish curiosity" as the field's defining trait.

**Caveats:** Cairo's DIKW framing is explicitly borrowed and, as he notes, contested (he cites Weinberger's HBR critique). The data-explosion numbers (Eric Schmidt's "five exabytes every two days," 2010 IDC figures) are period pieces from 2012. His definitional move — folding academic infovis and journalistic infographics into one continuum — is a deliberate stance, not consensus; the academic definition he brushes aside ("computer-supported, interactive, visual representations... to amplify cognition") is the Card/Mackinlay/Shneiderman standard. Readers sometimes misread the book title: "functional art" is meant to *subordinate* art to function, not to elevate charts into art.
