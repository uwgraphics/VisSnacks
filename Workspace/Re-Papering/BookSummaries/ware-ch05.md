# Ware, "Getting the Information: Visual Space and Time" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 5)

**What it is:** This chapter examines the perceptual and cognitive structure of three-dimensional space — how depth is perceived, and how the costs of navigating through space (physical or virtual) shape information access — building toward a set of practical design principles ("2.5-dimensional design") for when and how to use depth, occlusion, and spatial layout in visual designs and interfaces.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the idea that "moving our eyes is the lowest-cost method we have for getting additional information about our environment," and generalizes this into a cost hierarchy: eye movements, rummaging through filing cabinets, and business travel are all methods of "traversing space to get information into our working memory systems," differing enormously in cognitive cost. A map of travel times around Cambridge, England, illustrates that physical distance and cognitive-access cost are not the same thing — some outlying villages are as fast to reach as nearby suburbs. He notes video-conferencing (with a nod to COVID-19) as a technology that changes these costs by preserving social cues cheaply while cutting travel cost.

The chapter's core theoretical section covers depth perception and cue theory. Perceptual egocentric space has *up*, *sideways*, and *towards-away* dimensions; the *towards-away* dimension carries far less information than the other two (because we can't see through most objects), which is why vision is sometimes called "2.5-dimensional" — though Ware cautions the "0.5" shouldn't be taken literally, since depth information is much less than half of what the other two dimensions provide. Depth cues split into *pictorial* (reproducible in a static image: occlusion, size gradients, texture gradients, linear perspective, cast shadows, height on the picture plane, shading, depth of focus, reference to known objects, atmospheric contrast reduction) and *nonpictorial* (stereopsis, structure from motion, lens focus, eye convergence). Each cue is presented in a compact reference-card format with an example image; occlusion is singled out as "the strongest depth cue" — when it competes with another cue like size constancy, occlusion wins — and as both a method and metaphor for rank-ordering information importance.

Stereoscopic depth gets its own section: it's optimal for guiding hand movements toward nearby objects (citing Arsenault & Ware 2004) and is most precise for objects at nearly the same depth, but the brain is poor at using stereo for large relative distances — which is why the 20% of people with little/no stereo vision still drive and walk fine, though they're "clumsy... threading needles." Ware attributes the repeated commercial failure of stereo technology (Victorian stereo photos, 1930s-40s stereo cameras, 2010s stereo movies/TV) to the fact that none of these let viewers manually interact with the represented 3D objects — stereo's real value is hand-guidance, which passive viewing doesn't exploit.

The chapter then introduces *2.5D design* (a term borrowed loosely from David Marr's "2.5D sketchpad," though Ware's principles aren't based on Marr's theory), with a bulleted list of tenets: use depth cues selectively and inconsistently with realism if it serves design goals; minimize overall occlusion; when occlusion is necessary, don't obscure critical information (use transparency); make joints between compound parts clearly visible; make spatial relationships (on top of, attached to, inside of) explicit; use shadows/depth-of-focus/transparency to clarify relative distance when depth is used; keep text in the image plane (slanted text is harder to read); and give common/canonical views of objects for rapid identification. A worked example is an XPLANE business-process diagram using occlusion, height-on-picture-plane, and layering without obscuring any important node.

A section on "how much of the third dimension?" argues that most information (business statistics, social networks, abstract concepts) isn't inherently spatial, and that 3D representations of such data usually fail because navigation in 3D is expensive and hard to build mental maps of — "three-dimensional information mazes are especially to be avoided." Even true 3D virtual spaces should still apply 2.5D principles because viewpoint always creates a 2D image-plane projection; practical interfaces should pair a 2D overview map (in the same orientation as the detail view) with a 3D/perspective detail view.

The chapter pivots to action with Gibson's concept of *affordances* — perceived possibilities for action (a ground surface affords walking, a waist-height surface affords support) — including negative affordances (a brick wall rules out passage) — then extends this to *cognitive affordances* (on-screen buttons as "possibilities for action" in interfaces). This connects to brain anatomy: the *where* pathway runs from V1/V2 to the parietal lobe, containing distinct coarse "action maps" (LIP for eye-movement planning in retinal coordinates, MIP for arm movements, plus head-centered and body-centered maps), each feeding the motor cortex; these maps are temporary and sparse, not detailed or permanent, and much of their operation is unconscious.

A section on "artificial interactive spaces" discusses how video games only need to get physics "roughly" right (the "naïve physics of perception") — gravity is often halved with no player complaint — because our real-world mental models are metaphors guiding interpretation, not exact physics engines. This leads into a discussion of how skill transfers: mouse-cursor correspondence, pull-down menus, and game controllers are becoming as "real" to motor-skill learning as physical objects, raising the possibility that virtual-world physics could eventually ground an alternative naïve physics for younger generations.

The chapter closes with "space traversal and cognitive costs," presenting a rough table of costs for information-access actions (internal pattern comparison ~0.04s, eye movements ~0.1s, mouse hover ~1.0s, mouse select ~1.5s, plus walk/drive/fly/train at their respective speeds, and zooming). Zooming and hyperlinks are highlighted as new "nonmetaphoric" navigation methods with no real-world analog: with exponential zooming at 4x/second, you could go from a view of the whole Earth to a view of a person's hand in about 14 seconds, and to the size of a molecule in 14 more — "the power of exponential change." The Eames-inspired "Powers of Ten"-style zoom sequence illustrates this. The conclusion restates the throughline: "the functional aesthetics of space are related to the cost of accessing information," and depth cues are used by the brain piecemeal, task-dependently — which is precisely what frees designers from having to apply them all consistently.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Depth cues are not a package deal — pictorial cues (occlusion, size gradient, linear perspective, cast shadow, height on picture plane, shading, depth of focus, known-object reference, atmospheric contrast) can be applied independently, each supporting different visual queries; good 2.5D design picks and chooses rather than aiming for photoreal consistency.
- Occlusion is the strongest depth cue and doubles as a rank-ordering device — deliberately layering objects so important ones partially occlude less important ones is a legitimate design technique, provided occluded objects stay identifiable.
- 3D representations of inherently non-spatial data (business stats, social networks, abstract concepts) usually backfire: navigation cost in 3D is high, mental-map formation is slow, and "three-dimensional information mazes" should be avoided — prefer 2D layouts with judicious depth cues for layering.
- Space traversal at every scale (eye movements, mouse actions, walking, driving, flying) has a cognitive cost, and interface design decisions (e.g., surfacing a frequent piece of information via an eye movement instead of a mouse click) can be evaluated and improved by attending to these relative costs.
- Zooming and hyperlinking are historically novel, "nonmetaphoric" navigation techniques with no real-world equivalent — they compress the cost of accessing distant information by orders of magnitude and have measurably changed social behavior (fewer library visits, more remote meetings).
- Affordances — including negative affordances — reframe interface elements (buttons, menus) as perceived possibilities for action, tying visual design directly to the brain's action-oriented "where" pathway rather than to passive image recognition alone.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The reference-card catalog of pictorial depth cues (pp. 91-93: occlusion, size gradient, texture gradient, linear perspective, cast shadows, height on picture plane, shading, depth of focus, known-object reference, contrast reduction) is essential — it's the vocabulary the rest of the book's spatial-design advice depends on — but can be skimmed quickly since each entry is self-contained and formulaic. The 2.5D design tenets list (pp. 95-96) and the "how much of the third dimension?" argument against gratuitous 3D (p. 97) are the chapter's most directly usable design guidance and worth reading closely. The neuroanatomy of the *where* pathway (LIP/MIP, parietal maps, pp. 100-102) is interesting context but not load-bearing for practical design decisions and can be skimmed. The opening travel/videoconferencing framing and the closing cost-table/zooming discussion are good illustrative material but largely restate the same "space traversal = cognitive cost" point in different guises.

**Memorable specifics:**

- Opening epigraph: "It is one thing to say with the psalmist 'I lift mine eyes up to the hills'—it is another thing to drag one's carcass up there" (Chesterton, paraphrased by John Prange).
- Stereo depth perception is precise only for near, similar-depth objects; ~20% of people have little/no stereo vision yet drive and walk without difficulty — they're only clumsy at fine tasks like threading needles.
- Video game designers commonly halve the force of gravity and players never notice — dubbed the "naïve physics of perception."
- Cost table (p. 104): internal pattern comparison ≈ 0.04s, eye movement ≈ 0.1s, mouse hover ≈ 1.0s, mouse select ≈ 1.5s; walking/driving/flying/train rated by speed and by "affordances for contemplative thought" vs. "affordances for interactive cognitive work."
- Zooming at 4x magnification per second gets you from a view of the whole Earth to a human hand in about 14 seconds, and from there to the size of a molecule in another 14 seconds — "the power of exponential change."
- "Live in" is called a "weasel phrase" for describing our relationship to 3D space, since the cognitive organism only ever has ~2.5D worth of usable depth information.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The Cambridge, England travel-time map (p. 88) — a striking real-world example of cognitive/access cost diverging from physical distance; great opening hook for a lecture on "distance is not cost."
- The pictorial depth cues reference cards (pp. 91-93) — each cue (occlusion, size/texture gradient, linear perspective, cast shadow, height on picture plane, shading, depth of focus, known-object reference, contrast reduction) has a clean, single-concept illustrative image; ideal for a slide-by-slide teaching sequence on depth cues.
- The magenta-vs-cyan balls on a checkerboard (p. 92, "cast shadows") is a crisp, minimal illustration of how shadow position alone anchors depth judgment — very reusable for a short in-class demo.
- The XPLANE business-process diagrams (p. 98) as a real-world, non-academic example of 2.5D design principles (occlusion, height, layering) applied to a practical infographic — useful for showing that these aren't just academic toy cases.
- The Eames-style Earth-to-hand zoom sequence (p. 105) — nine-panel image ladder illustrating exponential zoom scale change; good visual companion to the "cost of zooming" discussion.

**Caveats:** The chapter's cost table (p. 104) is explicitly flagged by Ware himself as "far too rough an approximation" to use for rigorous interface comparisons — it's meant as "food for thought," not a citable benchmark, so don't over-read precision into those numbers. The 2.5D design tenets partly anticipate concepts (joints, object views) that Ware says are "presented later in Chapter 6," so this chapter's list is a preview, not the full treatment. The discussion of stereo technology's commercial failure and of COVID-era videoconferencing is somewhat historically dated/anecdotal (2020-2021 vintage) and reads as topical commentary rather than a durable empirical claim. Finally, the "2.5D" terminology borrowed from Marr is explicitly noted as *not* based on Marr's actual theory — just an appropriated phrase — so it shouldn't be cited as if it carries Marr's specific computational-vision claims.
