# Munzner, "Rules of Thumb" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 6)

**What it is:** A set of eight slogan-titled design guidelines — synthesized from empirical studies, prior literature, and Munzner's own experience — covering 3D use, dimensionality, memory/animation, immersion, overview/zoom/filter, latency, color, and the form-vs-function tradeoff.

**AI summary:** Munzner opens by stating these rules are "not set in stone" — a current synthesis of an incomplete, still-evolving research frontier (Figure 6.1 lists all eight as slogans). The longest rule, **No Unjustified 3D**, argues that 3D is easy to justify only when the user's task involves understanding inherently 3D shape or spatial structure; in all other contexts it needs careful justification because of several costs. She grounds this in perceptual mechanics: the **power of the plane** (the shared "planar" spatial-position channel for up-down/left-right is highly accurate, but arbitrary 3D depth position is not equivalent perceptually); the **disparity of depth** (depth judgment accuracy has power-law exponent n=0.67, worse than area's 0.7, versus n=1.0 for planar length); **line-of-sight ambiguity** (we get information about only one point per ray along the depth axis, versus millions of rays for the other two axes) and the aphorism that we "see in **2.05D**" (Colin Ware) rather than true 3D; **occlusion** (the most powerful depth cue, but it hides information, requiring costly interactive navigation and **motion parallax** to resolve, especially costly for unfamiliar abstract structures like a node-link graph in 3D); **perspective distortion/foreshortening** (destroys the power of the plane and the size channel — 3D bar charts are harder to read than 2D ones); other depth cues like familiar size, shadows/shading (add clutter, can be mistaken for marks, interfere with color channels), and stereoscopic depth (a comparatively weak cue, most useful for near objects, that doesn't fix perspective distortion); and **tilted text isn't legible** (off-plane text rendering is blocky/jaggy with current display tech). The chapter balances this with **benefits of 3D: shape perception** — when the task requires understanding 3D geometric structure (streamlines through a volume, fluid flow, molecular structure), 3D with interactive navigation lets users build mental models faster than assembling multiple 2D views, and experimental evidence supports 3D outperforming 2D specifically for shape tasks while 2D wins for precise relative-position judgments. Section 6.3.9 (Empirical Evidence) stresses the documented dissociation between stated preference for 3D and actual measured performance, and walks through several studies (Data Mountain, Tory et al.'s information-landscape comparison) where careful controls overturned earlier claims of 3D benefit — points-based displays beat landscape/terrain idioms for search and estimation tasks, and 2D landscapes beat 3D landscapes.

The remaining rules are shorter. **No Unjustified 2D** parallels the 3D argument one dimension down: 1D lists have higher information density and are better for lookup/alphabetical search than 2D node-link layouts unless the task truly requires seeing topological structure. **Eyes Beat Memory** argues that switching between simultaneously visible external views has far lower cognitive load than comparing against internal memory of a prior view; this motivates overview+detail idioms (e.g., a viewport rectangle in an overview pane) over idioms that rely on the viewer remembering earlier state. It's grounded in **working memory** limits and **cognitive load**, and covers **animation vs. side-by-side views** in detail: Munzner distinguishes narrative storytelling, single transitions, and video-style multiframe playback, arguing transitions are effective for helping people track a small number of changes (with strong evidence they beat jump cuts for maintaining context) but multiframe animations with many simultaneous changes overwhelm attention — side-by-side small multiples are typically better for detailed comparison across dozens (not hundreds) of frames. This section also covers **change blindness** — the striking finding that people fail to notice drastic changes (e.g., a person being swapped mid-conversation) when attention isn't directed at the changing region — as a rationale for why multiframe animation tracking is hard. **Resolution over Immersion** argues pixels are a scarcer, more valuable resource than presence/immersion for almost all vis tasks, since immersive tech (HMDs, stereo, 6-DOF tracking) trades away both display resolution and integration with the user's normal desktop workflow; immersion is only worth it when the abstraction is inherently 3D spatial data and presence itself matters. **Overview First, Zoom and Filter, Details on Demand** presents Shneiderman's classic mantra, framed via the what-why-how abstraction as an idiom aimed at the "summarize" task; overviews are created via **reduce** actions (filtering and/or aggregation), and the mantra applies best to moderate-size datasets — for huge datasets the alternative slogan is **Search, Show Context, Expand on Demand**. **Responsiveness Is Required** covers **latency** as categorical rather than continuous, citing Card et al.'s three time constants (Table 6.1: perceptual processing 0.1s, immediate response 1s, brief tasks 10s), then discusses visual feedback design, the interplay of interaction-mechanism/feedback/system-update latencies, and the cost/benefit tradeoff of interactivity itself (risk of degenerating into "human-powered search" if too much reliance is placed on the user checking every possibility). **Get It Right in Black and White** (Maureen Stone) is a color-encoding check: literally convert your image to grayscale to verify the most crucial information survives via the luminance channel alone. **Function First, Form Next** closes with Munzner's rationale for the book's functional focus — effective-but-ugly designs can be refined toward beauty in collaboration with visual designers, while beautiful-but-ineffective designs usually must be scrapped, so function should come first, though she's explicit this is not "form never."

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The 3D rule is the chapter's centerpiece and directly supports the 3D/scientific-vis lecture theme "we sense 2D, we infer 3D": the mechanics (power of the plane, line-of-sight ambiguity, "2.05D," occlusion, perspective distortion) explain *why* 3D for abstract data is usually a bad default, and *when* (shape-understanding tasks on inherently spatial data) it's actually justified.
- Depth cues are not interchangeable or additive — occlusion is powerful but costly, stereo is weak and short-range, shading/shadows add clutter and interfere with color channels; a student should be able to name which depth cue is doing the work in a given 3D visualization and what it costs.
- "Stated preference for 3D" versus "measured task performance" is a real, repeatedly documented dissociation — a caution against trusting user self-report or first impressions (Data Mountain history is the canonical cautionary tale).
- Eyes Beat Memory gives a perceptual justification for the ubiquitous overview+detail / linked-views idiom family, and for skepticism about animation-heavy interfaces with many simultaneous changes.
- Change blindness is a striking, memorable, easily-demoed phenomenon worth using in class to motivate why multiframe comparison tasks need side-by-side small multiples rather than pure animation.
- The latency time-constants (0.1s / 1s / 10s) are a concrete, actionable design target — worth internalizing as numbers, not just "make it fast."
- Function First, Form Next is a useful stance for grading/critiquing student projects: ineffective-but-pretty should lose to effective-but-plain.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Careful read: 6.3 (No Unjustified 3D) in full — including 6.3.1–6.3.9 — since it's the richest, most citation-dense, and most exam/discussion-worthy section, and directly supports the 3D/scientific-data lecture. 6.5 (Eyes Beat Memory, including 6.5.1–6.5.3 on memory/attention, animation, and change blindness) also merits careful reading — foundational for the deeper-perception module. 6.7 (Overview First...) and 6.8 (Responsiveness) are solid but can be skimmed for the slogans and Table 6.1 if time-limited. 6.4, 6.6, 6.9, 6.10 are short and can be skimmed quickly — each is essentially one paragraph-length idea worth remembering as a slogan even without deep study. 6.11 (Further Reading) is a bibliography — skip unless chasing citations.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The eight rule titles as slogans (Figure 6.1): No Unjustified 3D; No Unjustified 2D; Eyes Beat Memory; Resolution over Immersion; Overview First, Zoom and Filter, Detail on Demand; Responsiveness Is Required; Get It Right in Black and White; Function First, Form Next.
- "We do not really live in 3D, or even 2.5D: to quote Colin Ware, we see in 2.05D" [Ware 08].
- "Line-of-sight ambiguity" — along the depth axis we get information about only one point per ray, versus millions of rays for the other two spatial axes.
- The "blink comparator" idiom (used by the astronomer who found Pluto) as a historical example of giving users control over jumping between two frames to detect localized change.
- The door-swap change-blindness experiment: a stranger asking directions is interrupted by people carrying a door, during which the questioner is swapped for a different person (even a different gender) — most people don't notice.
- Table 6.1's three latency time constants: perceptual processing 0.1s, immediate response 1s, brief tasks 10s [Card et al. 91].
- "Get It Right in Black and White" (Maureen Stone) — literally convert to grayscale and check legibility survives on luminance alone.
- "Given an effective but ugly design, it's possible to refine the form... In contrast, given a beautiful and ineffective design, you will probably need to toss it out and start from scratch" — Munzner's rationale for functional focus, paired explicitly with "I don't advocate 'form never.'"
- Search, Show Context, Expand on Demand [van Ham and Perer 09] as the alternative mantra for datasets too large for Overview First to work.

**Caveats:** These are explicitly Munzner's own synthesis/opinion ("my current attempt... deeply incomplete"), not settled consensus — some rules rest on solid empirical citations (3D vs 2D shape/position tasks, change blindness, Card's latency constants) while others are more heuristic/argument-based (Function First Form Next, Resolution over Immersion) and should be presented to students as reasoned design advice rather than proven law. The 3D section leans on human-factors/VR literature that is now itself somewhat dated (Cockburn and McKenzie studies, Data Mountain, ~2000s); modern high-resolution VR/AR headsets have shifted the resolution-vs-immersion tradeoff somewhat since the book's 2014 writing, so "resolution over immersion" may need updating commentary for current hardware. The animation discussion (small multiples vs. animation) is a common point of contention in the vis research community (the Tversky et al. 2002 meta-review is influential but contested); worth flagging as an area of ongoing debate rather than closed consensus.
