# Ware, "Creative Meta-seeing" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 9)

**What it is:** This chapter examines the psychology of constructive visual thinking in design — how designers move between mental imagery, sketching, and critique to develop ideas. It closes the book's cognitive-science arc by applying the perceptual and mental-model machinery from earlier chapters (contour extraction, working memory, predictive models) to the specific, effortful, acquired skill of design thinking through drawing.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel sketches to make the point that creative design work is rarely done purely in the head — it happens through a "dialogue with some rapid production medium," whether sketches for artists, clay maquettes for sculptors, or rough diagrams for engineers and programmers. He generalizes this into a four-step cycle: (1) a visual concept forms (constrained or free depending on the task), (2) externalization — a loose scribble puts the concept on paper, (3) constructive critique — visually querying whether the design meets requirements, and (4) consolidation and extension, where lines are strengthened, erased, or added. Steps 2–4 repeat many times.

The chapter then tackles mental imagery directly, contrasting three historical theories: Zenon Pylyshyn's view that imagery is purely symbolic/propositional with no spatial brain representation; Stephen Kosslyn's view that imagery reuses the same neural spatial maps as seeing, creating a "phantom image" in low-level visual cortex; and N.J.T. Thomas's "activity theory," which Ware endorses, holding that imagery consists of the same *active, constructive cognitive processes* as normal seeing rather than a static picture-in-the-head. Evidence marshaled includes eye-movement studies (people's eyes move when imagining a scene, matching fixation locations — though not sequence — from when they actually viewed it) and fMRI work (Ganis, Thompson & Kosslyn) showing higher visual areas activate similarly during seeing and imagining, but primary visual cortex does not activate during imagery — evidence against Kosslyn's low-level "phantom image" and for imagery being a high-level, active process using the same machinery that plans and executes eye movements. Specialized areas (fusiform gyrus for faces, parahippocampal area for spatial locations) activate for both perception and imagery of their respective content.

Ware then turns to the "magic of the scribble": because contour extraction is a generalized, meaning-agnostic perceptual mechanism, a single line or circle can support wildly different interpretations (a circle can read as coin, ball, ring, hole, or plugged hole; Saul Steinberg's 1959 New Yorker cartoon exploits this). He walks through Massironi's bird-drawing exercise — make a meaningless irregular loop scribble, then add a small circle (eye) and a small angle (beak), and "almost any scribble can become a bird" — to demonstrate that meaning is readily projected onto ambiguous marks, and that this projective act is itself a creative tool designers exploit to escape stereotyped thinking.

The chapter distinguishes sketches (visual prototypes resembling the finished thing) from diagrams (abstractions expressing concept structures, often mixing words and graphical conventions — transistors, doors and windows, etc.). Darwin's 1837 notebook tree-of-life sketch is presented as the canonical example of a concept diagram. Arrows get singled out as a uniquely important diagrammatic symbol, able to express motion paths, sequences, causal relationships, and taxonomic "is-a" hierarchies, combining the visual system's pattern-finding power with abstract symbolic meaning.

On process: requirements-gathering and design often proceed together in a "spiral design" methodology, since clients frequently can't articulate what they want until shown a prototype. Visual task analysis breaks design goals into cognitive tasks and then into visual queries the design must support. Citing Suwa and Tversky's study of architecture students designing a museum (and Bryan Lawson's interviews with architects like Santiago Calatrava, whose sketches serve "not... a wish to produce a drawing but rather to understand a problem"), Ware describes "constructive perception" — designers don't just externalize pre-formed ideas; they reinterpret their own marks, discovering new meaning in lines they've already drawn. Cognitive economics of sketching is framed as a tradeoff: visual working memory is fast and flexible but has almost no capacity and is impermanent; paper sketches are slower to modify (roughly a second or two per mark) but hold much more and persist; finished drawings cost the most but are most elaborate and stable.

The chapter closes on the perceptual critique — the meta-seeing of the title — distinguishing concept diagrams from prototype designs and describing self-critique as a simulated "cognitive walkthrough" of how another viewer would query the design. Self-critiquing one's own work is harder than critiquing others' because the designer already knows where everything is and what it means; letting 2–3 days pass lets neural priming from the original design session fade so fresh alternatives can be imagined. Formal cognitive walkthroughs with actual non-designer users are expensive and reserved for more finished designs. Visual skill develops from effortful to automatic (the mirror-reading fMRI study by Poldrack et al. is used as the model case), and — notably — sleep between practice sessions, not raw practice time, is what converts effortful visual skills into automatic ones.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Sketching is not decoration on top of thinking — it's the medium in which design thinking happens (the "dialogue with paper"). This reframes "just sketch it out" as a cognitively necessary step, not a nice-to-have, for anyone teaching iterative design or prototyping.
- The four-step design cycle — form concept, externalize, critique, consolidate/extend — is a reusable teaching frame for any studio or critique exercise, including visualization design reviews.
- Lines are radically ambiguous (the coin/ball/ring/hole/plugged-hole example, the bird-scribble exercise) because contour extraction is a generic perceptual mechanism — a useful concrete illustration of "the eye/brain finds structure eagerly, even from minimal evidence," relevant to warning students how easily viewers over-read sparse marks in a chart or diagram.
- Concept diagrams (abstract idea structures, e.g., Darwin's tree) versus prototype designs (rough previews of the finished artifact, e.g., a mockup) is a clean vocabulary distinction for talking about early-stage visualization/design artifacts.
- Arrows are a genuinely special diagrammatic primitive — they can encode motion, sequence, causality, and taxonomic hierarchy simultaneously, worth flagging explicitly when teaching diagram/network encoding choices.
- Critiquing your own design is a harder, distinct skill from critiquing others' work, and self-critique improves just by letting time pass (2-3 days) for priming to fade — directly actionable advice for students revising their own visualization drafts.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The mental-imagery theory section (Pylyshyn vs. Kosslyn vs. Thomas, the fMRI evidence) is the most skimmable part for a practical design course — it's neuroscience background supporting Ware's preferred "activity theory," interesting but not load-bearing for design practice. The core material worth close reading is: the scribble/bird exercise and its lesson about projected meaning, the sketch-vs-diagram distinction, the arrow discussion, the Suwa/Tversky "constructive perception" finding, and the closing sections on visual task analysis, the perceptual critique, and visual skill development (especially the sleep-and-automaticity point). The conclusion is a tight, quotable synthesis and worth reading even if skimming the body.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The four-step creative design process: concept formed → externalization (scribble) → constructive critique → consolidation and extension, with steps 2-4 repeating many times.
- Massironi's bird exercise: draw a meaningless irregular loop scribble, add a small circle and a "<" shape, and "almost any scribble can become a bird" — head, wings, tail, and body suddenly appear.
- "A circle on paper can have five different meanings": coin, ball, ring, hole, plugged hole — all from the identical mark.
- Darwin's 1837 notebook page with the first known sketch of the evolutionary tree concept (held at Cambridge University Library) is Ware's canonical example of a concept diagram.
- Adding a mark to a sketch takes "a second or two," while visual working memory can shuffle concepts in and out "several times a second" — the cognitive-economics contrast between mental imagery and externalized marks.
- Quote on Calatrava (from Bryan Lawson's interviews): his sketches are never "the result of a wish to produce a drawing but rather to understand a problem."
- Skill automaticity requires sleep between practice sessions, not just practice hours: "we may practice a task for hours and gain little benefit; but if we practice for only half an hour, then wait a day, some measure of automaticity will be gained" (citing Poldrack et al.'s mirror-reading fMRI study).

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The five-circles-five-meanings figure (coin/ball/ring/hole/plugged hole) — a compact, instantly graspable demonstration of contour ambiguity, ideal for a slide on how little visual information is needed to trigger an interpretation.
- The Massironi scribble-to-bird sequence (random loops → same loops with a dot-and-beak added → recognizable birds) — a strong in-class exercise or figure for teaching how minimal marks carry meaning and how designers exploit ambiguity generatively.
- The Saul Steinberg 1959 New Yorker cartoon (a single continuous line reading simultaneously as a child's whimsical drawing and an adult's harsh scribble) — a vivid, licensable-attribution example (credited to Steinberg Foundation/ARS) of line quality carrying both content and tone; useful for a lecture on expressive line use, though rights would need checking for reuse beyond fair-use teaching contexts.
- Darwin's evolutionary-tree notebook sketch — a strong real-world, high-status example of a "concept diagram" distinct from a visual prototype, good for illustrating diagrams-as-idea-structures versus sketches-as-appearance.
- The spiral-design diagram ("vague concept" → alternating "design improvements" and "better understanding of requirements" loops → "completed design") — a clean, reusable schematic for teaching iterative/spiral design methodology in any studio context.

**Caveats:** The chapter leans on a contested area of cognitive science (imagery theory) and takes a clear side — Thomas's activity theory, which "inspired the creation of this book" — so the fMRI evidence presented (Ganis/Thompson/Kosslyn; Laeng/Teodorescu) should be read as supporting Ware's chosen framework rather than as a neutral survey; readers shouldn't treat the Pylyshyn/Kosslyn debate as fully resolved by these two studies. The architecture-heavy examples (Calatrava, Jiricna, Suwa & Tversky's museum-design study) mean most illustrative cases come from architectural sketching rather than data visualization directly, so instructors will need to bridge the analogy to charts/diagrams explicitly.
