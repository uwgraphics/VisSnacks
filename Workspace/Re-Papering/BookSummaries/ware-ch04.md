# Ware, "Color" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 4)

**What it is:** This chapter builds a theory-based framework for using color in design, grounded in the physiology of color vision (cone receptors, opponent-process channels) and the perceptual phenomena that follow from it (contrast, assimilation, saturation limits, spatial-resolution differences between channels). It deliberately skips colorimetry and color reproduction as separate technical disciplines, focusing instead on deriving a compact set of design principles — for showing detail, coding categories, emphasizing/highlighting, and building color sequences for maps — from a small amount of underlying theory.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with an evolutionary framing: color vision is nearly worthless to grass-eating animals like zebras and cows (motion matters more than color for a predator's approach) but essential to fruit eaters, who need color to spot ripe fruit against foliage — the chapter's opening photo pair contrasts a color and grayscale view of berries on a bush to make this point vivid. Humans and other great apes have three-dimensional color vision (three cone types); birds have four or more dimensions; most mammals have two.

The retina has rods (numerous, tuned for low light, largely wasted on modern humans who rarely need night vision) and three cone subtypes — short- (S), medium- (M), and long- (L) wavelength sensitive — which is why color vision is three-dimensional and why displays use three color channels (RGB pixels or phosphors). Using David Williams' retinal imaging, Ware shows that S-cones (blue-sensitive) are far fewer and less sensitive than M- or L-cones, and that L- and M-cone sensitivity functions overlap heavily (400–700nm range), with the brain computing their difference to extract hue information. A practical consequence: blue text on black (or small detailed blue elements generally) reads poorly because of insufficient luminance contrast from the sparse S-cones — likewise pure yellow text is nearly as light as white, making small yellow-on-white text hard to read even though yellow-on-black is highly distinct.

The core theoretical machinery is **opponent-process theory** (traced to Ewald Hering, 1878): in V1, cone signals are transformed into three color-opponent channels — **red-green** (L minus M difference), **yellow-blue** (combined L+M cone signal minus S), and **black-white/luminance** (L+M combined, also computing spatial differences across the retina). From this Ware derives a long list of "channel properties," each with a worked example: (1) **Simultaneous contrast** — a gray or hued patch looks different depending on its surround (lightness contrast in the black-white channel, chromatic contrast in red-green/yellow-blue); this isn't mere illusion but supports real-world surface-color constancy, letting us interpret a beach scene reproduced at 1/100th the light of the original. (2) **Unique hues** — black, white, red, green, yellow, and blue are special because each is a strong signal on one channel with neutral signals on the others; Berlin and Kay's 1960s cross-linguistic study of 100+ languages found these are the most universally-named basic color terms. (3) **Spatial detail** sensitivity is much higher in the luminance channel than the chromatic channels — fine wavy-line patterns are legible in black-white but nearly illegible when rendered as pure chromatic (red-green) differences. (4) **Motion** and (5) **stereoscopic depth** are likewise processed mainly via luminance, not chromatic, channels — motion of red-green-only stimuli appears to slow down. (6) **Shape-from-shading** depends on luminance; Ware demonstrates this by remapping a grayscale shaded-surface photo into red-green values, destroying the sense of 3D topography while sharp boundaries remain visible. (7) **Saturation** reflects chromatic-channel signal strength, and (8) maximum saturation is constrained at both light and dark ends (partly perceptual, partly a limitation of display/print gamuts — white is necessarily desaturated on a monitor since it requires all channels maxed). (9) **Color assimilation** is the opposite of contrast: patches take on the hue of adjacent patterns rather than contrasting with them (demonstrated with identical-color arcs that look different against different striped backgrounds); the conditions triggering assimilation vs. contrast are "poorly understood." (10) The luminance channel is **nonlinear** — sensitive to dark differences more than light ones against a dark background, and vice versa against a light background (an effect called *sharpening*). (11) **Color segmentation**: smoothly varying color is perceptually carved into blocks of the unique hues even when the actual gradient is continuous. (12) **Color blindness** (~8% of males) is loss of the red-green channel, not the yellow-blue channel, leaving 2D rather than 3D color space with grayscale perception intact. (13) Color is a **surface property** perceived relative to context, not absolute light level — demonstrated via R. Beau Lotto's checkerboard/cube illusion where two patches that are pixel-identical read as brown vs. yellow depending on apparent lighting.

The design-principles section translates all this into rules. **Showing detail** requires luminance contrast — black/white is most extreme, but yellow-on-black or dark-blue-on-white also work; the ISO recommends a minimum 3:1 luminance ratio between text and background for legibility, which sharply restricts usable text/background color combinations for small text (though for large elements like poster titles, contrast requirements relax). **Color-coding** (e.g., land-use maps) should rely on the unique hues (red, green, yellow, blue) first for maximum learnability, since these have the most consistent cross-linguistic names, supplemented by pink, brown, orange, gray, purple; no more than about a dozen codes can be used reliably in dense, small-symbol designs (studies converge on a range of six to twelve). Visual search for a colored target is easiest when the chromatic difference from other symbols is large, hardest when many other distinct symbol colors compete, and easiest again when non-target symbols are similar to the background (so they can be tuned out) or when both a luminance and chromatic difference exist together. **Large vs. small areas** need opposite treatment: small color-coded symbols should be strongly saturated with luminance contrast against their background, while large background regions should use low-saturation, subdued colors — because area size interacts with perceived saturation and small elements are more vulnerable to contrast-induced color distortion. **Emphasis/highlighting** requires a genuine luminance-contrast difference, not just a saturated hue swap — Ware calls out the common PowerPoint mistake of "highlighting" text with a saturated color that actually reduces luminance contrast, making it less visible; a spinal-fusion medical illustration by Christy Crames is used as an example of good highlighting via desaturating/neutralizing the non-critical background.

**Color sequences** for maps (colormaps) serve two tasks: revealing spatial patterns (best served by luminance-varying sequences) and reading quantitative values via a key (where pure grayscale sequences can produce errors up to 20% of the data range, per Ware's own 1988 study, due to contrast effects). Ware recommends colormaps that "spiral up through color space," increasing luminance monotonically while also varying hue — illustrated with an ocean-height dataset comparing grayscale, a poor green-red map (low red-green channel resolution), a rainbow map (which "can be confusing and emphasizes certain features while failing to show others"), and a double-ended dark-blue-to-white-to-dark-red map by Francesca Samsel that "effectively doubl[es] the luminance range" and reveals the most detail. The rainbow/spectrum sequence is specifically critiqued: because it's ordered by wavelength it's often wrongly presumed to be a perceptually ordered sequence, but an experiment sorting red/green/yellow/blue chips shows no consistent ordering preference for the spectrum sequence versus others — parts of the spectrum are locally ordered, but the whole is not, whereas gray chips are reliably ordered black-to-white or the reverse. Zero-centered data is best shown with a neutral color at zero and increasing saturation for values above/below. **Color on shaded surfaces**: any colored pattern painted on a surface must not interfere with the luminance channel or it destroys shape-from-shading perception — light-colored, low-saturation overlays preserve shape perception while dark, highly saturated overlays "almost completely destroy" it. Finally, **semantics of color** are culturally determined (red = danger/stop in the West but good fortune in China; white = purity in the West but mourning in most of Asia) except perhaps for quantity mappings, where more-saturated or higher-contrast colors more universally signal "more."

The **conclusion** restates the throughline: opponent-channel theory (red-green, yellow-blue, black-white) explains simultaneous contrast, why only a handful of maximally distinct hues can be reliably used as labels, why large areas should be subdued while small areas need strong saturated contrast to compensate for the chromatic channels' poor spatial resolution, and why the six "special" colors (red, green, yellow, blue, black, white) make the best labels. Ware closes candidly: "Color design is subtle and can be a source of beauty and pleasure or disgust and irritation. Mostly this is a matter of socially constructed taste—there are no absolute standards. Our goal in this book is to find the bedrock of functional aesthetics in the wiring of the brain, and sadly, we have little to say about beauty."

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Luminance contrast — not hue difference — is what carries detail, fine text, motion, stereo depth, and shape-from-shading; a saturated-hue "highlight" that lowers luminance contrast (the classic PowerPoint mistake) actually makes text harder to see, not easier.
- There is a real ceiling on how many colors can be reliably used as categorical codes (roughly six to twelve for small, dense symbols); build codes from the "unique hues" (red, green, yellow, blue, plus black/white) first, since these are the most consistently named across languages and the most visually distinct.
- Small color-coded symbols/marks and large background regions need opposite treatment: strongly saturated symbols against low-saturation, subdued large areas — because the chromatic channels have poor spatial resolution and large areas distort the appearance of small ones far more than the reverse.
- Rainbow colormaps are not a perceptually ordered sequence just because they follow wavelength order — they can obscure or exaggerate features; a monotonically increasing luminance ramp (possibly "spiraling" through hue as well) supports both pattern-finding and accurate value-reading better, and simple grayscale ramps, while good for pattern-finding, can produce large (~20%) reading errors due to contrast effects.
- Simultaneous contrast and color assimilation are not just illusions/errors — contrast supports real-world surface-color constancy (why a beach scene looks right on a screen at a fraction of real-world brightness), while assimilation shows perception can also decrease apparent color difference under conditions that are still poorly understood.
- Color meaning is culturally constructed and reverses across cultures (e.g., white = purity in the West, mourning across Asia), so symbolic color choices need audience-specific checking, whereas quantity-to-saturation/contrast mappings may generalize more safely.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The physiological setup (cone types, Williams' retinal images, the S/M/L sensitivity graph) is worth a quick read for vocabulary (opponent channels, unique hues) but doesn't need deep study. The heart of the chapter — and what's most reusable for teaching — is the "Channel Properties" section (contrast, unique hues, spatial detail, saturation, assimilation, luminance nonlinearity, segmentation, color blindness) and the "Principles for Design" section (showing detail, color-coding, large/small areas, emphasis/highlighting, color sequences, color on shaded surfaces). The semantics-of-color section at the end is short and mostly common-sense/cultural-caveat material that can be skimmed. The conclusion is a tight, quotable restatement worth reading even if skimming the body.

**Memorable specifics:**

- ISO recommends a minimum 3:1 luminance ratio between text and background — a concrete, citable number for the "always ensure luminance contrast" rule.
- Color-blind individuals (~8% of males) lose the red-green channel specifically, retaining yellow-blue discrimination and full grayscale perception — yellow-blue colorblindness is "much less common."
- Ware's own 1988 study found gray-scale colormap reading errors as large as 20% of the data range due to contrast effects (C. Ware, "Color sequences for univariate maps," IEEE CG&A, Sept. 1988, pp. 41–49).
- Berlin and Kay's 1960s survey of 100+ languages found black, white, red, green, yellow, and blue are the most universally named basic color terms — direct empirical support for "unique hues" as a design vocabulary.
- The chapter's closing line: "Mostly this is a matter of socially constructed taste—there are no absolute standards. Our goal in this book is to find the bedrock of functional aesthetics in the wiring of the brain, and sadly, we have little to say about beauty."
- R. Beau Lotto's checkerboard-cube demonstration: two patches that are the literal same color read as brown (top center) vs. yellow (front face) purely from apparent lighting context — a striking, reusable illusion for teaching context-dependence of color perception.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The ocean-height colormap comparison (p. 82: grayscale vs. green-red vs. rainbow vs. Francesca Samsel's dark-blue-white-dark-red double-ended map) — an excellent, ready-made teaching example of colormap tradeoffs between pattern-revealing and value-reading, with a clear "winner" and clear reasons why.

**Caveats:** This is a physiology-first, "functional aesthetics" account — deliberately narrow in scope (Ware explicitly excludes colorimetry and color reproduction/gamut mapping) and explicitly disclaims any theory of color beauty or taste, which he calls "socially constructed." Some claims are stated with more confidence than the evidence fully supports for a general audience — e.g., color-assimilation conditions are candidly described as "poorly understood," and the recommended six-to-twelve color-code limit is presented as a range from multiple studies rather than a single hard number. Readers should be careful not to over-generalize the opponent-channel model as literally three independent neural populations; Ware's own sidebar concedes "the reality is actually more complex, but opponent channel theory is an extremely useful simplification." The chapter also draws several examples from Ware's own prior publications (e.g., the 1988 colormap study), so some of the "principles" reflect one lab's body of work rather than a broader consensus survey.
