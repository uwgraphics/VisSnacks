# Ware, "Visual Queries" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 1)

**What it is:** This opening chapter lays out the book's central model of perception — that seeing is not passive registration of a complete visual world but an active, task-driven process built from a rapid sequence of "visual queries," each satisfied by an eye movement and the pattern-finding machinery that follows it. It introduces the bottom-up/top-down architecture of vision (features → patterns → objects, with attention working in reverse), the severe capacity limits of visual working memory, and the design implication that follows directly from all of this: good information design starts from an analysis of the cognitive tasks and visual queries a display must support.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with Simons and Levin's "door study": an actor asking for directions is swapped mid-conversation for a different person (sometimes a different gender) behind a passing door, and more than half of unsuspecting participants fail to notice. He uses this to motivate the chapter's core paradox — we feel like we see everything vividly and completely, yet we retain almost nothing outside of what we're currently attending to. The resolution, via Kevin O'Regan's line "the world is its own memory," is that the brain doesn't need to store the world internally because it can sample any part of it almost instantly with an eye movement; consciousness is better described as "rapid access" to a field of information rather than direct awareness of the world itself.

The chapter then works through the "apparatus of seeing." The eye is compared to a digital camera (upside-down image, three-cone color sampling, ~100 million receptors compressed to ~1 million optic-nerve fibers) but diverges sharply in that "brain pixels" are wildly non-uniform: the fovea resolves roughly 100 points across a pinhead at arm's length, while the periphery can barely resolve something the size of a human head — over half of visual processing power is devoted to the parafovea, under 5% of the visual field. Saccades — ballistic eye movements reaching angular velocities up to 900 degrees/second, each taking about a tenth of a second, with vision suppressed during the jump — are the mechanism that compensates for this non-uniformity, and we are unaware both of the jerkiness and of the blind spot (demonstrated with the classic X/B disappearing-dot exercise).

Ware then builds a three-stage bottom-up hierarchy — features (parallel, massively distributed processing of orientation, color, motion, depth in V1), patterns (mid-level Gestalt-style grouping into contours and regions), and objects (the tiny number, "about three," of nexus objects held in visual working memory at any moment) — and pairs it with the top-down story: attention biases feature detectors toward what's being sought, and Hayhoe and Ballard's eye-tracking study of people making a peanut butter and jelly sandwich shows this concretely — an initial burst of exploratory fixations to get "the lay of the land," followed by long, economical "just-in-time" fixations tied tightly to the action underway (e.g., looking ahead to a jar lid rather than at the hand). The chicken-and-egg problem of visual search (how do we know where to look if we haven't processed the scene, and why look if we already know what's there?) is resolved by positing a crude, low-level feature map that guides a coarse search before detailed processing occurs.

The design implications section works a sustained London Underground map example: a traveler planning a route poses a series of cognitive tasks (which lines connect, is there a shorter route, where to change trains, trip duration, distance to stations, cost), and Ware shows Harry Beck's iconic schematic map supports route-finding beautifully (clear color-coded contour-tracing) but almost completely fails to support proximity/distance tasks, because it deliberately distorts geography for clarity. He walks through the actual query sequence a rider's brain executes — locate start station, locate destination station, trace the connecting colored line via repeated fixations (~2 seconds), then estimate stop-count by judgment rather than counting — and generalizes this into a "nested loops" model: an outer problem-solving loop (find candidate strategies), a middle visual-search loop (eye movements tracing patterns), and an inner loop (pattern testing at each fixation, evaluating ~1-4 simple patterns per fixation at roughly 20 evaluations/second). He notes Google Maps as an example of "distributed cognition" extending outside the skull, invoking Don Norman's "the power of the unaided mind is highly overrated" quote, and closes the chapter's substance with a note on distributed, modular brain architecture (V1/V2, V4, fusiform gyrus, frontal lobes, lateral interparietal area) — deliberately kept functional/abstract rather than deep neuroscience.

The conclusion crystallizes the book's thesis: perceiving is "a skilled active process" of eye movements executed to satisfy information need; the designer's special skill is not drawing ability but "the talent to analyze a design in terms of its ability to support the visual queries of others." Ware explicitly distances the book's concerns from fine art (whose goals are often the opposite of clarity) and states the book's aim as helping designers move from intuitive to explicit, science-grounded understanding of visual task analysis.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Perception is task-driven and sample-based, not a complete internal picture — a display should be designed around the specific cognitive tasks and "visual queries" it needs to support, not around depicting all available data faithfully.
- Visual working memory holds only about 3-4 objects at a time; this is why external visual aids (diagrams, maps, dashboards) matter — they offload work that the brain's limited capacity cannot hold.
- "Just-in-time" processing (get information right when the task needs it, not before) explains both skilled visual behavior and change blindness — a design that requires holding many things in mind simultaneously will fail even if all the information is technically present on screen.
- A single visualization is rarely good at everything: the Underground map excels at route-tracing but fails at distance/proximity judgments — evaluate any chart or map against the specific list of questions it's meant to answer, not in the abstract.
- Attention operates top-down at every processing stage, from raw feature detection up through object recognition — what a viewer perceives is actively shaped by what they're looking for, which is a design lever (e.g., color-coding to make a sought pattern "shout louder").
- Design skill is fundamentally an analytic skill (task analysis → visual queries → color/form/space choices), separable from drawing/software skill — a claim directly relevant to teaching a systematic design process rather than a purely aesthetic one.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The core material — the door-swap experiment, the fovea/periphery non-uniformity, the bottom-up/top-down three-stage model, and the London Underground worked example with its four-step query breakdown and nested-loops diagram — is essential and highly teachable. The anatomical detail (specific brain regions, receptor counts, saccade velocities in degrees/second) is interesting color but skimmable for a design-focused course; it's there to ground the functional claims in real neuroscience, not because students need the numbers. The closing few paragraphs distinguishing this book's design-communication goals from fine art's expressive goals are a useful framing note but can be read quickly.

**Memorable specifics:**

- In the Simons/Levin door study, "more than 50 percent of the time" people failed to notice their conversation partner had been swapped for a different person — "Incredibly, people even failed to notice a change in gender!"
- Kevin O'Regan's line: "the world is its own memory" — the resolution to why we feel we see everything but retain almost nothing.
- The eye/brain has ~100 million retinal receptors feeding into only ~1 million optic-nerve fibers, comparable to a digital camera's data compression.
- The fovea resolves about 100 points across the head of a pin at arm's length; at the edge of the visual field we can barely resolve something the size of a human head — "over half of our visual processing power is concentrated in" the fovea/parafovea, covering less than 5% of the visual field.
- Saccades reach angular velocities of up to 900 degrees per second and take about one-tenth of a second; vision is suppressed during the movement itself.
- Visual working memory holds "about three objects" at a time — the "temporary nexus of meaning and action" — and this is why the door-swap experiment fooled people: the interlocutor's face wasn't task-relevant and got displaced.
- Don Norman (quoted): "The power of the unaided mind is highly overrated... How have we increased memory, thought and reasoning? By the invention of external aids: it is things that make us smart."

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The three-panel door-swap cartoon (p.2) — a clean, classroom-ready illustration of change blindness that pairs perfectly with the >50% failure-rate statistic.
- The brain-pixel "distorted map" diagram (p.5) showing wildly non-uniform circle sizes across the visual field, plus the companion blurred-spreadsheet/blurred-person photos (p.6) — a strong, immediately intuitive demonstration of foveal vs. peripheral acuity for a lecture on visual attention.
- The bar-chart-vs-map "visual queries" example (p.4) — a compact, reusable illustration of how the same underlying cognitive act (search-then-read) applies across totally different chart types.
- The feature → pattern → object pipeline diagram with the dog (p.9-11) — a good schematic for teaching the bottom-up/top-down processing hierarchy in one image.
- The Hayhoe/Ballard peanut-butter-sandwich eye-tracking photo (p.13), with its yellow fixation-circle overlay — a vivid, real-data illustration of "just-in-time" eye movement strategy that instructors can point to when discussing task-driven attention.
- The London Underground map itself (p.15) plus the nested-loops diagram (p.18) — together they make an excellent worked example for a lecture segment on task analysis: a famous, familiar visualization critiqued rigorously against a concrete list of cognitive tasks it does and doesn't support.

**Caveats:** This is an introductory, scene-setting chapter, so its neuroscience is deliberately simplified ("we will simplify to a three-stage model") and several claims are stated with more confidence than hedging (e.g., "about three objects" in visual working memory, "about 20 per second" pattern evaluation) — these are useful rules of thumb for teaching but shouldn't be quoted as precise, uncontested figures without checking Ware's citations in later chapters. The chapter is explicit that it is not a neuroscience text and that exact brain locations "ultimately does not matter" for design purposes, which is a fair caveat to pass on to students who might otherwise over-index on the anatomical labels. The Underground map critique is a designed illustration of a general principle rather than a comprehensive design case study — it's worth noting Ware himself says "this is not to say that it is a bad design" to avoid students walking away thinking Beck's map is being criticized rather than used as a teaching example of trade-offs.
