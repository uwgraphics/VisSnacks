# Tufte, "Color and Information" (*Envisioning Information*, ch. 5)

**What it is:** Chapter 5 of Edward Tufte's *Envisioning Information* examines how color can help or hurt the communication of information, working through the four fundamental uses of color in information design (label, measure, represent, decorate) via a running cast of examples — Swiss relief maps, architectural drawings, a redesigned visual Euclid, computer interfaces, bathymetric charts, and value scales — building toward a set of concrete design rules and a warning about color's perceptual instability.

**AI summary:** Tufte opens by noting the paradox of color: human eyes can discriminate roughly 1,000,000 colors under contrived pairwise comparison, and about 20,000 colors are practically accessible, yet for encoding abstract information "more than 20 or 30 colors frequently produce not diminishing but negative returns." He frames the task with Paul Klee's line that painting well is simply "to put the right color in the right place," and immediately complicates it: "so difficult and subtle that avoiding catastrophe becomes the first principle in bringing color to information." He identifies four fundamental uses of color in information design, exemplified by a Swiss topographic map of the Matterhorn region: to **label** (color as noun — distinguishing water, glacier, and field), to **measure** (color as quantity — contour darkening for altitude), to **represent or imitate reality** (color as representation — river blues, shadow hachures), and to **enliven or decorate** (color as beauty).

A large portion of the chapter is organized around Eduard Imhof's four rules from *Cartographic Relief Presentation* for avoiding color damage. The **first rule**: pure, bright, strong colors have loud effects when unrelieved over large adjacent areas, but can work in small doses against dull backgrounds — "Noise is not music. Only a piano allows a crescendo and then a forte, and only on a quiet background can a colorful theme be constructed." Tufte illustrates the violation with a garish 1970 US home-heating-fuel county map. The **second rule**: placing light, bright colors mixed with white next to each other over large areas usually produces unpleasant results — illustrated by a "puffy white band" artifact and "1 + 1 = 3" effects (colors interacting to produce a visual effect greater than the sum of the parts). Positively, Daniel Burnham's 1909 Plan of Chicago drawing shows how "color spots against a light gray or muted field highlight and italicize data, and also help to weave an overall harmony." The **third rule** (introduced later, alongside a Gretchen Garner birch-grove photo series): large background/base-colors should work quietly so small bright areas stand out vividly — "gray is regarded in painting to be one of the prettiest, most important and most versatile of colors." Tufte's own prescription: build palettes from colors found in nature (skies, shadows — mostly blues, yellows, grays) since they carry "a widely accepted harmony to the human eye," reserving strong spot color for local emphasis. The **fourth rule**: unity in multi-color pictures requires that colors be repeated/interwoven ("scattered like islands") rather than segregated into solid blocks, or "the picture falls apart" — Tufte notes Mondrian and Malevich routinely violate this rule, "the problem is with the rule not Mondrian."

A long central case study is Oliver Byrne's 1847 color-coded edition of Euclid's *Geometry*, which replaces algebraic letter-labels (angle DEF) with colored/shaped diagram elements — "color serves as a label most nobly of all." Tufte praises how Byrne uses only primary colors plus black (maximizing differentiation, since "no four colors differ more"), keeps black — the least-used color — off large solid areas, and uses spacious type leading to create "lines" of type visually harmonious with the geometric shapes. He connects Byrne's 1847 design to 20th-century Neo-Plasticism/De Stijl (Mondrian, van Doesburg), noting Byrne "anticipates the pure primary colors, asymmetrical layout, angularity... characteristic" of that movement decades early.

Turning to computer displays, Tufte critiques the "grim parody of a video game" that garish interface color schemes can produce, contrasted with a de-gridded design where color defines edges without clutter. He notes screens face "two powerful information-processing capabilities, human and computer," but all communication passes through a "low-resolution, narrow-band video display terminal, which chokes off fast, precise, and complex communication" — color can help by softening bright-white glare and, for framing active windows, yellow uniquely satisfies the joint requirement of being light in value (avoiding 1+1=3 clutter) yet intense/saturated (giving a strong signal). He also floats using color's inherent 3D structure (hue/saturation/value or RGB) to encode multidimensional data, citing Ware and Beatty's scatterplot-matrix work showing color clusters as an "obvious improvement over black-only dots."

On quantification, Tufte discusses the *General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans*, which encodes ocean depth (bathymetric tints) and land height (hypsometric tints) in 21 steps under the metaphor "the deeper or higher, the darker," with numbered contours outlining color fields to improve reading accuracy — "every color mark on this map signals four variables: latitude, longitude, sea or land, and depth or altitude." He warns that value scales, while easy to learn, are vulnerable to "disturbing contextual effects" like edge fluting and simultaneous contrast, and that rainbow (ROYGBIV) scales, though visually clear light-to-dark, can reduce viewers to "mumbling color names and the numbers they represent" — contrary to Paul Valéry's axiom "To see is to forget the name of the thing one sees."

The chapter's perceptual core draws on Josef Albers's *Interaction of Color*: adjacent color fields shift each other's apparent hue via "subtraction" of color from the surrounding field, and carefully engineered pairs can make two different colors look identical, or make one color look like two different colors depending on surround — demonstrated with matched gray/blue squares against different backgrounds. Because these contextual shifts are real but unpredictable, "color differences should not be relied upon as the sole method for sending a message amidst a mosaic of complex and variable data." His remedy is **multiple/redundant signals**: a road map example where thin red lines double as small-road labels but a deeper red flanked by parallel blue stripes marks larger roads, extending the effective palette without an extra printing plate. He cautions that redundancy has a cost too — "a resulting danger of fussy, cluttered, insecure, committee-style design. A crystalline, lucid redundancy will do" — and that redundant encoding should be reserved for cases where ambiguity is a real, diminishable risk, not applied gratuitously.

The chapter closes with paired historical map examples (the bathymetric chart and 18th-century Hampshire county maps by Badeslade/Toms) showing how contour lines combined with color fields create "cognitive contours" and a "homogeneous edged field," versus a case where broad, unrefined color bands merely restate boundaries already visible in the outline, adding "so much visual excitement, so little data." Tufte also notes color perception's practical hazards: the translation from printed color to quantitative data is "nonlinear (thus gamma curves), often noisy and idiosyncratic," varies substantially among viewers, and several percent of viewers are color-deficient — so crucial distinctions should not hinge on red/green differences alone.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Color in information design serves four distinct functions — to label, to measure, to represent/imitate reality, and to decorate — and good designs are explicit about which job each color choice is doing.
- "Avoiding catastrophe" is the realistic first goal with color: bad color is easy and common, so the design rules here are mostly damage-control (Imhof's four rules), not recipes for beauty.
- Strong, saturated colors over large adjacent areas cause loud, unpleasant "1 + 1 = 3" perceptual effects; save vivid color for small spots against quiet, muted, or gray backgrounds — this is the single most transferable rule for dashboards, maps, and slides.
- Color differences are perceptually unstable (simultaneous contrast, edge fluting, Albers's "subtraction of color") and should never be the *sole* channel carrying critical distinctions — pair color with redundant cues (contours, labels, position) when the ambiguity risk is real, but don't over-apply redundancy or you get cluttered, "committee-style" design.
- Nature-derived palettes (muted sky/shadow blues, yellows, grays) provide an accepted perceptual harmony and a principled way to suppress "garish, content-empty colorjunk," reserving bright accents for genuine emphasis.
- Rainbow/value scales for quantity are common but imperfect: value scales fight contextual shifts, and ROYGBIV encoding can make viewers name colors instead of reading data — a caution relevant to any modern discussion of colormap design (viridis, etc., even though not named here).
- Because a real fraction of viewers are color-deficient and color-to-number translation is nonlinear and noisy, critical data distinctions (especially red/green) need a non-color-dependent backup.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The four Imhof rules (pp. 82, 90) and the Albers color-interaction discussion (pp. 92–93) are the essential theoretical core — read these closely, they're the load-bearing content for a course. The extended Oliver Byrne Euclid case study (pp. 84–87), while visually striking and useful as a "color as label" teaching example, can be skimmed for its historical/typographic detail (Ruari McLean quotes, Mondrian's Neo-Plasticism manifesto) unless the point is specifically about labeling-by-color. The computer-interface section (pp. 88–89) is dated (video display terminals, 1980s GUIs) but the underlying point about bandwidth-limited screens and yellow's joint light/saturated property is still a useful illustration of a design tradeoff. The closing map comparisons (pp. 94–95) can be skimmed quickly as reinforcing examples of the redundancy/contour principle already established.

**Memorable specifics:**

- "A trained colorist can distinguish among 1,000,000 colors... Some 20,000 colors are accessible to many viewers... For encoding abstract information, however, more than 20 or 30 colors frequently produce not diminishing but negative returns."
- Imhof's First Rule, quoting H. Windisch: "Noise is not music. Only a piano allows a crescendo and then a forte, and only on a quiet background can a colorful theme be constructed."
- Yellow is identified as the only color that is simultaneously light in value (to avoid glare/clutter) and intense/saturated (to give a strong signal) — hence its use for framing active windows on screen.
- The *General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans* encodes depth/height in 21 tint steps, with "every color mark on this map signals four variables: latitude, longitude, sea or land, and depth or altitude."
- Oliver Byrne's 1847 Euclid used only primary colors plus black because "no four colors differ more," anticipating Mondrian/De Stijl by roughly 80 years.
- Albers on color interaction: "any ground subtracts its own hue from colors which it carries and therefore influences" — demonstrated with matched-looking squares that are actually different colors, and different-looking squares that are actually the same color.
- Reproducing the chapter's color comparison examples required "21 separate flat colors for this signature" (per the citation note) — a concrete indicator of the production complexity of teaching with real printed color.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The Imhof "puffy white band" bad-example and the 1970 home-heating-fuel US county map (p. 82) — a clean, immediately legible negative example of Imhof's first/second rules for a slide on "what not to do with color."
- The Albers matched-square color-interaction pairs (pp. 92–93) — the single best teaching image for simultaneous contrast/contextual color shift; directly demonstrates why color-only encoding is risky.
- Oliver Byrne's visual Pythagorean proof (pp. 84–87) — a striking, reusable example of "color as label" that reads well even out of context and pairs nicely with a Mondrian comparison for a design-history aside.
- The bathymetric/hypsometric ocean chart (pp. 90–91) — a strong worked example of a value-scale-plus-contour "sensitive multiplicity" design, useful for teaching redundant encoding done well.

**Caveats:** This chapter is a designer's essay built from illustrative examples and historical case studies, not an empirical study — the "rules" (Imhof's four, plus Tufte's nature-palette and redundancy guidance) are craft heuristics, asserted with authority but not tested against reader studies in the text itself. The computer-interface material (scroll bars, video display terminals, gamma curves) is visibly dated to late-1980s hardware and shouldn't be over-read as current UI guidance, though the underlying bandwidth/glare tradeoffs still generalize. Readers sometimes flatten Tufte's nuanced position on rainbow/value scales into a blanket "never use rainbow colormaps" rule; the text itself is more measured, noting rainbow scales are "widely-used" and effective for a light-to-dark visual sequence, just vulnerable to viewers naming colors instead of reading values.
