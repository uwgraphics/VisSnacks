# Heer & Shneiderman, "Interactive Dynamics for Visual Analysis" (Communications of the ACM, Vol. 55, No. 4, April 2012, DOI:10.1145/2133806.2133821)

**What it is:** A practitioner-oriented taxonomy essay, not a system paper or an empirical study. It organizes the interaction techniques used in visual analytics tools into a checklist-style taxonomy — 12 task types grouped into 3 high-level categories — and illustrates each task with one or two named example systems. This particular PDF is the *Communications of the ACM* "practice" section printing (April 2012, pp. 45–54); the masthead notes the article was originally "developed" through ACM Queue (queue.acm.org) before running in CACM, so it should be cited as the CACM 2012 version rather than the Queue original. The stated audience is broad and explicit: designers, researchers, professional analysts, procurement officers, educators, and students — the goal is to give all of them a shared vocabulary for evaluating and building visual analysis tools, not to advance a new technique or theory of its own.

**AI summary:** The paper opens from a simple but consequential observation: a single static image typically answers only a handful of questions, so real visual analysis is inherently iterative — a repeated cycle of view creation, exploration, and refinement as analysts develop insight about relationships, context-specific patterns, and causal structure in data. The quality of the *interactive* layer therefore matters as much as the quality of the *encoding*: confusing widgets, hidden operations, complex dialog boxes, or slow response times constrain how much of a topic an analyst can consider and can introduce outright errors, while tools that support "fluent and flexible" interaction at rates resonant with human thought let analysts pursue questions, uncover patterns, and catch errors more effectively. The authors are explicit that they are deliberately setting aside the visual-encoding side of the field (bar charts, scatter plots, timelines, node-link diagrams, and the perceptual principles behind them) to focus entirely on the *interactive dynamics* layered on top of those encodings — and they note up front that within each branch of the taxonomy the example systems are illustrative, not an exhaustive survey; each is chosen to convey the nature and diversity of the operations, not to catalog every tool that supports them. The paper also defines "analyst" broadly and deliberately: not a specific person or job title, but anyone seeking to understand data — traditional financial or intelligence analysts, scientists exploring new findings, journalists piecing together a story, or ordinary people tracking personal data like blood pressure, spending, electricity use, or miles traveled.

The taxonomy itself (given as a table early in the paper) has three high-level categories, each containing four task types:

**Data and View Specification** — the tasks by which an analyst tells the system what to show. **Visualize**: choosing visual encodings for the data — the "chart typology" approach familiar from spreadsheet programs (pick a chart type, assign variables to axes/size/color) is simple and familiar but limits the space of possible visualizations; the paper contrasts it with data-flow graphs (used in classic scientific-visualization systems) and formal grammars — Wilkinson's *Grammar of Graphics*, ggplot2, and Protovis are named — which describe visual mappings more expressively and can even drive automated generation of multiple visualization suggestions from a partial specification. Tableau (referred to by its research name, Polaris) is the worked example: it lets analysts specify visualizations by dragging data variables onto "shelves" corresponding to visual encodings like spatial position, size, shape, and color (Figure 1). **Filter**: because analysts rarely want to see an entire dataset at once, tools need controls — direct selection (e.g., lassoing then hiding/highlighting) or *dynamic query widgets* (a term the paper attributes to Shneiderman's own earlier work, ref. 17) — for controlling item visibility. The choice of widget follows data type: checkboxes/radio buttons for small categorical sets, scrollable/searchable lists for large or free-text categorical sets, sliders for a single quantitative threshold, range sliders for a bounded interval; Spotfire and Google Hotel Search are the named examples (Figure 2), and CrimeSpotting.org's zoomable crime map (Figure 3) shows query widgets for time-of-day, date span, and crime type augmented with a histogram of the underlying distribution. **Sort**: reordering records by one or more variable values, or by a more meaningful unit of analysis (days of week, fiscal quarters), can surface trends and clusters directly; the paper notes sorting gets harder for data that doesn't have an obvious linear order (multivariate tables, networks), which instead needs *seriation* methods (citing Wilkinson) that reorder items to minimize a distance measure and reveal latent structure — illustrated with a reorderable adjacency-matrix visualization of a social network (Figure 4) where alphabetical ordering shows almost no structure, sorting by node degree reveals more, and permuting by network connectivity reveals community clusters. TimeSearcher (Hochheiser & Shneiderman) is cited for querying time series by slope via an angular selection widget (Figure 5). **Derive**: as analysis proceeds, analysts often find the raw input data insufficient and need to transform it or compute new attributes — normalization or log transforms for comparability, or descriptive statistics (mean, median, variance), model fits (regression curves), or group-by aggregates (counts, sums) to summarize the data, often exposed through a calculation language resembling spreadsheet or database query syntax. The authors flag derivation as "a promising frontier" for VA research: open questions include programming-by-demonstration for defining patterns, automatic statistical model fitting tied to the current visualization state (e.g., mapping "pivot"-table nesting onto a linear model), and more principled frameworks unifying visualization with statistical modeling/forecasting, which the paper says are "still emerging." Figure 6 (selection queries in a parallel-coordinates plot of car attributes — cylinders, displacement, weight, horsepower, acceleration, mileage, year) closes the section, showing a derived/filtered subset of low-weight, high-mileage cars highlighted across all axes.

**View Manipulation** — the tasks by which an analyst manipulates an already-created view to highlight patterns, test hypotheses, and drill into detail. **Select**: pointing to an item or region is treated as a fundamentally communicative act (the paper draws an explicit analogy to how people coordinate gesture, gaze, and speech in physical conversation, citing named hand-gesture types — oriented flat hand for angle, horizontal flat hand for height, thumb-and-index "C" shape for intervals, circling for groupings, an accelerating fist for force); in software this range collapses to a narrower set of actions — hover, click, rectangular/elliptical/free-form lasso regions, and area cursors such as "brushes" or a dynamic bubble cursor that selects whichever item is currently closest to the pointer. **Navigate**: how an analyst moves through a large information space — scrolling, panning, and zooming (citing Shneiderman's widely-cited mantra "overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand"), contrasted with van Ham and Perer's alternative pattern "search, show context, expand on demand" for cases where a full overview isn't practical (their example: a legal analyst who shouldn't start from an overview of the entire history of U.S. court decisions, but instead keyword-searches to the most relevant cases and expands outward via citations). The paper distinguishes *semantic zooming* (changing what information is shown, not just its scale — CrimeSpotting's markers gaining detail labels as the analyst zooms in) from purely geometric zoom, and covers two named strategies for maintaining context while focusing on detail: *focus+context* / *overview+detail* paired displays (a zoomed-in detail map alongside a small zoomed-out overview map with a highlighted viewport rectangle), and *distortion* techniques such as fisheye views (the Mac OS X dock's one-dimensional fisheye is the everyday example) — the authors are candid that more sophisticated multidimensional distortion methods, while visually striking, have "yet to prove their worth in real-world applications," disorienting viewers without measurable performance gains over simple zooming. Navigate also covers *degree-of-interest* (DOI) functions (citing Heer & Card's DOITrees work), which combine a general-importance score (e.g., a node's centrality or hierarchy level) with a current-interest score (e.g., recent clicks or search matches) to decide what to show, elide, or reduce to an aggregate — illustrated with a DOI tree over a 600,000-item multilingual taxonomy (Figure 7) that reveals and hides branches dynamically as an analyst clicks or searches. **Coordinate**: linking multiple views of the same data so a selection in one view highlights or filters corresponding items in the others — *brushing and linking* is the named mechanism (citing Becker, Cleveland & Wilks), demonstrated on a baseball statistics dashboard in GGobi (Figure 9) where selecting high-income players in one scatterplot shows little relationship to career length or fielding, but strong correlation with hitting performance in a linked plot, while selecting a different cluster (players who record more assists than putouts) reveals a strong positional dependence elsewhere. Tufte's *small multiples* (a set of visualizations sharing spatial layout, measures, and scale to enable rapid comparison) is cited as one coordination pattern; Improvise's multiview election-analysis dashboard (Figure 8, analyzing Michigan election outcomes) is the example of heterogeneous linked views (bar chart, map, table, scatter plot) with annotations showing how selections correspond across views. The authors flag that coordinated views, while more effective than overloading a single visualization with dimensions, can also confuse newcomers or analysts returning to a session, and call for future research on how coordination behaviors are specified and understood. **Organize**: managing the layout of multiple visualization windows, legends, and controls — tiled layouts to avoid scrolling, tabs for switching view sets, trellised/small-multiple view generation (e.g., automatically producing one bar chart per region), and more advanced automated layout support (scatterplot matrices, auto-resizing as views are added/removed). The authors note this becomes more consequential as large displays, and conversely tablets and phones, become common, calling for compact, gesture-reconfigurable layouts.

**Process and Provenance** — the tasks that scaffold the longer, often social and multi-session process of analysis, not just the moment-to-moment manipulation of a single view. **Record**: capturing interaction histories (view specifications, sorts, filters, zooms) beyond basic undo/redo, typically visualized as a chronological "timeline" or sequential "comic strip" (Figure 10, Improvise's business-operations history display, citing Heer et al.'s "graphical histories" paper). Histories support revisiting and resuming prior states, can carry metadata (comments, tags, ratings) for review, can be saved as reusable named macros for repeating an analysis across similar datasets, and can be exported (screenshots to full interactive presentations) for sharing or later study of analytic process. **Annotate**: textual or freeform graphical annotation of findings — freeform graphical marks (circling a cluster, arrows pointing at a peak) are expressive and can carry emotional/emphatic cues via angle, color, or shape, but the paper stresses they lack an explicit tie to the underlying data unless made *data-aware*: representing an annotation as a selection (a set of items, a declarative query, or both) lets the same annotation be reapplied across different views or encodings of the same data, and makes it machine-searchable. Sense.us (Figure 11, citing Heer, Viégas & Wattenberg's "Voyagers and Voyeurs") is the worked example, combining an interactive visualization, graphical annotation tools, bookmark trails for saved views, a comment entry field, and a shareable, auto-updating URL. **Share**: visual analytics as a social, not purely individual, process — export of views/data subsets, and collaboration strategies that depend on whether collaborators work synchronously vs. asynchronously and co-located vs. distributed (each needing different access-control, presence, and activity-awareness support). *Application bookmarking* — modeling and exporting a tool's internal state as a shareable URL/identifier, richer than a static screenshot, so a collaborator can resume exactly where another left off — is named as a simple but effective aid; the paper flags an open design question of whether a bookmarked view should snapshot historical data, show current live data, or offer both. *Publishing* dashboards (Spotfire and Tableau are named as commercial examples supporting interactive selection/search/drill-down after publication) and embedding via services like IBM's Many Eyes are the second sharing mode, with the caveat that publishing alone is necessary but not sufficient for engagement — embedding within an already-active blog or forum audience is called out as often more effective than a standalone site. **Guide**: formalized workflows that lead newcomers through common analysis tasks or give experts progress indicators, as opposed to ad hoc exploration. SocialAction (Figure 12, citing Perer & Shneiderman's "systematic yet flexible discovery") is the example — it structures social-network analysis into a sequence of steps (rank nodes, plot nodes, find communities, and more) with progress indicators, while still letting analysts skip steps rather than forcing a strict pipeline. The paper's last named category is *narrative visualization* (citing Segel & Heer), the journalistic practice — exemplified by interactive graphics from the New York Times, Washington Post, and the Guardian — of guiding a reader through a linear, annotated story built on the data (Figure 13, a New York Times budget-forecast graphic) before opening up into freeform exploration at the story's end; the authors describe this narrative structure as doing double duty, communicating key findings while also functioning as a "tacit tutorial" that demonstrates the available interactive controls to the reader before they're asked to use them unaided.

The paper closes with a short conclusion and an explicit statement of scope: the taxonomy is meant to give students/newcomers orientation, give developers a checklist for building new tools, and give researchers a map of underinvestigated areas (new methods for interactive view specification, closer integration of visualization and statistical modeling, and better approaches to guided analytics). The authors are candid that any taxonomy of a broad domain will be imperfect — some techniques span categories (dynamic query widgets serve both Filter and Navigate; selection techniques underpin both Select and data-aware Annotate) — and that they deliberately did not go deep on implementation concerns: real-time interactivity at scale (low-latency architectures, sampling/aggregation methods), how best to integrate statistical methods into visualization environments (of which the Derive discussion, they say, "only scratches the surface"), and data formatting/cleaning/integration, which they call a significant barrier for newcomers even though it's out of scope here.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The 12-task/3-category taxonomy (Data and View Specification: visualize, filter, sort, derive; View Manipulation: select, navigate, coordinate, organize; Process and Provenance: record, annotate, share, guide) is a clean, reusable checklist for critiquing *any* interactive visualization tool — "what does this tool let you do, and which of these 12 things is missing or clunky?" is a directly transferable exercise.
- The framing that a single static view only answers a handful of questions, and that real analysis is an iterative loop of view creation → exploration → refinement, is the paper's core justification for why interaction deserves as much design attention as encoding — a good one-line answer to "why does interaction matter, not just the chart type?"
- Several named design patterns are worth knowing independent of the taxonomy itself: Shneiderman's "overview first, zoom and filter, details-on-demand" mantra versus van Ham & Perer's "search, show context, expand on demand" as two different, situationally appropriate navigation strategies; brushing and linking as the mechanism behind coordinated multiview dashboards; degree-of-interest (DOI) functions as a principled way to decide what a focus+context display shows versus elides.
- The distinction between a freeform (merely visual) annotation and a *data-aware* annotation (backed by a selection or query) is a small but sharp idea worth remembering: it's the difference between an annotation that survives panning/zooming but becomes meaningless after filtering or re-encoding, and one that can be faithfully reapplied across different views of the same underlying data.
- Narrative visualization's double duty — telling a story *and* tacitly teaching the reader the available interactions before freeform exploration opens up — is a reusable idea for anyone designing an explorable data story, not just journalists.
- The paper is a good example of a taxonomy article that is explicit about its own incompleteness: several categories openly overlap (dynamic query widgets are both Filter and Navigate tools), which is a useful reminder that clean, non-overlapping taxonomies are the exception, not the rule, in this literature.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The introduction (framing) and the taxonomy table are short and worth reading closely — they set the vocabulary the rest of the paper reuses. The three main sections (Data and View Specification, View Manipulation, Process and Provenance) are each short subsections per task (a paragraph or two) built around one or two named example systems and a figure; they read quickly and reward reading start to finish rather than skimming, since each subsection's payoff is largely the specific example (Tableau's shelves, DOI trees, Sense.us, SocialAction, etc.) rather than abstract claims that could be skimmed for gist. The figures (13 total, mostly screenshots of named tools) are worth glancing at alongside the text they illustrate rather than skipped, since the prose regularly refers to specific plot regions in a figure (e.g., the left/center/right panels of Figure 4's reorderable matrix, or the left/right plots of Figure 9's baseball brushing example). The Conclusion is short and mainly restates scope/limitations — worth a quick read for the explicit "what we left out" list, but it introduces no new taxonomy content. The reference list and author bios are reference material only.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The taxonomy: 12 task types in 3 categories — Data and View Specification (visualize, filter, sort, derive), View Manipulation (select, navigate, coordinate, organize), Process and Provenance (record, annotate, share, guide).
- Named systems/techniques cited as exemplars: Tableau/Polaris (visualize, drag-and-drop shelves, Fig. 1); Spotfire and Google Hotel Search (filter, dynamic query widgets, Fig. 2); CrimeSpotting.org (filter/navigate, semantic zooming on crime markers, Fig. 3); reorderable social-network matrix via seriation (sort, Fig. 4); TimeSearcher (sort/query, angular slope selection, Fig. 5); parallel coordinates over a car dataset (derive/select, Fig. 6); a 600k-item multilingual taxonomy tree (navigate, degree-of-interest, Fig. 7); Improvise's Michigan election dashboard (coordinate, Fig. 8); GGobi baseball dashboard (coordinate, brushing and linking, Fig. 9); Improvise's "comic strip" history view (record, Fig. 10); Sense.us (annotate/share, Fig. 11, citing "Voyagers and Voyeurs"); SocialAction (guide, systematic-yet-flexible workflow with skippable steps, Fig. 12); New York Times budget-forecast graphic (guide, narrative visualization, Fig. 13).
- Two contrasting navigation mantras, both named and quoted: Shneiderman's "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand" and van Ham & Perer's "Search, show context, expand on demand."
- Named hand-gesture types cited as an analogy for selection's communicative role: oriented flat hand (angle), horizontal flat hand (height), thumb-and-index "C" shape (interval), circling (grouping), accelerating fist (force).
- The Mac OS X dock is cited by name as the paper's everyday example of one-dimensional fisheye distortion.
- Commercial/named publishing tools called out by name for dashboard publishing: Spotfire, Tableau, and IBM's Many Eyes.
- The paper's own honest self-critique in the Conclusion: dynamic query widgets serve as both a filter and a navigation mechanism, and selection techniques underpin annotation — offered by the authors themselves as evidence the taxonomy's categories are not fully disjoint.
- Explicit statement that distortion-based focus+context techniques "have yet to prove their worth in real-world applications" and show "no significant performance improvement" over simpler zooming — a rare direct critical claim in an otherwise descriptive/celebratory survey.

**Caveats:** This is an essay/survey, not an empirical study — it makes no experimental claims, reports no user study data of its own, and the "taxonomy" is the authors' own organizing scheme rather than something derived from a systematic literature-coding process (contrast this with Sarikaya et al. 2018's QCA methodology, which counts and validates categories against a sampled corpus). The authors state directly that the example systems in each branch are illustrative, not an exhaustive survey of tools supporting that task, so absence of a technique or system from the paper is not evidence it doesn't exist or wasn't considered. The taxonomy's categories are explicitly acknowledged by the authors as overlapping in places (dynamic query widgets span Filter and Navigate; selection underlies Select and data-aware Annotate), so it should be read as a useful organizing checklist rather than a strict partition. The paper deliberately excludes visual-encoding design (chart types, perceptual/color principles) from its scope, deferring readers to other books/articles for that material — a reader looking for encoding guidance will not find it here. It also deliberately stays shallow on implementation and systems concerns (real-time performance at scale, statistical-modeling integration, data cleaning/formatting), flagging these explicitly as open problems rather than covering them. One minor internal-consistency note: the paper's own taxonomy table lists Filter under "Data and View Specification," but the body text discusses dynamic query filtering again at length under "View Manipulation" (via the CrimeSpotting Figure 3 discussion of pan/zoom and query widgets together) — this isn't an error so much as the overlap the authors themselves flag in the Conclusion, but a reader skimming only the table might not expect filtering to resurface there.
