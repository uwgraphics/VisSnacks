# Moritz, Wang, Nelson, Lin, Smith, Howe & Heer, "Formalizing Visualization Design Knowledge as Constraints: Actionable and Extensible Models in Draco" (IEEE TVCG, Proc. InfoVis 2018 / published 2019)

**What it is:** A systems/formalization paper, in the same lineage as Mackinlay's APT — it doesn't propose a new chart type but a new *representation* for automated visualization design knowledge, plus a working system (Draco) built on that representation. The paper has three moving parts it develops in sequence: (1) a knowledge-representation scheme — visualizations as sets of logical facts, design guidelines as hard and soft constraints over those facts, implemented in Answer Set Programming (ASP); (2) a way to *learn* the soft-constraint weights from ranked pairs of visualizations (a "learning to rank" formulation trained with RankSVM) instead of hand-tuning them; and (3) three demonstrations of increasing ambition — reimplementing Mackinlay's APT, reimplementing Wongsuphasawat et al.'s CompassQL (the engine behind Voyager/Voyager2), and then going beyond both by learning weights directly from two independent graphical-perception studies (Kim et al. and Saket et al.). The paper closes with a discussion of Draco as a software-engineering move (decoupling knowledge representation from search implementation) and four speculative extensions (spell-checker, design-space corpus generation, cross-study comparison tool, sub-symbolic re-ranking), plus an appendix reinterpreting the preference model as a Markov logic network.

**AI summary:** The motivating gap (Section 1) is that empirical vision-science and perception studies keep producing new visualization-design knowledge, but that knowledge rarely makes it into actual tools — existing automated design systems (APT, SAGE, ShowMe, CompassQL) each bake their design knowledge into bespoke, idiosyncratic implementations that are hard to extend, compare, or combine. The paper's core claim is that encoding design knowledge *declaratively*, as a shared, extensible library of constraints rather than procedural code, would let the field accumulate and reuse design knowledge the way a shared codebase accumulates features — tool builders get to reuse existing constraint solvers instead of re-implementing search, and researchers get a common medium for expressing and comparing design guidelines.

Section 2 positions Draco against three lines of prior work it explicitly builds on. First, automated visualization design: Mackinlay's **APT** ranks encodings by *expressiveness* and *effectiveness* using ~200 rules in a declarative logic program with depth-first search and backtracking; the **SAGE** project extended APT with cardinality, uniqueness, and functional-dependency reasoning; Casner's **BOZ** added low-level perceptual reading/comparing-value tasks; **ShowMe** used heuristics for encoding suggestion including small-multiples/trellis layouts; and Wongsuphasawat et al.'s **CompassQL** (the query language behind **Voyager**/**Voyager2**) uses hand-tuned scores for space-efficiency and legibility based on encoding and data properties, but is implemented as imperative JavaScript with heuristic, ad-hoc rules — Draco is described as "most similar to CompassQL and its weighted preferences," differing mainly in using a declarative constraint solver and a learned, "programmatic" way of turning experimental results into preference weights rather than hand-tuning them. Second, effectiveness models: beyond Bertin and Cleveland & McGill (which APT already draws on), the paper cites newer work on reading *ensembles* of marks — aggregates, distributions, trends, correlation — including studies by Kim et al. and Saket et al. showing effectiveness varies by task and by data characteristics like cardinality and entropy; Draco's contribution here is combining classical single-mark channel-effectiveness rankings with this newer task/data-characteristic-dependent knowledge inside one declarative system, using a simple task taxonomy borrowed from Kim et al.: **value tasks** (reading/comparing individual values) versus **summary tasks** (comparing ensembles). Third, visualization specification languages: Draco's design space is built on **Vega-Lite** (itself descended from Wilkinson's Grammar of Graphics, ggplot2, and Tableau's VizQL), specifically the single-view and faceted-view (row/column) subset — enough to express bar charts, histograms, dot plots, scatter plots, line graphs, and area graphs, but not layered or arbitrarily concatenated multi-view specs. Fourth, constraint programming generally, and specifically why ASP: imperative recommendation engines like CompassQL waste effort generating and testing infeasible candidates and require overhauling generator code whenever the specification changes, whereas a sufficiently expressive constraint language (the paper picks **ASP**, solved with the **Clingo** solver) lets new tests be added declaratively without new search algorithms.

Section 3 is a self-contained primer on Answer Set Programming for readers unfamiliar with it: atoms/literals/rules, facts (bodiless rules), integrity constraints (headless rules that eliminate contradictory answer sets), "stable models"/"answer sets" as the solutions ASP computes, aggregate rules (e.g., `1 { mark(bar); mark(line); mark(area); mark(point) } 1.` to pick exactly one mark type), and — the mechanism the whole preference model rests on — **soft constraints**, written `:~ Body. [w]`, which *can* be violated but each violation adds a fixed weighted penalty `w` to a solution's cost; an optimal answer set minimizes total weighted violation cost.

Section 4 is the technical core. **4.1** shows how a Vega-Lite spec is compiled into ASP facts/predicates: `mark/1` for mark type, `encoding/1` plus `channel/2`, `field/2`, `aggregate/2`, `bin/1`, `stack/1`, `zero/1`, `log/1` for encoding properties, `num_rows/1`, `fieldtype/2`, `cardinality/2`, `entropy/2`, `extent/3` for the data schema, and a single `task/1` predicate (value vs. summary) plus `interesting/2` to mark task-relevant fields. **4.2** defines a Draco "visualization model" as two things: a *design space* (which specs are valid at all) and an *evaluation function* over that space (which valid specs are preferred). The design space is carved out with aggregate rules (declaring each attribute's domain) plus two flavors of hard integrity constraints — **well-formedness constraints** (e.g., only point marks may use the shape channel; a log scale can't include zero in its domain; Vega-Lite only supports 8 distinct shape values) and **expressiveness constraints** in Mackinlay's sense (e.g., a bar chart's continuous axis must include zero as a baseline; the size channel can't be used to encode nominal data, since size implies an ordering the data doesn't have). Preferences over the remaining valid space are then expressed purely as soft constraints with weights — e.g. `:~ continuous(E), not zero(E). [5]` penalizes (but doesn't forbid) a continuous field's encoding omitting a zero baseline. The cost of a full specification is the weighted sum of all its constraint violations, `Cost(v) = Σ wᵢ·n_{pᵢ}(v)`, which the paper reframes as a dot product `Cost(v) = xᵀw` between a fixed weight vector and a per-visualization **feature vector** `x` of violation counts — the representation that later makes weight-learning tractable. **4.3** describes how partial specifications ("queries") are answered: a query (which can also come from a converted CompassQL query or partial Vega-Lite spec) is compiled into additional facts/constraints restricting the design space to completions matching the user's intent, Clingo is invoked to find the lowest-cost completion(s) within that subspace, and the result is translated back into a Vega-Lite spec. The paper is explicit that "optimal" here is relative to the formally-defined preference model, not a claim of objectively correct design — the system can't fully know user intent, and any visualization is necessarily an abstraction that emphasizes some things over others.

Section 5 addresses the weakness of hand-tuned weights: tuning soft-constraint weights by hand doesn't scale as the constraint set grows, because it requires an expert to reason globally about many competing preferences at once. Draco instead learns weights from **ranked pairs** of complete visualizations (rather than from complete-specification "ground truth," which doesn't really exist for visualization design) using a **RankSVM** formulation: given a labeled pair (v1, v2, y) indicating which is preferred, train a linear weight vector w such that `sign(Cost(v1) − Cost(v2))` matches the label, by minimizing a hinge loss with L2 regularization (`λ = 0.1` in their experiments) over the *difference* of the two visualizations' feature vectors. Because the classification problem is symmetric about the origin, pair order doesn't need special handling for class imbalance.

Section 6 is the evaluation, structured as three demonstrations of increasing scope:

- **6.1 Draco-APT** reimplements APT's importance-ordering channel preferences as soft constraints of the form `:~ type(E,T), channel(E,C), priority(E,P). [w@P,E]`, where cost is incurred at a priority level tied to the field's user-specified importance, so hard constraints are satisfied first, then soft constraints are resolved in priority order. A worked four-field query (two quantitative, one nominal, one unlabeled field with descending priority) reproduces APT-style optimal completions (quantitative fields to x/y, nominal to color, the fourth field to size).

- **6.2 Draco-CQL** reimplements CompassQL's semantics as hand-tuned soft constraints (channel/mark/aggregation-function rankings, preferences for compact layouts, horizontal-axis-first conventions, etc.), reproducing CompassQL's exact optimal specification on **all 17 partial specifications in CompassQL's own test suite**. The paper reports a large implementation-complexity reduction: Draco-CQL is **70 hard + 110 soft constraints**, versus CompassQL's **4,324 lines of imperative JavaScript** (1,134 for hard constraints, 786 for scoring logic) — concrete examples given include a channel-reuse restriction that takes 14 lines of JS in CompassQL vs. one line in Draco (`:- single_channel(C), 2 { channel(_,C) }.`), and a continuous-field-grouping penalty that takes 12 lines in CompassQL vs. `:~ aggregate(_,_), continuous(E), not aggregate(E,_). [3]` in Draco. Draco-CQL also benchmarks faster and more scalable (Figure 7): on identical hardware (CentOS 7, Xeon E5-2690 v3 @ 2.60GHz; CompassQL on Node v9.9.0), Draco returns an answer in under half a second for a 25-field dataset with a 5-encoding query, while CompassQL's exhaustive generate-and-test approach runs out of heap memory (capped at 4GB) within a few minutes on the same problem.

- **6.3 Draco-Learn** harvests ranked visualization pairs from two published graphical-perception studies with very different designs — **Kim et al.**, a large scatterplot study (1 categorical + 2 quantitative fields, x/y/color/size/row channels; ~185,000 responses from 1,920 participants), and **Saket et al.**, a smaller cross-chart-type study (180 participants, one quantitative y-mean-aggregate field against a nominal/ordinal/quantitative x-field, comparing bar/line/scatter). Pairs are constructed by grouping responses by data schema and task, comparing every visualization pair within a group, and keeping only pairs where accuracy or timing differs significantly by Fisher's exact test (p < 0.01) — yielding about **1,100 ranked pairs from Kim et al.** and only **10 from Saket et al.** (because Saket et al. only compares three chart types per condition and few show significant differences). Feature vectors are computed by running Clingo on Draco-CQL's constraint set augmented with a handful of new task-channel/task-marktype interaction rules (added "in a few hours"), and weights are trained with scikit-learn's SVM. Directly measured on held-out ranked pairs (55% train / 15% validation / 30% test split), the **learned model reaches 93% test accuracy**, versus **65%** for Draco-CQL's hand-tuned weights (barely better than chance) — and the model trained on the combined datasets from both studies achieves this without degrading performance on either study individually, which the authors read as evidence the model generalizes rather than overfitting to one source. Accuracy only degrades noticeably below ~250 training pairs. For the more practically relevant question — synthesizing the actual best encoding for a query, not just ranking arbitrary pairs correctly — Draco-Learn (restricted to the design space actually covered by the two studies, via ~10 additional constraints each) is shown qualitatively against Draco-CQL (Figure 8): for a value task, both put the quantitative variable on y and the nominal category on color (a scatterplot, which minimizes overplotting for reading individual values); but for a summary task, Draco-CQL still returns the identical scatterplot (because its weights carry no task-dependent terms), while Draco-Learn instead spatially groups by category — better suited to perceiving distributional properties like min/max/average.

Section 7 discusses Draco as a software-engineering contribution first (decoupling declarative knowledge from search-algorithm implementation lets researchers focus on design-space/preference definition rather than reimplementing search, and lets a knowledge base be distributed and extended incrementally as independent constraint/weight updates) before sketching four "beyond automated design" extensions the authors have not built: (1) a visualization "spell checker" that flags or auto-corrects design-guideline violations, potentially using unsatisfiable-core techniques to find a minimal fix; (2) using Draco's design-space definition to auto-generate a full corpus of valid visualizations (just run Clingo without preferences to enumerate all answer sets) for future human-subjects studies; (3) using Draco as a tool to formally compare whether a newly proposed design guideline conflicts with or is subsumed by existing ones; (4) tooling to browse the design space/knowledge base and a future "re-ranking" strategy where low-level features impractical to encode in ASP (e.g., actual overplotting) re-rank a shortlist of Draco's top ASP-level candidates via a separately learned sub-symbolic classifier — plus a forward pointer to inductive logic programming (citing Law et al.'s ILASP) as a way to eventually learn constraint *rules*, not just weights, from noisy data. Named future extensions to the model itself include filtering/sorting transformations, Vega-Lite selections/interaction primitives, and multi-view/dashboard composition (with a specific example: consistent color encoding across multiple linked charts, which single-view preference reasoning alone can't guarantee is globally consistent).

An appendix formally reinterprets Draco's linear soft-constraint cost model as a **Markov logic network (MLN)**: the joint distribution over visualizations is a softmax over negative cost, `P(v) = e^{-Cost(v)} / Σ_u e^{-Cost(u)}`, so finding an optimal completion is equivalent to MAP inference in this probability model — solvable by the ASP solver directly minimizing cost without ever computing the (expensive) partition function. The MLN framing is offered as motivation for why a linear feature-count model can be more compact and interpretable than modeling correlations between every pair of attributes explicitly.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The central reusable move: separate "what makes a visualization valid" (hard constraints — syntax/grammar/expressiveness) from "what makes a valid visualization *preferred*" (soft constraints with learnable weights) — this expressiveness/effectiveness split is Mackinlay's, but Draco's contribution is making the *effectiveness* half data-driven and incrementally extensible rather than a fixed, hand-authored ranking.
- Constraint-based/declarative representation as a software-engineering strategy, not just an AI technique: the concrete payoff demonstrated is a large drop in implementation size (Draco-CQL's ~180 rules vs. CompassQL's 4,324 lines) and an escape from having to hand-roll a search algorithm — the actual search is delegated to a general-purpose, off-the-shelf solver (Clingo).
- "Learning to rank from pairwise comparisons" is a reusable pattern whenever ground-truth optimal completions don't exist (which is most of visualization design) — you can harvest such pairs from any study that reports accuracy/timing differences between conditions, even studies that weren't designed with this reuse in mind, as long as you can extract a significance-tested preference.
- The Kim-et-al.-vs.-Saket-et-al. harvesting example is a concrete illustration of how heterogeneous, differently-scoped perceptual studies can nonetheless be combined into one preference model, provided the feature representation (soft-constraint violation counts) is shared across them.
- The paper's own reflective framing of "optimal" — a specification is optimal only relative to a formally stated (and necessarily incomplete/task-blind) preference model, never a claim of objectively correct design — is a useful corrective against overclaiming for any automated-design tool, including Draco itself.
- The four "beyond design recommendation" extensions (spell-checker, corpus generation, cross-study comparison, re-ranking) are good discussion prompts even though none are built in the paper — they show how a formal representation opens up uses beyond the original "recommend me a chart" framing.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Section 3 (the ASP primer) is worth reading once carefully if you're not already familiar with answer set programming — it's short and everything downstream (the constraint syntax, `:~ ... [w]`, aggregate rules) depends on it. Section 4 is the paper's technical heart and rewards close reading, especially 4.2 (constraint types and the cost formula) and the worked query example in 4.3 — the concrete ASP snippets are short enough to actually parse and are more illuminating than the prose describing them. Section 5 (RankSVM formulation) can be skimmed if you're comfortable taking "pairwise ranking with a linear cost model" on faith; the loss-function derivation adds little beyond that summary. Section 6 is worth reading in full — it's where the paper's actual evidence lives (the 17/17 CompassQL match, the 4,324-vs-~180 line comparison, the 93%-vs-65% accuracy numbers, Figure 8's task-dependent example) and skimming it risks missing exactly the numbers a careful reader would want to quote. Section 7 (discussion/future work) and the appendix (MLN reinterpretation) can be skimmed — they're speculative and reflective rather than reporting new results, though the appendix is a nice two-minute read if you want the probabilistic framing of "cost" made explicit.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Draco-CQL reproduces CompassQL's optimal specification on **17/17** test-suite queries while being implemented in **70 hard + 110 soft constraints**, versus CompassQL's **4,324 lines of imperative JavaScript** (1,134 hard-constraint lines + 786 scoring lines).
- Draco-CQL scales where CompassQL doesn't: on a 25-field dataset with a 5-encoding query, Draco answers in under half a second while CompassQL exhausts a 4GB heap within a few minutes (Figure 7 benchmark, CentOS 7 / Xeon E5-2690 v3 @ 2.60GHz).
- Draco-Learn: trained on ranked pairs harvested from Kim et al. (~1,100 pairs, from ~185,000 responses across 1,920 participants) and Saket et al. (10 pairs, from 180 participants), the learned weight model hits **93% test accuracy** vs. **65%** for hand-tuned Draco-CQL weights (barely above chance) on the pairwise-ranking task, and **96%** on the validation split; training accuracy on Saket et al. alone reaches "perfect" even when Kim-et-al. data is mixed in without hurting either.
- Concrete code-size wins cited: a channel-reuse-restriction rule takes 14 lines of JS in CompassQL vs. one ASP line (`:- single_channel(C), 2 { channel(_,C) }.`) in Draco; a continuous-field aggregation penalty takes 12 lines in CompassQL vs. `:~ aggregate(_,_), continuous(E), not aggregate(E,_). [3]` in Draco.
- Named prior/adjacent systems: Mackinlay's **APT**, the **SAGE** project, Casner's **BOZ**, **ShowMe**, **CompassQL** (Voyager/Voyager2's recommendation engine), **VizDeck** (a prior linear-weight-learning system the paper distinguishes itself from, since VizDeck's features only capture direct chart-type/data-statistic correlations and its learning is limited to a small predefined visualization set).
- The paper's task taxonomy, borrowed from Kim et al.: **value tasks** (reading/comparing individual data values) vs. **summary tasks** (comparing ensembles/aggregates) — deliberately much simpler than richer activity-based task taxonomies, flagged explicitly as future-work territory.
- The RankSVM regularization parameter is set to λ = 0.1 "for our initial experiments" — stated as a specific but not deeply justified choice.
- Draco is implemented in **Answer Set Programming**, solved with **Clingo**; the design space in the paper is deliberately restricted to single-view and faceted (row/column) Vega-Lite specs — no layering, no arbitrary view composition, no filtering/sorting transforms, no interaction primitives (all named explicitly as future extensions).
- Open source release: https://uwdata.github.io/draco/.

**Caveats:** The paper is explicit about several scope limits rather than smoothing over them. The design space modeled is a genuine subset of Vega-Lite (single/faceted views only; transformations limited to binning and aggregation — no filtering or sorting; no interaction primitives such as Vega-Lite selections) — the authors list these as concrete near-term extensions rather than claiming full grammar coverage. The Draco-Learn demonstration is explicitly scoped down: because Kim et al.'s and Saket et al.'s studies each cover only a narrow slice of the full design space (a handful of channel/mark combinations), Draco-Learn is evaluated only within a *restricted* design space matching what the two studies actually tested, and the paper says directly that the harvested data alone isn't enough to learn weights for a system that could compete with the *unrestricted* Draco-CQL. The Saket et al. harvest yields only 10 ranked pairs (vs. ~1,100 from Kim et al.), a stark imbalance the paper reports plainly rather than hiding, and it's worth noting as a reader that a 10-pair sample is small enough that any claims resting specifically on the Saket-et-al. slice of Draco-Learn should be read cautiously even though the paper's headline accuracy numbers are dominated by the much larger Kim-et-al. set. The 93%/65% accuracy comparison is a *pairwise-ranking* accuracy metric, not a claim that Draco-Learn always recommends the single best chart for every query — the paper itself pivots to a separate, qualitative argument (Figure 8) for why getting the *top* recommendation right matters more in practice than ranking accuracy across all pairs, which is a fair point but also means the two evaluations (quantitative ranking accuracy vs. qualitative top-1 example) aren't the same claim and shouldn't be conflated. As with Mackinlay's effectiveness rankings, several specific weight choices (e.g., λ = 0.1, the p < 0.01 significance threshold for harvesting pairs) are stated as choices made "for our initial experiments" without extensive sensitivity analysis in the main text — the paper does report that accuracy improves as the harvesting p-value threshold is relaxed toward 0.1, but doesn't explore this parameter space exhaustively. Finally, this is a systems/evaluation paper about *representing and combining* design knowledge, not itself a new empirical study of human graphical perception — all the actual effectiveness evidence it draws on (Cleveland & McGill, Kim et al., Saket et al.) is imported from prior work, and Draco's contribution is the formal representation, weight-learning machinery, and software-engineering argument around that imported knowledge, not new perceptual findings of its own.
