# Ondov, Jardine, Elmqvist & Franconeri, "Face to Face: Evaluating Visual Comparison" (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, vol. 25, no. 1, January 2019, p. 861 ff.; presented at IEEE InfoVis 2018)

**What it is:** An empirical graphical-perception paper: a set of crowdsourced experiments (plus a small expert-interview case study) measuring how well different layouts support two primitive comparison tasks between two data series. Its claim is that the layout used to compare two datasets (overlaid, stacked small multiples, adjacent small multiples, mirror-symmetric small multiples, or an animated transition) measurably changes how subtle a difference viewers can detect. Some results confirm folk wisdom (overlaid beats small multiples). Others do not (animation does very well at finding the "biggest mover," and mirroring helps). It sits in the literature on visual comparison (the authors build on Gleicher et al.'s task taxonomies and design reviews, which are largely intuition-based) and in the Cleveland & McGill tradition of crowdsourced graphical perception, but borrows its motivation and its staircase method from perceptual psychology. The authors say a primary contribution is the method itself. The PDF is the author-circulated version: the first page carries an "Accepted to IEEE InfoVis 2018." banner, and the page headers and footers show the journal's volume/issue/date and IEEE Xplore download stamps (a University of Wisconsin licensed-use line), so it is the typeset TVCG layout with that banner added.

**AI summary:** The introduction argues that perceptual studies usually test tasks on a single data series (averages, trends, extremes, outliers), whereas comparing two datasets changes the goal: finding the largest delta from one set to another, or judging overall correlation, and comparison "can tax a substantially capacity-limited aspect of our visual system." The authors pick two primitive tasks. Task 1 is identification of a maximum delta ("biggest mover"), embedded in bar charts (length), simple line graphs reduced to slopes (slope), and donut charts (angle). Task 2 is estimation of overall correlation, embedded as a forced choice between two pairs of bar charts. For each embedding they test five layouts: stacked small multiples with a common baseline, adjacent small multiples with a non-common baseline, overlaid (superposition), adjacent small multiples that are mirror symmetric, and animated transitions. The first three are common and have intuitive but rarely measured efficacy differences. The last two are less common but may exploit the visual system's sensitivity to motion (especially common motion) and to mirror symmetry. A footnote notes that stacked and adjacent are only partly tested for donuts, since donuts have no inherent orientation. The contributions listed are two graphical-perception experiments (max delta in bar, slope and donut charts; correlation in bar charts), data-generation procedures designed for comparison perception studies, and a practical application of animation and mirroring to Krona, a sunburst browser for biological data.

Section 2 reviews background. It notes that most graphical perception work involves relationships within one series, and that comparative visualization can be seen as multivariate analysis where a categorical variable slices the data (the paper's own example: baby-name popularity or prices across countries). It cites Gleicher et al.'s taxonomies and reviews as valuable but largely intuition-driven, and cites the few quantitative studies: Qu et al. on consistent scales and colors, Robertson et al. on animation versus many small multiples (8 to 80) for trends in Gapminder data, Heer et al. on time-series variants within vertical juxtaposition, and Javed et al. on juxtaposition versus superposition with single-view-style tasks. Section 3 sets out three perceptual themes that motivated the arrangements. Co-location: comparing across two regions is harder than detecting a difference within one region because it may require storing one region while inspecting the other ("spot the difference" games illustrate this), and visual working memory holds about four items at most. Symmetry: the visual system is especially sensitive to mirror symmetry, and detects differences more efficiently between mirror-image regions than translated copies, especially when the symmetric information is spatially close. Juxtaposed datasets are normally translated horizontally with common axial directions to reduce the burden of interpreting different polarities, but mirror symmetry is occasionally used for similar series, as in population pyramids. Movement: motion is a primitive visual feature, and since speed co-varies with change size and direction with change direction, it may be useful for detecting value changes. But performance can fall drastically beyond 2-4 simultaneously moving objects, and evidence for animation in visualization is described as "early and mixed," with earlier studies assigning it questionable value for correlation by oscillation, trends in time series, and linking views in a scatterplot.

Section 4 gives the methods. The authors deliberately avoid response time, arguing it is contaminated by individual differences in conservatism, and use accuracy instead. The two tasks are explicitly not meant to represent the real-world suite of comparison tasks. In MAX DELTA, participants identify which data point changed most (increase or decrease, by absolute change, not percent change). Difficulty is raised by shrinking the biggest delta while enlarging distractor deltas, and a bimodal distribution of absolute values decouples the largest delta from the largest or smallest value in either set. In CORRELATION, participants choose which of two pairs of charts has the most correlated series. The target pair's correlation is varied while the control pair stays at a low fixed correlation (0.2). Because correlation may be too esoteric for crowd workers, instructions say to choose the "most similar pair," and each chart in a pair has comparable means and standard deviations (following Rensink & Baldridge and Harrison et al.).

The staircase ("titer") method is central. A titer is a value between 0 and 1 that scales the difference between stimuli. For MAX DELTA, a larger titer makes the biggest mover move more and the distractors move less; for CORRELATION, a larger titer pushes the test pair's correlation further above the 0.2 baseline until the two series of the test pair are nearly identical. Each arrangement starts at a titer of 0.5. After an incorrect response the next trial gets easier (larger titer), and after a correct response harder (smaller titer), with the increase three times as large as the decrease. The 3:1 modulation is intended to converge on the stimulus magnitude that yields 75% accuracy for that layout (Figure 3 illustrates a titration run). A larger final titer therefore means the layout made the task harder. Analysis uses within-subjects comparisons of each participant's mean titer over the final 5 trials per arrangement. The staircase is justified as the standard perceptual-psychology approach, and because the authors had no a priori hypothesis about which stimulus properties would span low to high accuracy across arrangements.

Chart types, with parameters tuned in internal piloting: bar charts (both tasks) with 7 data points per series; slope charts (MAX DELTA only), simplified two-point lines (0 and a generated datum) with 3 data points per series; and donut charts (MAX DELTA only) with 4 data points per series and several deviations from standard donuts for control (gray distractor buffers so adjacent data can change size without moving, concentric rings for the overlaid arrangement, and a 180-degree limit per chart for the mirrored arrangement so the two series form a complete circle). The arrangements (Figure 2) are as follows.

- Stacked: vertical small multiples, included as an expected floor, with Cleveland & McGill's aligned-baseline argument noted but offset by harder correspondence between paired values.
- Adjacent: side-by-side small multiples, a more realistic baseline.
- Mirrored: adjacent with the x-axis direction opposed in each chart (for bars, right-aligning the left chart and vice versa; for slope charts, reversing the x-axis of the left chart so the slope is negated).
- Overlaid: both series in the same space, which the authors expect to be a ceiling for MAX DELTA, with saturation and luminance slightly reduced in one dataset to distinguish adjacent elements.
- Animated: one chart morphs from one series to the other over the same time for all marks, so each mark's peak velocity encodes its delta. They used cubic easing, with maximum velocity at the midpoint.

Procedure: a fixation dot and chart outlines appear, the participant clicks to start, and after a countdown the stimulus shows for a short fixed time. For MAX DELTA this is 1.5 seconds for both static charts and animation. For CORRELATION, static charts show for 3 seconds (to account for the doubled number of charts) while animation stays at 1.5 seconds to preserve velocity. At the end one of the datasets is removed (so the participant must respond from memory of the other half) and the participant clicks the response: "Click on the bar that had the biggest difference in the two charts" for MAX DELTA, "Click on the color that had the most from similarity before to after" for CORRELATION (the quote is transcribed as in the PDF text). Feedback on correctness is given. There were twenty trials per arrangement (thirty for donut charts), and arrangement blocks were ordered differently across participants. Training included untimed practice that had to be answered correctly (once for MAX DELTA, 3 times for CORRELATION) before each block, plus 3 timed training trials at the easiest titer for the first non-animated arrangement, with data regenerated after wrong training answers to prevent answering by elimination. Participants came from Amazon Mechanical Turk. Based on pilot power analyses, 50 new participants were recruited per experiment, with self-exclusion for color vision deficiency. Each did a single combination of stimulus type and task (4 arrangements for donut, 5 for the others), and worker IDs enforced uniqueness. All 200 workers were adults in the United States: 61.3% male, 36.4% female, 1.10% other, 0.6% no response; ages 18-25 (7.7%), 26-40 (60.8%), 41-60 (28.7%), 61-80 (2.2%).

Section 5 reports results. The authors preview that results "largely dovetail" with prior recommendations (overlaid > adjacent > stacked), with three surprises: animation significantly improved detection of the most different data point in bar and donut charts (not slope charts); animation brought no benefit for the correlation task; and horizontally mirrored bar charts did better in both tasks. Participants whose overall proportion correct was more than two standard deviations below the mean of other workers were excluded because the staircase had evidently failed for them; this removed 3, 4, 2, and 2 participants in Experiments 1A, 1B, 1C and 2.

Experiment 1A (bar charts, MAX DELTA): arrangement affected final titers, F(2.98, 137.23) = 103.23, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.69 (Greenhouse-Geisser corrected). Animated bars were significantly more precise than overlaid, t(46) = 3.42, p = .001. Mirrored small multiples beat non-mirrored horizontal, t(46) = 2.73, p = .009, and vertical, t(46) = 4.82, p < .001. The authors then report a floor effect for this experiment. In the final five trials, accuracy was low for stacked (57%), adjacent (61%) and mirrored (64%), with large titers near the maximum of 0.75 (0.68, 0.64, 0.59 respectively), versus 74.6% accuracy and a mean titer of 0.35 for animated. The maximum titer was reached on 28% of stacked trials, 15% of adjacent, 6% of mirrored, 5% of overlaid and 0% of animated trials. Participants stuck at the capped titer kept erring with no further adjustment, so their final titer reflects the cap rather than ability. The authors conclude Experiment 1A cannot quantify the true floor for these layouts, nor appropriately compare stacked with adjacent, though MAX DELTA was clearly highly difficult in both. They suggest longer display times (which could cause ceiling effects for good layouts) or secondary tests of close arrangements, and report no floor effects in later experiments. The titer cap of 0.75 is explained in the appendix: difficulty rose above 0.75 due to alignment of bars.

Experiment 1B (slope charts, MAX DELTA): titers were generally more precise, and overlaid slopes outperformed all other arrangements including animated, t(45) = 10.13, p < .001. Small-multiple variants did not differ: horizontal mirrored versus horizontal non-mirrored t(45) = .25, p = .8; versus vertical t(45) = .77, p = .45. Arrangement effect: F(4, 180) = 101.87, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.69; no floor effects. Experiment 1C (donuts, MAX DELTA): arrangement effect F(3, 141) = 22.96, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.33; animated donuts outperformed all other arrangements; no evidence that the split-hemifield mirrored layout beat horizontal small multiples, t(47) = 1.26, p = .21.

Experiment 2 (bar charts, CORRELATION): arrangement effect F(3.22, 144.95) = 6.50, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.13, with no floor effects. There was no benefit of animation, and mirrored bar charts "outperformed all other arrangements for detection of correlated data" (the Discussion's summary; the planned comparisons reported are mirrored vs adjacent and adjacent vs stacked). Mean staircased correlation was 0.74 overall (Pearson R, versus a 0.20 base pair). Mirrored beat adjacent, t(45) = 2.13, p = .04: performance was reached at a target correlation of 0.70 for mirrored versus 0.75 for adjacent. Adjacent beat stacked, t(45) = 3.31, p = .002, with a correct-pair correlation of 0.82 for stacked. Accuracy patterns were similar except mirrored versus adjacent was only marginal, t(45) = 1.95, p = .057.

Section 6 (Discussion) summarizes: in MAX DELTA, animated charts consistently beat small-multiple arrangements; overlaid findings were mixed (best of all for slope charts, better than any multiple-bar arrangement, but without strong benefits over small multiples for the other cases as described); mirrored small multiples helped marginally in bar charts only for MAX DELTA. The authors argue animation may be a special case for MAX DELTA because velocity directly encodes the delta but does not directly encode what observers use for correlation or mean. Limitations: only two series at a time (animation has an upper bound of a few simultaneous objects, which may explain poor correlation performance with two simultaneous animations; mirroring implies two datasets and may not scale, though quadrant-style arrangements are suggested; juxtaposed and overlaid might extend to more sets, with screen distance becoming a factor); the two tasks may not represent real high-level comparison tasks, and low-level difficulty may not translate to sensemaking; only three chart types; highly controlled generated data (especially favorable to animation in MAX DELTA, which lacks distractors in position). Implications: results "do not suggest an easy set of guidelines that specify a best arrangement or encoding for a given task," and the authors suspect simple rules are unlikely, because comparison is mediated by whichever visual features the eye extracts. They argue guidelines will need empirical evaluation across tasks, chart types and arrangements, ideally combined with models of the mediating visual features (motion signals for animation, serial inspection for stacked). They take animation to be a valuable addition to the designer's toolbox when emphasizing differences matters, but note it is ephemeral and needs constant interaction and attention. For static two-dataset small multiples, results support center-aligned horizontal comparison, and the paper claims to provide "for the first time" an experimental validation of that practice (plain center-aligned mirrored bars, as in population pyramids), consistent with a symmetry-perception mechanism.

Section 7 is the Krona case study, offered because tightly controlled studies can lack ecological validity. The domain is the human microbiome, where even single datasets are complex hierarchies (Sunburst as in Krona, Treemaps as in MetaTreeMap, Sankey/flow as in Pavian), and adding variables such as change between datasets is difficult. The authors adapted Krona (which already supported animated transitions) to add adjacent and mirrored layouts (Figure 7: a single circle split in two for mirror symmetry, versus a standard small-multiple pair). Overlaid was not implemented since it is unclear how to overlay nested sunbursts. Two scientists studying the microbiome at the National Human Genome Research Institute, both experienced with Krona for single datasets, tried all three arrangements on real human skin microbiome data from two time points ("M3 skin" days 0 and 1, from Caporaso et al.), asked to find significant differences in fractions of particular organisms, especially pathogens; the example in Figure 7(a) is that Gammaproteobacteria decrease a lot from day 0 to day 1, but within that group Pseudomonas increases. The study was qualitative, not seeking specific correct answers. Findings: both experts found animation made differences particularly salient, but a large change shifted other wedges "in a disorienting way" (because wedge position could not be controlled with distractors as in the experiments), and the authors suggest optimizing wedge ordering to limit offsetting, as has been done for stable treemaps. Animation could be engaging for an audience highlighting a specific difference, but both preferred static views for their own exploration. One preferred small multiples (consistency with standard sunbursts, more than two samples); the other preferred the mirrored view (better use of space, less eye travel). Experts noted small multiples may be best for dissemination to an audience unfamiliar with the split view. The takeaway offered: no ideal arrangement, and platforms should support many layouts and let users switch.

The conclusion states the results "merely scratch the surface," lists open factors (displacement of compared values, mark shape, axial distance, viewer comprehension of the mirror metaphor, combining factors such as animation within mirrored displays), suggests next tasks closer to analyst work (highest overall mean, most consistent change), and notes from Experiment 1A that where animation is infeasible (handouts), the best static alternative is overlaid, at least for max delta. Appendix A describes data generation. For MAX DELTA, the titer sets the biggest mover's change to t times the chart's range, the largest distractor changes by (1 - t) of that, the next by (1 - t) of the previous, and so on (at titer 0.75: 3/4 of range, then 3/16, then 3/64). At the minimum titer every point changes a small equal amount (chance performance by design, never reached in practice); at the maximum titer there are only two possible values, used by the biggest mover while others stay at one and never change. Algorithm 1 gives the procedure, outputs are linearly transformed (for example, minimum bar width), and the authors checked the ordinal ranking of answers among distractors, acknowledging "areas of bias" they judged too unlikely to be exploited. The number of trials (20 for MAX DELTA, 30 for CORRELATION) was set by piloting how titer standard deviations plateau in 5-trial bins. For CORRELATION, pairs were generated with simulated annealing inspired by Matejka & Fitzmaurice, with means and standard deviations fixed within 10 percent of the range, and correlation transformed by the optimal perceptual formula from Rensink & Baldridge so the reported titers correspond to g(r) in their Equation 7. Appendix B covers rendering: D3 in the browser, 256-pixel square charts for MAX DELTA, 200-pixel squares (four charts) and 141-pixel squares (two charts) for CORRELATION for equivalent total area, Tableau 10 subsets (7 for bars, 3 for slopes, 4 for donuts), white backgrounds with faint gray chart boundaries, and automatic full-screen mode (not enforced). Appendix C gives titer histograms for Experiment 1A showing participants disproportionately reaching the maximum titer for stacked and adjacent.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- For comparing two datasets, the paper's measurements back the common ordering (overlaid generally better than small multiples; adjacent better than stacked where that could be measured, which was only Experiment 2), but with exceptions that depend on the chart type and task. It is not a universal ranking.
- A cheap, concrete design idea with evidence: mirroring the two small multiples (center-aligned, as in a population pyramid) improved performance for bar charts in both tasks, though not for slope charts or donuts, and the correlation advantage over adjacent was modest (p = .04).
- Animation is surprisingly good for finding the single biggest change (bar and donut charts) because speed directly encodes delta, and it does not help (here) for judging overall correlation. The mechanism matters: a layout helps a task when the visual feature it produces is the one the task needs.
- The authors themselves say there is no easy guideline of the form "best layout for task X." A reader should treat this as evidence about two low-level tasks in controlled conditions, and as a demonstration of a method, not as a rule book.
- The staircase/titer method is a useful way to evaluate designs: instead of fixing difficulty, adapt the stimulus to a 75%-accuracy threshold and compare thresholds across designs. It also teaches the pitfalls: capped difficulty creates floor effects (Experiment 1A), and generated data must keep the task from being solvable by shortcut (hence the bimodal data generation).
- Ecological validity check: the expert case study shows animation's benefits shrink when marks cannot be placed to avoid disorienting shifts, and that practitioners value flexibility (switching layouts) plus static layouts for dissemination.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The paper is short (the body is about 8 pages plus appendices and references) and rewards a full read. Read Section 3 (the three perceptual themes) for the reasoning behind why mirroring and animation were tried, Section 4 for the staircase design (4.2 especially), and Section 5.4 (the floor effect) for the honest limitation. Figure 1 (the three-panel overview), Figure 2 (the five arrangements) and Figures 5 and 6 (mean final-five titers with 95% confidence intervals for MAX DELTA and CORRELATION) are the figures to study. Figure 3 explains how the titer moves, and Figure 7 shows the Krona mirrored and adjacent views. The statistics (F and t values) can be skimmed once the pattern is clear. Section 2 is background that can be skimmed. Appendix A (data generation) matters only for someone replicating the study or wanting to understand why the stimuli look the way they do. Appendix B is rendering detail; Appendix C is one histogram figure supporting the floor-effect claim. The reference list (70 numbered items, spilling onto a second reference page) is mostly perceptual psychology plus the comparison and graphical-perception literature the paper builds on; skip unless chasing a source.

**Memorable specifics:**

- Abstract wording: participants completed tasks "across a variety of layouts: overlaid, two arrangements of juxtaposed small multiples, mirror-symmetric small multiples, and animated transitions." Results "align with previous work in perceptual psychology."
- Five arrangements: stacked, adjacent, mirrored, overlaid, animated. Three chart types for MAX DELTA: bar (7 data points per series), slope (3), donut (4). Correlation was tested only with bar charts.
- Staircase details: start titer 0.5; titer goes up three times as much after an error as it goes down after a correct answer; intended to converge on 75% accuracy; analysis uses the mean of the final 5 trials; titer capped at 0.75; the Procedure section says twenty trials per arrangement (thirty for donut charts), while Appendix A says piloting suggested 20 trials for MAX DELTA and 30 for CORRELATION (the PDF does not reconcile the two statements explicitly).
- Display times: 1.5 s for MAX DELTA (static and animated); 3 s for static CORRELATION; 1.5 s for animated CORRELATION.
- Participants: 200 Mechanical Turk workers in the US, 50 per experiment; exclusions of 3, 4, 2 and 2 in Experiments 1A, 1B, 1C and 2.
- Experiment 1A floor effect: final-five accuracy 57% (stacked), 61% (adjacent), 64% (mirrored), versus 74.6% for animated (mean titer 0.35); max titer reached on 28% of stacked trials and 0% of animated trials.
- Experiment 1A tests: ANOVA F(2.98, 137.23) = 103.23; animated vs overlaid t(46) = 3.42, p = .001; mirrored vs horizontal t(46) = 2.73, p = .009; mirrored vs vertical t(46) = 4.82, p < .001.
- Experiment 1B: overlaid beat animated, t(45) = 10.13, p < .001; no mirroring benefit. Experiment 1C: animated best; mirrored not better than horizontal, t(47) = 1.26, p = .21.
- Experiment 2: mean staircased correlation 0.74 against a base of 0.20; mirrored needed target R of 0.70, adjacent 0.75, stacked 0.82; mirrored vs adjacent t(45) = 2.13, p = .04 (accuracy only marginal, p = .057).
- Quotable lines: "The present results do not suggest an easy set of guidelines that specify a best arrangement or encoding for a given task." And: "We therefore consider a primary contribution of this paper to be a demonstration of a method for empirically evaluating a given arrangement design, for a given task, in a given visualization."
- Data-generation example: at titer 0.75 the biggest mover covers 3/4 of the chart range, the first distractor 3/16, the next 3/64.
- Krona example: Gammaproteobacteria (red wedges) decrease a large amount from day 0 to day 1 while Pseudomonas within that group increases; the experts' comment that large animated changes shift other wedges "in a disorienting way."
- Rendering: charts 256 px square for MAX DELTA; 200 px (four charts) and 141 px (two charts) for CORRELATION; D3 and Tableau 10 color subsets.

**Caveats:** The PDF's text extraction garbled the data in Figures 3, 5 and 6 (and the histogram in Figure 8), so the per-arrangement titer values for each experiment are known here only from the numbers the prose states (for example, the Experiment 1A titers and accuracies, and the correlations of 0.70, 0.75, 0.82 and 0.74), not from the plots; I did not view the figure images themselves. I did not independently check the F and t values. The paper's own limits are the ones listed in its Section 6.1: two series only, two low-level tasks, three chart types, highly controlled generated data (especially favorable to animation in MAX DELTA), and an Experiment 1A floor effect that prevents comparing stacked with adjacent. The case study is qualitative, with only two expert participants, and the authors state it does not identify an ideal arrangement. The PDF text has a few internal oddities worth rechecking against the figures before quoting: the Discussion's sentence on overlaid findings is awkwardly worded, and the statement that mirrored bars beat "all other arrangements" in Experiment 2 is stronger than the two planned comparisons reported. The reference list is dated to 2018; later work on these tasks is not covered.
