# Brehmer & Munzner, "A Multi-Level Typology of Abstract Visualization Tasks" (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics / InfoVis, 2013)

**What it is:** A typology paper that resolves a specific confusion in prior visualization task classification — terms like "derive" or "filter" being used ambiguously as either the *end* or the *means* of a task — by treating why, how, and what as three separate, independently-specifiable questions (Figure 1), and demonstrating the approach on a case study and a systematic survey of 30 prior classification systems.

**AI summary:** The paper opens from a diagnosis, not a proposal: existing task classification systems in visualization, HCI, cartography, and information retrieval cannot be used as rigorous analytic tools because they conflate the ends and means of a task. The recurring symptom is *ends-means ambiguity* — the same term shows up in the literature sometimes naming why a task is done and sometimes naming how it is done. The paper's worked example is "derive": a user may derive data items as an end in itself (to reduce dimensionality), or as a means to another end (deriving a low-dimensional space in order to verify a clustering hypothesis). The same ambiguity affects filter, navigate, record, and sort. The paper's central methodological move is to stop trying to classify tasks with a single vocabulary and instead ask three separable questions of any task description: why is it performed, how is it performed, and what does it pertain to (its inputs and outputs). It is explicit that this is a *typology*, not a *taxonomy* — a typology classifies abstract concepts, a taxonomy classifies empirically observable events (citing Bailey 1994) — and the paper's three-question structure is offered as a lens for describing abstract task concepts, not as a coding scheme derived from observed behavior.

Section 2 makes the case for why existing systems fail, using two worked examples redescribed with several extant classification systems each. The first is the paper's own running example from the introduction: a person casually comparing Texas's and California's results on a choropleth election map while reading a blog post, not motivated by any explicit hypothesis or presentation need. Redescribed via Andrienko and Andrienko's taxonomy, this becomes an "elementary direct comparison task" — richer than Amar et al.'s low-level "retrieve value," but still silent on why the comparison happened or how it was carried out. Redescribed via Roth's cartographic-interaction-primitives taxonomy (goals/objectives/operators/operands), the task becomes goal *procure*, objective *compare*, operators *retrieve*/*calculate*, operand *attribute-in-space* — closer to why/how/what, but "procure" loses the user's actual casual motivation, and Roth's taxonomy's spatial constraint on operands can't capture what's being compared in a non-spatial sense. The second example is a user of two *different* tree-visualization tools, SpaceTree and TreeJuxtaposer, both used to locate and present a path between two nodes in a large tree. Described via Heer and Shneiderman's taxonomy of interactive dynamics, this becomes data/view specification (visualize, filter) plus view manipulation (navigate, select) — a how-only description with no why. Complementing it with Lee et al.'s graph-visualization task taxonomy adds a what (a "topology" task, determining connectivity and the shortest path) but still only a partial why. Neither description, crucially, distinguishes how the task differs between the two tools (TreeJuxtaposer lets the user arrange areas of the tree for visibility; SpaceTree couples selection with aggregating and filtering unselected items) — the tools' actual behavioral difference is invisible to both classification systems. This SpaceTree/TreeJuxtaposer example is the same one Munzner's textbook reuses in chapter 3, §3.7.1 (Figures 3.8–3.9).

Section 3 introduces the typology proper (Figure 1), in three independent parts. **Why** (Fig. 1a) narrows from high-level to low-level: **consume** (splitting into **discover** — generating or verifying hypotheses — **present**, and **enjoy**, for casual, non-goal-driven encounters like the election-map example) versus **produce** (whose leaves are **annotate**, **record**, and **derive** — generating new artefacts such as transformed data, graphical annotations, or recorded interaction histories). Consumption tasks narrow further through **search** — **lookup**/**locate**/**browse**/**explore**, classified by whether the search target's identity and location are known a priori (a 2×2: known-identity+known-location is lookup, known-identity+unknown-location is locate, unknown-identity+known-location is browse, and both unknown is explore) — down to **query** — **identify**/**compare**/**summarize**, distinguished by how many targets are under consideration (one, a subset, or the whole set). **How** (Fig. 1b) covers methods — families of related encoding and interaction techniques — for **encode**, for **manipulate** (**select**/**navigate**/**arrange**/**change**/**filter**/**aggregate**, methods that alter existing visualization elements), and for **introduce** (**annotate**/**import**/**derive**/**record**, methods that add new elements). The paper is explicit and deliberate that **derive**, **record**, and **annotate** appear as leaf terms in *both* the why-part (under produce) and the how-part (under introduce): this dual appearance is not an oversight but the paper's own worked demonstration of the very ambiguity it opened with — a task like "derive" can be the end in itself in one description (produce: derive) or the means toward some other end elsewhere in the same description (how: derive, in service of some other why). **What** (Fig. 1c) is deliberately minimal: rather than propose its own vocabulary of data/view objects, the paper adopts a "bring your own what" stance, with the only requirement being that a task's input and output be explicitly stated, so that sequences of interdependent tasks can be chained together (one task's output becomes the next task's input).

Section 4 works through a case study demonstrating this chaining: Overview, a visual analytics tool for exploring large text document collections. The full task is producing a set of semantic tags for document clusters. It decomposes into task 1a/1b (order-independent): **produce: derive** and **aggregate**, taking the high-dimensional document distance matrix as input and outputting a low-dimensional scatterplot layout and a cluster hierarchy respectively, via multidimensional scaling; task 2: **discover: explore**, then **identify**, encoded via **navigate** and **select** in the linked scatterplot/tree views, taking the cluster hierarchy as input and outputting the clusters of interest; and task 3: **produce: annotate**, encoded via **encode**, taking the identified clusters as input and outputting tags for them. Figure 3 diagrams this dependency chain explicitly, and the paper contrasts it with descriptions generated from Chuah and Roth's classification and from Chi and Riedl's operator framework, both of which either miss the interdependencies, mischaracterize dimension reduction/clustering as visualization-transformations rather than data-transformations, or fail to capture the final annotation step.

Section 5 is a systematic survey against prior work: 30 works that explicitly contribute a task taxonomy, typology, characterization, or framework, plus 20 other references making relevant assertions about user behavior, compared node-by-node against the typology's vocabulary in Table 1. Five general classification systems — ones spanning both low- and high-level tasks — are singled out as having most influenced the typology's own structure: **Springmeyer et al. (1992)**, a characterization of scientific data analysis (investigation vs. integration of insight) that covers both how and why without clearly separating them; **Mullins and Treu (1993)**, an over-150-element taxonomy of mediation/coordination tasks and low-level object-oriented interactions; **Pike et al. (2009)**, whose characterization of analytic discourse distinguishes high-level modes of inquiry (goals) from low-level tasks and interactions, and separates representation from interaction intents, without integrating them for task analysis; **Heer and Shneiderman (2012)**, whose interactive-dynamics taxonomy maps ten of its twelve task types onto the how part of the typology, with the remaining two (share, guide) captured by present in the why part; and **Roth (2012)**, whose cartographic-interaction-primitives taxonomy (based on Norman's Stages of Action model) is structurally the closest match to why/how/what, differing mainly in imposing a spatial constraint on operands that the new typology avoids by keeping what restricted to input/output and letting location live under the search node. Section 5.2 also traces four theoretical foundations: distributed cognition's pragmatic/epistemic action distinction (mapping respectively to why and how), Norman's Stages of Action model and its gulfs of execution and evaluation, sensemaking/information-foraging models (bridging to discover, present, and the search subtypes), and Stephenson's Play theory (motivating enjoy).

The discussion (Section 6) and conclusion (Section 7) frame the typology's contribution along three axes of power — descriptive, evaluative, and generative — and are candid that only the descriptive power has actually been validated in the paper itself, through the worked examples and case study. Evaluative and generative validation are named explicitly as future work: the authors "plan to validate the typology's evaluative power" via observational/qualitative studies (their own ongoing post-deployment study of Overview is mentioned), and "aim to validate the typology's generative power" in future design studies. The paper also flags real limitations: it does not currently address collaborative visualization use explicitly (though the authors considered the issue during development), and it does not provide the low-level specificity needed for controlled experiments on specific interaction/encoding techniques — though it may help establish external validity for such benchmark tasks. The introduction's framing that the typology is "very much informed by previous work" but also "the result of new thinking" with "many points of divergence with previous models" is echoed almost verbatim in the discussion's opening, underscoring that this is presented as a synthesis-plus-departure, not a from-scratch invention or a pure literature summary.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The core diagnostic move: whenever a task-classification term feels slippery, ask whether it's being used to answer *why* (the goal), *how* (the technique/method), or *what* (the input/output) — many apparent disagreements in the literature are really answers to different questions being compared as if they were answers to the same one.
- The typology-vs-taxonomy distinction is worth internalizing on its own: a typology organizes abstract concepts (why/how/what as categories), a taxonomy organizes empirically observed instances — useful vocabulary for describing what kind of classification a given paper (including this one) is actually offering.
- The search 2×2 (lookup/locate/browse/explore by known/unknown identity × known/unknown location) is a reusable, teachable unit on its own, independent of the rest of the typology.
- The deliberate double-appearance of derive/record/annotate in both the why-part and how-part of Figure 1 is the single clearest illustration in the paper of what "ends-means ambiguity" actually means in practice — worth walking through slowly rather than skimming past.
- The Overview case study (Figure 3) is a template for how to write up a chained task analysis: name each task's why, how, what-in, and what-out, and show how one task's output threads into the next task's input.
- The paper is candid that its evaluative and generative power are unvalidated as of publication — a good example of a methods/framework paper being honest about the difference between "we used this to describe things" and "we've shown this actually helps you design or evaluate."

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read the abstract, Section 2 (both worked examples), and Section 3 in full — Section 3 is where the typology itself lives, and Figure 1 is worth studying directly rather than through paraphrase. Section 4's case study is short and worth reading in full as the paper's clearest demonstration of chaining. Section 5.1 can be skimmed past the five singled-out systems (Springmeyer, Mullins and Treu, Pike et al., Heer and Shneiderman, Roth) — Table 1 itself is reference material to consult later, not to read row-by-row on a first pass. Section 5.2 (theoretical foundations) is worth a moderate-depth read if the distributed-cognition or sensemaking framing is new to you; otherwise skim. Sections 6 and 7 are short and worth reading for the discussion of what's validated versus what's future work, but contain no new taxonomy content.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The paper's own worked ambiguity example, verbatim in spirit: "A user may derive data items as an end in itself, for example to reduce the number of dimensions in a dataset, or as a means towards another end, such as to verify a hypothesis regarding the existence of clusters in a derived low-dimensional space."
- "We denote this work as a typology, rather than a taxonomy, as the former is appropriate for classifying abstract concepts, while the latter is appropriate for classifying empirically observable events."
- The choropleth election-map example (Texas vs. California, read casually in a blog post) as the paper's running instance of *enjoy* — a task none of the surveyed classification systems could motivate, since it isn't driven by a need to present or discover.
- The SpaceTree/TreeJuxtaposer example: both tools support locating and presenting a path between two nodes in a tree, but "TreeJuxtaposer allows the user to arrange areas of the tree to ensure visibility for areas of interest, while SpaceTree couples the act of selection by aggregating and filtering unselected items" — the tool-level difference that existing taxonomies couldn't express.
- Table 1's underlining convention (a term in the typology appearing, underlined, in cited prior work) turns the whole table into a visible map of vocabulary overlap and gaps — the paper notes that non-leaf nodes in the how-part are "poorly represented" in prior work, evidence of the very high/low gap the paper is trying to bridge.
- The acknowledgment crediting Ron Rensink "for the original idea of classifying lookup, browse, locate, and explore according to target identity and location" — the search 2×2 wasn't solely the authors' invention.
- Reference [45] in the bibliography — "T. Munzner. Visualization Analysis and Design... AK Peters, 2014 (forthcoming)" — is Munzner's textbook, cited here as still forthcoming at the time this paper went to press.

**Figures worth borrowing:** Figure 1, the three-part why/how/what diagram, is the standout candidate — it's the single image that carries the entire typology, and Munzner's ch. 3 book figures (3.1, 3.2, 3.6, 3.7) are themselves adapted from it (split across why/how/what content that the book distributes differently, see below). Figure 3 (the Overview case study's chained-task diagram) is a strong second candidate for a page illustrating how to write a multi-task, interdependent analysis — it's compact, concrete, and shows input/output threading explicitly. Figure 2 (the notational side-by-side of Example #1 and Example #2 redescribed using the typology) is a good compact illustration of the "sticky-note" visual notation the paper recommends over formal grammars, useful if a page wants to show what a finished task description actually looks like written out.

**Caveats:** This is fundamentally a conceptual/methodological paper; its own validation is explicitly partial. The authors state plainly that "only the descriptive aspect of the typology has been directly validated in this paper" via the worked examples and case study, and that the typology's evaluative power (as a code set for qualitative studies) and generative power (as a design aid) are both flagged as future work requiring further study. The 30-work survey in Table 1 is a literature comparison, not an empirical validation exercise, and the classification of each prior system's terms against the typology's nodes is the authors' own interpretive judgment. The typology also explicitly declines to specify what (data/view semantics), deferring that question to other work (the paper points to Munzner's separate publications) — so on its own, the paper is incomplete as a full task-and-data framework; it needs to be paired with a data-abstraction vocabulary to do full analysis. Readers should also not mistake the typology for a decision procedure: it is a descriptive lexicon for making task descriptions precise and comparable, not an algorithm that outputs correct design choices.

**Relationship to Munzner, ch. 3 "Task Abstraction" (from *Visualization Analysis and Design*):** Munzner's book chapter is built directly on this paper — its own Further Reading cites it by name — and inherits the paper's *why* vocabulary almost verbatim: consume/produce, discover (split into generate/verify) versus present versus enjoy, annotate/record/derive as produce's leaves, and search's lookup/locate/browse/explore with the identical known-identity/known-location logic (the book even keeps the mammal-tree-style illustrative device the paper's search discussion suggests, applied to its own examples). The book's query vocabulary (identify/compare/summarize) also carries over unchanged, with the book adding "overview" as an explicit synonym for summarize that the paper mentions only in passing (the paper notes summarize "is also often associated with overviews of the data"). Where the book chapter diverges is in what: the paper takes a deliberate "bring your own what" stance, declining to commit to a targets vocabulary and pointing elsewhere for one; the book chapter instead commits to a concrete, positive targets vocabulary (trends, outliers, features, distribution, dependency, correlation, similarity, topology, paths, shape) as its own answer to the same open question the paper left unanswered.

The most consequential restructuring is that the book chapter does not include the paper's *how* part at all. The paper's Figure 1b — encode, manipulate (select/navigate/arrange/change/filter/aggregate), and introduce (annotate/import/derive/record) — has no counterpart in ch. 3; that vocabulary is instead distributed across several later, how-focused chapters of the book (manipulate view, facet, reduce, and others, roughly chs. 11–13). In other words, the book splits the paper's single three-part why/how/what typology across the book's entire two-part macro-structure, presenting a why-only chapter where the paper presents why, how, and what side by side in one figure.

This restructuring has a specific, traceable cost: the paper's central rhetorical move — showing that a term like derive is genuinely ambiguous between why and how by deliberately letting it appear as a leaf in *both* halves of Figure 1 — is invisible to a reader of ch. 3 alone. The book chapter presents only the why-side derive (under produce, in §3.4.2.3, where it gets extended and design-relevant treatment: "don't just draw what you're given... create it with a series of transformations... and draw that!") and never juxtaposes it against the how-side derive that lives instead in a later chapter the reader may not have reached yet, or may read in a different session entirely. A student who has read only ch. 3 will come away knowing the resolved vocabulary — derive as a produce-goal — without ever encountering the ends-means ambiguity argument that motivated the paper's entire project in the first place. The book teaches the answer without teaching the question.

The SpaceTree/TreeJuxtaposer example is a second point of direct inheritance with an inverted rhetorical function. The book's §3.7.1 (Figures 3.8–3.9) draws the example straight from this paper's Section 2, but uses it oppositely: the paper's Section 2 deploys the example negatively, to show that Heer and Shneiderman's and Lee et al.'s taxonomies fail to capture why the task is performed and fail to distinguish how the two tools differ; the book instead presents the same pair of tools as a positive worked example of a complete, successful why-how-what analysis, using the book's own (paper-derived) vocabulary to show what the two tools have in common and where they diverge. Same source material, same two tools, opposite pedagogical use.

Finally, the paper's Section 4 case study — the Overview text-document tool, walked as a three-task chain (derive+aggregate → discover/explore/identify → produce/annotate) — does not appear anywhere in the book chapter. Munzner's ch. 3 instead illustrates chaining and derive-as-design-decision with its own separate worked examples (Strahler numbers for tree filtering, trade balance as a derived attribute, and a fluid-dynamics derived-variable-space example), none of which overlap with Overview. Despite these structural differences, the two documents converge on the same underlying claim, stated in two different registers: the paper's academic framing, "distinguish[ing] ends from means," is the book's textbook aphorism, "why doesn't dictate how" — the same idea, argued at length in one and asserted as a repeated refrain in the other.
