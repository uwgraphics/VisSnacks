# Ghulam Jilani Quadri and Paul Rosen, "A Survey of Perception-Based Visualization Studies by Task" (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, 28(12):5026–5048, 2022)

**What it is:** A literature survey (a STAR-style report, not an empirical study) of perception-focused visualization studies from 1980 to 2019. It claims the community lacks a comprehensive guide for contextualizing the many graphical-perception results, and it organizes the studies by analysis task (Amar et al.'s ten low-level tasks plus a derived "compare" task), then subdivides each task by visual encoding and by visualization type. It sits in the line of Cleveland and McGill, Mackinlay, Ware, and Healey and Enns, and it explicitly positions itself against design-recommendation books and against Elliott et al.'s design space of vision-science methods. It is a map of what has been studied and where the gaps are, not a set of design rules. The authors say so directly.

**AI summary:** The introduction argues that perception sits upstream of the cognitive processes of analysis, traces the field back to Cleveland and McGill's ranking of visual encodings, and notes a rising number of perception-based studies (Fig. 1, publications per year since 2000). The scope is information visualization only, and perception is read as "graphical perception." The authors flag two chronic problems: studies have limited scope and are rarely reproduced, and human studies are resource-limited, so results transfer on "tenuous footing." The survey's stated goal is to collect and organize findings so readers can judge whether a result applies to their context. An audience guide targets students, practitioners, and researchers. Related work covers design recommendations (Ware, Healey and Enns, the VisGuides forum), frameworks (Rensink's extended-vision and optimal-reduction theses, Elliott et al.'s design space), and evaluation (crowdsourcing per Borgo et al., quality metrics per Behrisch et al.).

The method section describes a keyword search of the ACM, IEEE, and EG/CGF libraries for 1980–2019, with PRISMA used as a guide for screening. Table 1 counts surveyed papers by source (TVCG, InfoVis, VAST, PacificVis, CHI, TOG, CGF, EuroVis and others; a small "others" bucket holds pivotal works found along the way). Inclusion examples are experiments on graphical perception of visual tasks or designs, studies of visualization methods, and studies that offer design guidelines. Exclusions are user studies comparing models, quality-metric checks of a system, evaluations of tools or systems, and studies of graphics or UI rather than visualization. An interactive version of the taxonomy, a spreadsheet of papers, the PRISMA flow, and a categorization template are hosted on a companion site.

Section 2 sets the taxonomy. The authors first considered Brehmer and Munzner's task typology but rejected it as too high-level (perceptual plus cognitive). They adopted Amar et al.'s ten low-level tasks (retrieve value, filter, compute derived value, find extremum, sort, determine range, characterize distribution, find anomalies, cluster, correlate) and added "compare" as an eleventh. Visual encodings are collapsed into two families, spatial and color, each with two subcategories: Spatial Position and Shape, Spatial Size, Color Hue, and Color Intensity (which absorbs saturation, luminance, shading, and opacity). They reproduce Cleveland and McGill's encoding list (Fig. 3) and Mackinlay's rankings by data type (Fig. 4). Visualization type has ten categories plus "other": scatterplot, bar chart, line chart, parallel coordinates, map, network, heatmap, pie chart, bubble chart, and text.

Section 3, the bulk of the paper, takes the eleven tasks in turn, each with encoding, visualization-type, and summary subsections.

- **Retrieve value:** Saket et al.'s small-data comparison finds tables best on accuracy, time, and preference, with bar and pie charts good on accuracy and time. Position-based scatterplots encode extra attributes more slowly with size than with color, though size improved accuracy. Embellished or pictorial bar charts hurt retrieval, and parallel coordinates were learned quickly by first-time users in an eye-tracking study. The summary advises bar charts when accuracy matters.
- **Filter (including search):** Popout studies, word-cloud and text-layout studies (alphabetical order speeds keyword search; larger fonts are recalled more), and cartogram comparisons.
- **Compute derived value:** The area that holds Cleveland and McGill and its crowdsourced replication. It also covers "perceptual pull" bias in estimating average position, colormap effects, affective priming (Harrison et al.), social information, and working-memory effects. Bar, line, pie, and scatterplot designs are all usable for summary tasks.
- **Find extremum, determine range, and sort:** Described as thinly studied, mostly on bar and line charts. For extremum, line charts suit time series and scatterplots and bar charts suit position- or length-encoded quantities. For range, scatterplots win on speed, bar charts on accuracy, and parallel coordinates for multidimensional data. For sort, bar charts and text-based visualizations are preferred for quantitative and textual data respectively. Wrapped bars come out best among ranked-list designs, though treemaps were the most accurate.
- **Find anomalies:** The studies cover symbol-size discrimination via a power-law model, color/motion/layout grouping effects on outlier search, and luminance discriminability. Scatterplots and bar charts did best in Saket et al., and scatterplots have been most heavily studied. Time-series outlier detection with line charts has low accuracy.
- **Characterize distribution:** Covers density and gaps, symbol size and lightness, scagnostics, and a line-versus-scatterplot choice that depends on noise (line chart for low noise, scatterplot for high noise). Also covered is "regression by eye," where visual trend estimates diverge systematically from least-squares fits.
- **Cluster:** Scatterplots dominate. The section covers mark size, opacity, aspect ratio, and color assignment for class separation, a taxonomy of cluster-separation factors (scale, point distance, shape, position), and algorithmic measures (Sedlmair and Aupetit's 15 class-separation measures, ScatterNet, ClustMe, scagnostics). It notes that scagnostics "do not reliably reproduce human judgments" and cites the authors' own topology-based perception model. Priming and anchoring biases are discussed. Whether scatterplots or parallel coordinates are better for clusters is flagged as unresolved.
- **Correlate:** The most extensively studied task, mostly on scatterplots. It covers the Weber's-law/JND modeling of Rensink and Baldridge, Harrison et al.'s ranking of nine visualizations, and Kay and Heer's Bayesian log-linear reanalysis, with the conclusion that scatterplots lead on precision for both positive and negative correlation. This is set against Saket et al., where line graphs came out on top with few points. Scale effects, map-lineup studies on choropleths, and geo-temporal guidance are included.
- **Compare:** Treated as compound, a retrieval or derived-value step followed by a comparison. It covers bar-chart variants, embellishments, horizon graphs and colorfields for time series, and node-link versus matrix views for networks. The summary says the choice of visualization is "subtask-driven and design-dependent."

Section 4 draws conclusions. Effectiveness is task-dependent, and the authors echo Saket et al.'s "No One Size Fits All." Design variants within a single chart type also matter a great deal. Section 4.2 describes a maturing field in which much accepted "fact" has never been adequately tested, with parallel coordinates as the example. Section 4.3 covers limited scope and reproducibility: few studies are replicated (Heer and Bostock's crowdsourced replication and Kay and Heer's reanalysis are noted exceptions), there are weak incentives for replication, and the authors encourage open data. Section 4.4 lists open questions: studies concentrate on scatterplots, bar charts, and line charts, and on the tasks retrieve value, compute derived value, and compare (Table 2 tabulates study counts by task, encoding, and visualization type). Data-collection methods, metrics, apparatus, and participant pools vary, and ecological validity is often low. Cognition and perception are hard to separate, and subject confidence is largely unreported. An appendix (Section 5) gives a primer on psychophysics (thresholds, Weber's law, JND) and on perceptual and cognitive biases (clustering illusion, change blindness, attraction effect, anchoring, priming, affective priming).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- This is a navigation aid for the perception literature. Organizing by task (what the viewer is trying to do) rather than by chart or channel is a useful lens for the "which chart for which job" question, and the per-task summaries are short enough to use as quick orientation.
- The paper's recurring theme is that effectiveness is task-dependent and that no single visualization suits every situation. This supports teaching chart choice as a function of task, data, and design variant instead of a universal ranking.
- Rankings such as Cleveland and McGill's and Mackinlay's have been validated and replicated, but most follow-up findings come from narrow, rarely replicated studies. Treat a single study's result as a local finding, not a rule.
- Even within one chart type, design variations (embellishments, stacked versus superimposed bars, line-chart variants, horizon-graph band counts) can change performance significantly.
- Some "known" things are less settled than assumed. The paper cites a study suggesting parallel coordinates may not be as hard for first-time users as their reputation implies, and a study showing scagnostics do not reliably match human judgments.
- For correlation specifically, the scatterplot evidence is strongest, and the rankings can flip with the sign of the correlation (parallel coordinates in particular). Perception of correlation follows Weber's law, which is the paper's main example of a quantitative perceptual model.
- Bias and context matter: affective priming, anchoring, social information, and individual working memory all shifted judgments in the studies cited. This is useful for the "how much to trust a chart reading" thread.
- The study-count table is a map of research gaps: sort, find anomalies, determine range, and filter are the least studied tasks, and non-standard chart types get little perceptual evaluation.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read Section 1 (especially 1.3), Section 2, and Section 4 closely. They carry the framing, the taxonomy, and the paper's conclusions. Section 3 is a reference catalog of about eleven task subsections. Read it selectively by task, using the "Summary" subsection at the end of each (3.x.3 or 3.x.2) to decide whether to read further; the correlation (3.10) and cluster (3.9) sections are the densest and most relevant for scatterplot-related work. Table 1 (surveyed papers by source) can be skipped unless the search method matters. Table 2 (counts by task, encoding, and visualization type) is worth studying as a gap map. Figures worth looking at: Fig. 4 (Mackinlay's rankings by data type), Fig. 5 (Saket et al.'s pairwise comparison of visualization types across tasks), Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 (Harrison et al.'s correlation ranking and Kay and Heer's refinement), and Fig. 7 (Szafir's color difference versus size). The appendix (Section 5) is a self-contained primer on Weber's law, JND, and biases; read it if those concepts are new, skip it otherwise. The reference list (about the last third of the PDF) is useful as a bibliography of the field.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The task set: Amar et al.'s ten (retrieve value, filter, compute derived value, find extremum, sort, determine range, characterize distribution, find anomalies, cluster, correlate) plus a derived "compare," for 11 low-level task groups.
- Encoding families: "roughly two main categories: spatial encodings and color encodings, each with two subcategories." Visualization types: ten categories plus "other."
- Search window and sources: papers from 1980–2019, with PRISMA used "as a guide." The paper says the survey drew on ACM, IEEE, and EG/CGF libraries.
- Table 2's per-task study totals (the "Total" column): compute derived value 48, compare 34, retrieve value 31, find extremum 19, characterize distribution 19, correlate 17, filter 15, determine range 14, cluster 13, sort 12, find anomalies 10. Among visualization types, scatterplots have the most studies in the table's total row (42), then bar charts (38) and line charts (27).
- Saket et al.'s finding that "No One Size Fits All" (quoted in Section 4.1), used by the authors to summarize the whole survey's thrust.
- On the survey's own claim, Section 4.1: "there is no single visualization suitable for all situations."
- Perceptual pull: estimates of average position in line and bar charts were "pulled" toward irrelevant data in the series (Section 3.3).
- Kay and Heer's Bayesian log-linear reanalysis of Harrison et al. is reported to swap the rankings of parallel coordinates and scatterplot for negative correlation (Fig. 9 caption: "parallel coordinates–negative and scatterplot–negative swap positions").
- Fig. 8 caption observation: "judgment precision had a striking variation between negatively and positively correlated data on certain visualizations, e.g., parallel coordinates."
- Weber's law as given in the appendix: the just-noticeable change is "a constant ratio of the original stimulus," illustrated with adding 0.1 kg to a 5 kg weight for a Weber fraction of 0.02. JND is "usually 50% of the time."
- The appendix's attraction-effect example (Fig. 10) uses hypothetical election candidates Alice, Bob, and the decoy Eve, with Dimara et al.'s scatterplot study cited as evidence of the effect in visualization.
- Authors' own stated purpose, Section 4.5: "the aim of this survey was not to directly summarize the visualization recommendations but instead to provide a broad understanding of the topics that have been evaluated."
- Directive to researchers, Section 4.4: "Researchers should consider focusing their new research efforts on the less studied tasks and visualization types."
- Gleicher et al.'s large-scale crowdsourced study on aggregate relative judgments in multi-class scatterplots is cited under compute derived value (Section 3.3.1).

**Caveats:** This is a summary of the July 2021 arXiv author preprint (arXiv:2107.07477v1). The page header ("Journal of LaTeX Class Files, vol. 14, no. 8, August 2015") and the "Manuscript received" line are template boilerplate and should be ignored; the published TVCG version (28(12), 2022) may differ in content, counts, and wording. The survey is explicitly broad rather than deep: it describes what was studied and does not synthesize design rules, and each study "comes with caveats that we could not enumerate." Many conclusions rest on single studies from small or narrow settings (small datasets, student or crowd participants). The paper acknowledges that data-collection methods and metrics vary enough to hinder meta-analysis. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are given only as examples ("We could not include all filtering categories due to space limitations"), so exact coverage is not reproducible from the PDF alone. The corpus stops at 2019, so later work is absent. Read problems: the PDF is a two-column layout, and text extraction interleaved columns and scrambled some figure labels. The figures themselves (Figs. 1–10) were not visible in extracted text, so figure descriptions here rely on captions and body text. Table 1's paper counts and Table 2's column alignment were hard to read reliably in the extracted layout, so only Table 2's per-task Total column and its Total row are quoted. The Total row does not equal the sum of the rows above it (presumably because papers appear in multiple cells), and no grand total of surveyed papers is stated in the extracted text. Table 1's per-source counts were not confidently attributed to rows and are not quoted.
