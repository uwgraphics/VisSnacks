# Schulz, Nocke, Heitzler & Schumann, "A Design Space of Visualization Tasks" (IEEE TVCG / InfoVis, 2013)

**What it is:** A design-space paper (not a taxonomy) that proposes **five orthogonal design dimensions** — goal, means, characteristics, target, cardinality — meant to unify the scattered, inconsistent vocabulary of prior visualization task taxonomies, demonstrated with a climate-impact-research use case and three role-based interfaces (developer, author, end user).

**AI summary:** The paper opens from a consolidation motive typical of a maturing field: infovis has accumulated "a wealth of classifications, taxonomies, and frameworks" for tasks — verbal lists, functional/query-based descriptions, faceted schemes, hierarchical task models — each serving the purpose it was built for, but with "the research question of how to consolidate them under the hood of one common design space... still open." The authors distinguish a *design space* from a *taxonomy* explicitly: a taxonomy classifies what already exists, while a design space is a "hypothetical construct that follows the mechanistic belief that the whole is the sum of a number of independent parts," so it can also describe combinations that don't yet exist, expose gaps as research opportunities, and externalize implicit design decisions for teaching, requirements analysis, or API specification. They note two long-standing quasi-standard taxonomies as the field's reference points — Shneiderman's Task-by-Data-Type Taxonomy (TTT, high-level/system-centric) and Wehrend & Lewis's taxonomy (TWL, low-level/user-centric) — and treat the paper's job as pulling those and other schemes' distinct aspects together as dimensions of one integrated concept, rather than adjudicating between them.

The five dimensions are derived from the journalistic "5 W's" (Why, What, Where, Who, When) plus How, a framing the authors note has precedent in technical communication, task analysis, and prior visualization work. Two of the six — When (task order) and Who (the executing user) — are set aside as *context* rather than intrinsic task properties and handled separately (workflows in §3.3.3; user roles in §5.3), leaving five: **Goal** (why — exploratory analysis / confirmatory analysis / presentation), **Means** (how — Navigation [browsing/searching the extent, elaborating/summarizing the granularity], (Re-)organization [extraction via filtering/sampling, abstraction via aggregation/generalization, or enrichment from external sources], and Relation [comparison, variation-seeking, or its inverse, relation-seeking]), **Characteristics** (what data pattern is sought — low-level: look-up, inverse look-up/localization; or high-level: trends, outliers, clusters, frequency, distribution, correlation), **Target** (where in the data — a relational view distinguishing attribute relations [temporal, spatial] from structural relations [causal, topological, order, equivalence]), and **Cardinality** (how many instances — single, multiple, or all). An individual task is a 5-tuple, e.g. `(exploratory, search, trend, attrib(temperature), all)`, interpreted as a single point in the design space. Compound tasks are non-empty subspaces, built either bottom-up (enumerating tuples) or top-down (a "cut" through the space using wildcards, e.g. `(exploratory, search, *, attrib(temperature), all)`). Workflows chain individual and compound tasks into a DAG or path — the paper re-expresses Shneiderman's mantra ("overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand") directly as a three-step workflow in this notation, as one demonstration that existing taxonomies map onto the space.

A full discussion section (§4) works through the space's completeness and consistency. On completeness, the authors sort existing tasks into three buckets: those directly expressible (like the mantra example), those expressible only after *adapting* the space (system-dependent tasks tied to concrete UI actions, or domain-dependent tasks tied to a field's vocabulary — handled by renaming, refining, extending, or wholesale substituting design choices on a dimension), and those that lie genuinely *outside* the space by design — chiefly the collaborative Who-context and **meta-tasks** (tasks about tasks: undo/redo, revisit, task division, annotate), which the paper deliberately excludes even though taxonomies like the TTT often list them alongside regular data tasks. They also walk a non-trivial example (Ahn et al.'s network-evolution taxonomy) showing how a taxonomy with different-looking dimensions (entities, properties, temporal features) maps onto their target/characteristics dimensions, with the key structural difference being that a taxonomy treats means as *dependent* on what's actually observed in practice, while the design space treats means as an *independent* dimension of what's possible. On consistency, they concede design choices can have real interdependencies (e.g., "compare" as a means only makes sense with cardinality multiple/all, not single) and argue these can be encoded and checked as constraints within the same tuple notation, while cautioning that many apparent inconsistencies are just unfamiliar-but-valid combinations (e.g., "presentation" as a goal paired with rich navigational means, once interactive storytelling is considered) or artifacts of loaded wording rather than real constraints.

The use case grounds all this in climate impact research, based on a survey of eight domain experts and two visualization developers. The survey had two parts: first, free-form listing of tasks users perform, which the authors classified by complexity (compound vs. general-workflow tasks) and by terminology register (visualization-, intermediate-, or domain-specific language) — surfacing that domain experts think in inconsistent, field-specific terms. Second, users rated a list of seven candidate tasks (T1–T7, drawn from an earlier study) for relevance; the feedback led to concrete revisions — "visualize periodicities" was broadened to "analyze periodicities," and three frequently-requested tasks (analyze outliers, compare measurements with simulation data, present data for general audiences) were added — producing a ten-task domain-specific subspace (Table 2), each task expressed as a design-space tuple. This subspace is then put to two uses: evaluating whether existing tools (GGobi, ComVis) support the domain's tasks by comparing their feature-based tuples against Table 2 (surfacing, e.g., that both lack strong geospatial support), and — the paper's more ambitious demonstration — powering a recommendation pipeline across three role-based interfaces: **developers** work with a low-level XML encoding of the raw 5-tuples for full flexibility; **authors** get a GUI that lets them refine, rename, and describe a developer-defined compound task for a specific domain audience; **end users** pick a named, described task from a list and receive a ranked list of suitable visualization techniques from a rule-based matching mechanism, with a "contribution" column (usually hidden, exposed to authors for debugging) showing which task facets drove the ranking. The conclusion is deliberately modest: the authors frame the contribution as "a first step towards concretizing the 'colorful terminology swirl in this domain'" (borrowing Shneiderman's phrase), report informally that the design space improved their own team's communication about tasks, and flag as future work testing the space in other application domains and building an analogous "interaction design space" on top of it.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- A concrete instance of the "5 W's + How" framing applied to *tasks* specifically — useful to read right before or after Gleicher et al.'s Problem Space, which explicitly adapts this same paper's 5W+H structure but shifts the unit of analysis from "task" to "problem," making Why (goal) only one of six axes instead of the organizing structure. Reading them back to back makes that generalization step (task-space → problem-space) legible.
- A worked demonstration that Goal and Means correspond closely to what Rind et al.'s Task Cube calls **perspective** (why vs. how) — but Schulz et al. don't have an explicit counterpart to Task Cube's abstraction or composition axes. Instead, level-of-abstraction is handled informally via the "adapting the design space" mechanisms (renaming/refining/extending/substituting, §4.1.2), and composition is handled by the three-tier individual-task/compound-task/workflow structure (§3.3) rather than a labeled axis. Comparing the two papers is a good exercise in seeing how the same underlying distinctions (why/how, level of granularity) get formalized differently depending on a paper's goals.
- The Characteristics dimension (low-level: look-up/localization; high-level: trend/outlier/cluster/distribution/correlation) is close kin to Amar et al.'s ten low-level analytic tasks — Amar et al. is a bottom-up, empirically-derived vocabulary for roughly this one dimension, at finer grain, while Schulz et al. treat it as one of five orthogonal slots to be filled in alongside goal, means, target, and cardinality.
- The mantra-as-workflow example (§3.3.3) is a clean illustration that Shneiderman's "task by data type taxonomy" and its mantra aren't competitors to this design space — they're expressible *within* it, which is exactly the consolidating claim the paper is making.
- The practical, software-engineering payoff most other task papers don't offer: a literal tuple/XML encoding used to drive a rule-based visualization recommender, and a three-role (developer/author/end-user) interface pattern for who gets to touch which part of a task specification — worth remembering as a template if a reader ever needs to operationalize any task framework in actual software, not just use it as an analysis lens.
- The candid discussion of what's deliberately left out (meta-tasks, the Who/collaboration context) is a useful model for scoping any design space: the paper is explicit that keeping a design space useful requires excluding things that would otherwise need their own multi-dimensional treatment (they cite a 12-dimension model of "context" that they pointedly did not fold in).

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the Introduction and §2 (related work, for how they position TTT/TWL and the design-space-vs-taxonomy distinction), and all of §3 (Construction of the Design Space) — this is the paper's core and is short enough to read in full, including the individual/compound/workflow tuple notation. §4 (Discussion) is worth a careful read too, especially §4.1 on completeness (the three-bucket sorting of tasks and the meta-task exclusion) — it's where the paper argues for its own scope. The climate-impact use case (§5) is the most skimmable section for a general reader: the survey methodology (how Table 2's ten tasks were arrived at) is worth a skim, but the framework-comparison (GGobi vs. ComVis, Table 3) and the developer/author/end-user interface walkthrough (Figs. 1–3) are domain- and tooling-specific detail that mainly matters if you're building a similar recommender system yourself.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The five dimensions, verbatim definitions: Goal ("the intent with which the task is pursued" — exploratory / confirmatory / presentation), Means ("the method for reaching the goal" — navigation / (re-)organization / relation), Characteristics ("the facets of the data that the task aims to reveal" — low-level vs. high-level), Target ("determines on which part of the data it is carried out" — attribute vs. structural relations), Cardinality ("how many instances of the chosen target are considered" — single / multiple / all).
- The 5-tuple notation and worked example: `(exploratory, search, trend, attrib(temperature), all)` — "the user is searching for a trend among all available temperature attribute values."
- The mantra re-expressed as a workflow: `(exploratory, summarize, *, *, all) ⇒ (exploratory, elaborate|filter, *, *, multiple)+ ⇒ (exploratory|confirmatory, gather, look-up, *, single)`.
- The use case: 8 end users from climate impact research + 2 visualization developers; a two-part survey (free listing, then rating a T1–T7 candidate list) producing 10 domain tasks (Table 2), including additions like "analyze outliers" and the T7 rename from "visualize periodicities" to "analyze periodicities."
- The consistency example: "compare" as a means implicitly requires cardinality multiple or all — "compare" with cardinality "single" is called out as an inconsistent combination.
- The closing self-description, quoting Shneiderman: "a first step towards concretizing the 'colorful terminology swirl in this domain.'"
- Design-space vs. taxonomy, stated plainly: "design spaces... do not only describe the existing, but also the (so far) not existing... In this aspect, design spaces inherently differ from taxonomies, which are by definition classifications of what actually exists."

**Figures worth borrowing:** No single figure lays out all five dimensions side by side as a clean diagram the way Rind et al.'s Task Cube has its labeled three-axis cube — the design space here lives mainly in prose and tuple notation, not a diagram. Table 2 (the ten domain tasks, each with its full 5-tuple notation) is the paper's most useful reusable artifact for teaching — a compact, concrete demonstration of the tuple scheme applied to real tasks — though it would need redrawing/simplifying since it's dense and climate-domain-specific as printed. Fig. 3 (the end-user visualization-recommendation interface, showing a ranked technique list with a "contribution" transparency column) is a nice illustration of *task specification driving a concrete recommendation*, useful if a page wants to show what task abstraction is actually good for downstream, beyond taxonomy for its own sake.

**Caveats:** This is a conceptual design-space proposal, not an empirically validated one — the climate-impact use case is a demonstration of applicability (via interviews and worked matching against two real tools), not a controlled study testing whether the five dimensions are the right ones, sufficient, or usable by non-experts unaided. The authors are explicit that the space's completeness/consistency balance was a judgment call ("we have struck a particular balance") and that they are "still in the process of consolidating and evaluating" it even for this one domain, with plans to test it in other domains as future work — so treat the five dimensions as a considered proposal, not a settled consolidation of the field. Compared to Rind et al.'s Task Cube, the two are complementary rather than competing: Task Cube is a narrower, purely conceptual paper whose entire job is disentangling the overloaded word "task" into three meta-level axes (perspective/abstraction/composition) so people can talk about task *concepts* more precisely — it does not offer a task specification scheme. Schulz et al. is broader in a different direction: it offers an actual operational 5-tuple specification (goal/means/characteristics/target/cardinality) meant to be filled in for real tasks and fed into software (XML encoding, rule-based recommendation) — but it handles level-of-abstraction and composition informally (via ad hoc "adaptation" mechanisms) rather than as first-class axes the way Task Cube does. A reader expecting Schulz et al. to resolve the why/how or abstraction-level ambiguities Task Cube worries about will be disappointed; a reader expecting Task Cube to offer a usable task-specification tuple will likewise be disappointed — each paper solves a different half of the same underlying mess. Neither paper claims to have achieved field-wide adoption of its scheme.
