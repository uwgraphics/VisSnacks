# Dimara, Bezerianos & Dragicevic, "The Attraction Effect in Information Visualization", IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, vol. 23, no. 1, pp. 471-480, 2017 (DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2016.2598594)

**What it is:** An empirical paper: two crowdsourced experiments testing whether the attraction effect (also called the decoy effect or asymmetric dominance effect), a cognitive bias from decision-making research, shows up when choice alternatives are shown in a visualization (a scatterplot) rather than a numerical table. The claim is that it does, both in a classical three-alternative setup and in a new extension with about 20 alternatives. It is presented as the first infovis study of the attraction effect. It sits between two literatures: visualization work on decision support and on (mostly probabilistic) cognitive biases, and the marketing/psychology debate over whether the attraction effect generalizes beyond numerical presentations. The PDF read here is the HAL authors' version (submitted to HAL 29 Nov 2016), not the typeset journal version; the cover page says "Changes were made to this version by the publisher prior to publication."

**AI summary:** The introduction opens with a hypothetical election: Bob is strong on education and weak on crime control, Alice the reverse, and a third candidate, Eve, resembles Alice but is worse on crime control. Adding Eve shifts preference toward Alice (the paper cites O'Curry and Pitts for a similar study). The authors ask whether this transfers to visualization: if someone uses a visualization to choose among options (the paper's example is buying an apartment), does the presence of inferior options affect the choice? They argue it matters because visualization systems are increasingly used for decisions (drug-trial portfolio tracking, software release planning, and research tools for choosing a house, nursing home, cereal, camera, investment, or factory site). They also argue that visualizations are usually judged by whether they help people extract accurate information, but full information does not guarantee good decisions. Prior visualization work on cognitive bias has focused on probabilistic reasoning and uncertainty. The attraction effect is different because the irrationality comes from irrelevant information, not from uncertainty. They chose this bias because it is heavily studied but almost always tested with three alternatives in numerical formats, and there is "conflicting evidence and a heated debate" on whether it generalizes beyond numbers.

The background (Section 2) first surveys visualizations for decision support (HomeFinder, Dust & Magnet, treemap-based AHP, ValueCharts, Decision Map/Table & Box, Stratos, VisIDM, FinVis) and prior studies in which visualizations helped, or failed to help, with biases. It also notes that some biases, such as the within-the-bar bias, appear only with visualizations. It then defines the terminology. An alternative dominates another if it is strictly better on one attribute and at least equal on all others. An alternative is asymmetrically dominated if it is dominated by at least one alternative but not by at least one other. The decoy is the asymmetrically dominated option, the target is the option that dominates the decoy, and the competitor is the option that does not. The target and competitor are formally uncomparable. The effect is that adding a decoy raises preference for the target. It is observed between groups, not within an individual, and it violates the principle of regularity of rational choice theory (adding an option should not increase the preference for an existing one). Two families of explanation are described. Strategic theories hold that people use dominance over the decoy as a simplifying heuristic and as a justification. Perceptual theories hold that the decoy shifts the perceived importance of attributes, by analogy with contrast effects. All explanations require that the dominance relation be perceptible. The authors note that visualizations could therefore make the effect easier to see, perhaps even amplify it. They review evidence that the effect appears with consumer products, gambles, hiring, voting, and even animals. They also review the dispute: Frederick et al. and Yang and Lynn argue it appears only with numerical attributes, while Huber et al. and Simonson questioned those failed replications. The closest prior test was Frederick et al.'s gambling task with a probability wheel, where the effect vanished. The authors consider that design domain-specific, because only one of the two attributes was encoded visually.

Experiment 1 (the "gym" experiment, Section 3) replicates a standard design and then swaps the table for a scatterplot. It copies the scenario of Malkoc et al. (choosing a fitness club described by variety and cleanliness). Attribute values were incremented by one to keep points off the scatterplot axes: gC (variety 2, cleanliness 5), gV (5, 2), gC\* (1, 5), and gV\* (5, 1), the last two being decoys. There were three decision tasks: no decoy, decoy on cleanliness, and decoy on variety. Tables put attributes in rows and alternatives in columns. Scatterplots were static, with points labeled A, B (C), and the choice made with radio buttons. Tables and scatterplots were made as visually similar as possible. Because order could matter, the authors randomized the order of attributes and alternatives. After removing placements that put the decoy not next to the target (tables) or in the middle (scatterplots), they used 4 table stimuli and 2 scatterplot stimuli per decision task, 18 stimuli in all. Quality control used highly rated Crowdflower contributors (level 3), a required text justification, a bacteriophobia question (to catch strong prior preference for cleanliness), a numerical table test, and a scatterplot test (choose the dominating laptop of three, to check visualization literacy and dominance perception). Of 437 submitted jobs, 16% were rejected (Red) and 14% were accepted but discarded (Orange). Specifically, 1% had abnormal completion time, 14% had no proper justification, and 12% failed the table test (Red), while 12% reported bacteriophobia and 13% failed the scatterplot test (Orange). The design was 3 (decision task) x 2 (table or scatterplot) between-subjects. There were 305 valid participants, with n = 54, 51, 50 for the three table tasks and n = 47, 53, 50 for the three scatterplot tasks (target n = 50 per cell). They were mostly young, educated men. The job took about 6 minutes and paid $0.60. The hypotheses were H1 (the target is chosen more with a decoy than without, on tables) and H2 (the same on scatterplots).

Results for Experiment 1 are reported with interval estimation (95% confidence intervals), not significance tests, and the stimuli, data, and analysis scripts are posted at aviz.fr/decoy. Only one participant chose a decoy, far below the up to 13% in earlier studies (Frederick et al.), which the authors read as evidence that participants were attentive and could perceive dominance. On tables, the decoy on variety raised target choice by 20%, but the decoy on cleanliness lowered it by 6%. On scatterplots the decoy on cleanliness raised target choice by 18% and the decoy on variety by 3%. The confidence intervals for the table decoy-on-cleanliness reversal and the scatterplot decoy-on-variety effect are too wide to conclude anything, but the other two conditions give "good evidence" of an attraction effect, comparable in size to Malkoc et al. The authors therefore describe H1 and H2 as partially confirmed and "less 'clean'" than the original. They then computed a "combined attraction effect" (the sum of the two decoy-condition effects, equivalently the difference in target choice between the two decoy conditions), which has more statistical power and can be compared with experiments that have no no-decoy condition. A Bayesian analysis with a Jeffreys prior gave these results. For tables, a combined effect is 34 times more likely than a practically null effect (set at +/-1%) and 11 times more likely than a "repulsion" effect. For scatterplots it is 150 times more likely than a practically null effect and 66 times more likely than repulsion. Confidence ratings were high (mean 5.9 to 6.1 on a 7-point scale) across conditions. In discussion the authors have no explanation for the table asymmetry and attribute it largely to noise. They say the table effect is about half the size of Malkoc et al.'s and list possible reasons for the smaller effect: more varied stimuli, crowdsourcing instead of lab students, their rejection criteria, and participants' familiarity and prior preferences. They conclude that the combined effect gives "compelling evidence" that the attraction effect generalizes to two-point scatterplots.

Section 4 extends the effect to more than three alternatives. The authors argue that Bettman et al. predicted the effect would vanish with many alternatives, because pairwise comparisons and dominance relationships become hard to see in tables, but that scatterplots are designed to support comparison of many points. They consider three ways to add alternatives: more non-dominated options (rejected, because it breaks the target/competitor dichotomy and brings in the compromise effect), more decoys, and "distractors" (irrelevant dominated options present in both conditions). Their procedure starts from a baseline task T0 with two non-dominated alternatives A and B plus distractors in three dominance regions: RA (dominated by A only), RB (by B only), and RAB (by both). Test task TA adds extra decoys to RA (so A is the target and B the competitor), and TB adds extra decoys to RB. With no distractors and one decoy this reduces to the classical experiment. Decoys, targets, and competitors are always defined relative to a baseline task.

Experiment 2 (the "bet" experiment, Section 5) is a within-subjects replication of Wedell (1991), who used lottery tickets. Each ticket has a probability of winning and a prize. The non-dominated alternatives come from A to F: probabilities 0.83, 0.67, 0.5, 0.4, 0.3, 0.25 with prizes $12, $15, $20, $25, $33, $40 (Table 2). All have an expected value of about $10, so the tasks have the same dominance structure as two independent attributes. F was added, and tasks with adjacent target/competitor pairs were excluded, so that target and competitor are not too close in the plot. This gave 10 pairs of "matched" tasks (XY and YX), 20 tasks in total. For each pair, distractors (1 or 2, randomly drawn) were added in each of RA, RC, and RAC (the paper's example pair is A and C), and then 10 to 20 decoys were added to the target's region, drawn from a bivariate half-normal distribution so that they cluster near the target and thin out with distance. Overlaps were removed. The counts were randomized so participants could not easily infer a pattern. Task order kept matched tasks at least 5 pages apart and alternated each alternative's role, with a second order that swapped each task for its matched task. Seven irrelevant filler tasks (one or three non-dominated options, no imbalance) were added, and a short flicker between pages was added to induce change blindness. The scatterplots were interactive: points were unlabeled, highlighted on hover with projection lines and X/Y values on the axes, clicked to select, and then confirmed with a button. Quality control added a tutorial (probability explained qualitatively, to encourage intuition) and a test question with 13 tickets (three non-dominated), and made the task consequential: winning tickets paid a bonus proportional to the prize (conversion rate 0.0025 between virtual dollars and USD), about $0.60 total on average. Of 120 valid-code jobs, 12% were rejected (abnormal time 0.8%, failing the tutorial test 11%, or choosing a dominated option more than half the time 12%) and 27% were discarded for always choosing the highest probability (0% for always choosing the highest prize), a strong risk-averse prior preference. That left 73 participants (61%). The hypothesis H3 was that the mean attraction score is strictly positive.

Results for Experiment 2: there were 1460 choices (73 x 20), forming 730 matched pairs. Only 24 pairs (3.3%) included a dominated option (Wedell reports 2%). The remaining 706 pairs are shown as a contingency table (Figure 8) next to Wedell's. In the paper's table, 59% of pairs chose the highest-probability ticket both times, 10% chose the highest prize both times, 23% always chose the target, and 8% always chose the competitor. Participants favored high probability overall, but when inconsistent they chose targets more often than competitors, which matches Wedell's pattern. The attraction score is computed per participant: each task scores 1 for the highest-probability ticket, 0 for the highest-prize ticket, and 0.5 for a dominated ticket. Averages are taken over the 10 tasks with decoys on probability (Sprob) and the 10 with decoys on prize (Sprize), and S = Sprob - Sprize, multiplied by 100. The mean attraction score was 15%, 95% bootstrap CI [10%, 22%], giving "very solid evidence" for H3, though smaller than Malkoc et al.'s gym effect. In the follow-up questionnaire, 65 participants answered, since 8 could not see the image. Asked whether the uneven distribution of tickets affected their choices, 41% said "never" or "rarely", 46% "sometimes", 12% "often", and none "always". Asked why there were more tickets in one region, 86% gave irrelevant or unintelligible answers, 5 of the other 9 cited a strategy of the lottery organizer, and 4 cited other players' past choices. Only 4 said they had that explanation in mind during the task, so there is "little evidence" that preference for the target came from deliberate reasoning. The data on participants' knowledge of expected value was lost to a bug.

The discussion concludes that the two experiments together suggest the attraction effect generalizes to visualizations and persists with many alternatives, contrary to Bettman et al.'s expectation for tables. The number and position of irrelevant alternatives can shift choices, violating rational choice axioms. For design, the authors say cognitive biases can affect decisions even when data are well visualized and understood, so "traditional visualization design rules may not apply when the goal is to support decision making." They suggest, without testing, that the simplest debiasing is to show only the Pareto front. They note two problems: it assumes the system knows the user's criteria, and dominated options can show dataset trends and give useful context. So debiasing should be optional and on demand. De-emphasizing dominated options or highlighting the Pareto front are other untested ideas. The stated limitations are as follows. Apparent irrationality may sometimes be a reasonable heuristic (many dominated options in a region could signal a larger market), although not in their bet task. They did not investigate why the effect occurs, and in the bet experiment the region with many decoys was more salient and the competitor was an isolated point, so part of the scatterplot effect may be perceptual. The datasets were synthetic and extreme (two non-dominated options plus many decoys). Their earlier experiment with real datasets, reported separately as a research report (Inria RR-8895), was inconclusive, which suggests real-data effects "may be small and hard to measure." Future work includes more realistic datasets, other chart types, other biases alone and in combination, and the interaction of cognitive biases with visual perception.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The attraction effect in one sentence: adding an irrelevant, clearly worse option next to one of two otherwise comparable options makes people choose the option it sits near more often. The paper's vocabulary (target, competitor, decoy, distractor, asymmetric dominance, Pareto front) is a useful toolkit for talking about it.
- The main message for visualization design is that a chart can show every value accurately and still not lead to a rational choice, because the context of irrelevant points shapes what people pick. "Accurate perception" is not the same as "good decisions."
- The effect showed up in a plain scatterplot with about 20 points (mean attraction score 15%, CI [10%, 22%]), not only in tables with three items, so the common belief that it is a numeric-table artifact is not supported by this study.
- The evidence is mixed in places and the authors say so: the gym experiment gave only a partial replication (two of four conditions clearly showed the effect), and the effect was weaker than in the original study. A reader should keep "this is real and worth studying" separate from "this is a large, reliable effect everywhere."
- The paper is a good model of replication and statistical practice in HCI: confidence intervals instead of p-values, analyses planned before data collection, a within-subjects design to gain power, a combined effect measure, quality-control tiers (Red/Orange/Green), a real monetary stake, and shared data.
- Design implications are speculative: showing only the Pareto front, de-emphasizing dominated points, or highlighting the Pareto front. The paper does not test any of them.
- Honest limits: synthetic data with only two non-dominated options, scatterplots only, and part of the effect may be perceptual (salience of the dense region) rather than a decision-making bias.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The paper is short enough to read in full. The essential parts are the introduction, Section 2.2 (terminology and the dominance definitions, which the rest depends on), Section 4.2 (the extended procedure with its Figure 6 diagram), the results sections 3.3 and 5.3, and Section 6 (discussion, limitations, design implications). Section 2.1 (the survey of decision-support visualization tools) can be skimmed as a reference list. Sections 3.1 and 5.1 are long design-rationale passages (scenario choice, stimulus ordering, crowdsourcing quality control) that only matter to someone replicating the work. The figures worth studying are Figure 1 (the gym stimuli, table and scatterplot, with and without decoy), Figure 7 (the bet stimuli, with the target, decoys, competitor, and distractors annotated), Figure 4 (point estimates and 95% CIs for Malkoc et al. and both experiments, the paper's key results figure), and Figure 8 (the contingency tables for matched tasks, compared with Wedell). Figure 3 (choice proportions) is the raw data behind Figure 4. Figure 2 (demographics) and Figure 5 (confidence and familiarity) can be skipped. Table 1 (Bob/Alice/Eve) is the quick intuition pump, and Table 2 (the A to F lottery tickets) matters only for replication. The reference list is mostly marketing and psychology literature on the attraction effect, which is useful if you want to follow that debate (Frederick et al., Simonson, Huber et al., Yang and Lynn, Wedell, Malkoc et al.).

**Memorable specifics:**

- Table 1: the Bob (education 5 stars, crime control 2), Alice (2, 5), and Eve (2, 4) election example. The paper cites O'Curry and Pitts for a similar study in which adding Eve shifted preference toward Alice.
- Terminology: decoy = asymmetrically dominated alternative; target = the alternative that dominates the decoy; competitor = the alternative that does not dominate the decoy; the set of non-dominated alternatives is the "Pareto front".
- Gym attribute values after incrementing by one: gC (2, 5), gV (5, 2), gC\* (1, 5), gV\* (5, 1), with (variety, cleanliness) order.
- Gym stimuli count: 4 table stimuli and 2 scatterplot stimuli per decision task, 18 in total.
- Gym participants: 305 valid, from 437 submitted jobs; 16% Red and 14% Orange; n = 54, 51, 50 (tables) and 47, 53, 50 (scatterplots); about 6 minutes, $0.60.
- Only one participant chose a decoy in the gym experiment (the text says "one participant out of 306", although the analyzed sample is stated as 305), versus decoy selection rates "as high as 13%" in Frederick et al.

- Gym Bayes factors (combined effect against a practically null effect of +/-1%): tables 34 times, scatterplots 150 times; against a repulsion effect: tables 11 times, scatterplots 66 times.

- Bet stimuli: 1 or 2 distractors per dominance region, then 10 to 20 decoys in the target's region, drawn from a half-normal distribution; overlap defined as distance under 0.025 in normalized coordinates (prize divided by 40).

- Post-hoc questionnaire: 41% "never" or "rarely", 46% "sometimes", 12% "often", none "always" when asked whether the uneven ticket distribution affected their choices; 86% of explanations for the uneven distribution were irrelevant or unintelligible.
- Quotable line: "traditional visualization design rules may not apply when the goal is to support decision making."
- Quotable line: the effects "may be small and hard to measure", about real datasets (in the limitations section).

**Caveats:** This is the HAL authors' version, not the typeset journal version, so page numbers, figure numbering, and wording may differ from the published paper. Figures were read through text extraction only, so I could not view the plots, and the figure descriptions above come from the captions and surrounding text. The Figure 3 percentages for the bet experiment came out of the extraction in a layout I could not reliably tie to specific bars, so I report bet results only from the body text and the Figure 8 contingency table. There is a small internal inconsistency: the text says 305 valid gym participants but also "one participant out of 306 chose a decoy." The paper reports differences as rounded percentages (for example, the text gives +20% for the decoy on variety on tables where the Figure 3 bars suggest about 21 points), so small mismatches between text and figure are rounding. The paper is hedged on its own limits: the datasets were synthetic with an extreme structure, part of the scatterplot effect may be perceptual, one planned analysis was dropped in each experiment, the expected-value knowledge data was lost to a bug, and the "inconclusive" real-dataset experiment is only cited from a separate report (Inria RR-8895), which I did not read. The paper has no debiasing experiment, so its design suggestions are untested proposals. The studies are from 2016, so any follow-up work on the attraction effect in visualization is not covered.
