# Ondov, Yang, Kay, Elmqvist & Franconeri, "Revealing Perceptual Proxies with Adversarial Examples" (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, vol. 27, no. 2, pp. 1073-1083, February 2021; presented at IEEE VIS 2020; DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2020.3030429)

**What it is:** An empirical, crowdsourced perception paper with a methodological bent. It asks which "perceptual proxies" (simple spatial heuristics the visual system might use in place of computing a statistic) people actually rely on when comparing two bar charts for the larger mean (MaxMean) or the larger range (MaxRange). Its central move is to build "adversarial" datasets, in which a candidate proxy points to the wrong answer, and to measure how large the true difference must get before viewers stop being fooled. It runs this two ways: a theory-driven experiment that tests four hand-picked proxies per task, and a data-driven experiment that tries to evolve deceptive charts from random data using participants' choices as the objective function. It sits directly downstream of Jardine et al.'s "The perceptual proxies of visual comparison" (a post hoc analysis of earlier data) and Ondov et al.'s "Face to face" (the titer and staircase method), and also builds on Yuan, Haroz & Franconeri's proxies for averages and Yang et al.'s feature work on correlation in scatterplots.

**AI summary:** The introduction frames vision as an information-processing system and asks what its "programs" are. A perceptual proxy is defined as "a heuristic shortcut for how the visual system extracts data from images using simple features, such as a shape's outline, center of mass, area, or color." The hypothesis is that the visual system relies on proxy computations across marks rather than computing statistics per se. The authors say existing work on proxies (correlation in scatterplots, comparisons in bar charts) is preliminary, in that we do not know how to measure which proxies are used, or how use varies across tasks and individuals. They address this for two tasks on pairs of bar charts. Experiment 1 optimizes datasets with simulated annealing so the correct chart (larger mean or range) is paired with an adversarial chart that would win if a given proxy were used. Experiment 2 randomly generates or perturbs datasets with identical statistics and lets participants' pairwise choices drive a black-box optimization that "evolves" potentially deceptive charts. Both used 65 Amazon Mechanical Turk participants per task. The headline results are that the most influential proxy for MaxMean was centroid and for MaxRange was slope, that individuals vary in which proxies they use and sometimes select against a proxy, and that Experiment 2's evolved charts show proxy-like motifs but could not be confirmed as consistently more deceptive than random data. Code, data and analysis scripts are said to be at an OSF link (https://osf.io/2re7b/).

The background section (Section 2) covers four areas. On visual comparison, the authors note that comparison is a "compound" task (Amar et al.) that has only recently been studied empirically, and they take their methodology (the "titer" measure) from Ondov et al. and their tasks from Jardine et al. On perceptual proxies, they describe the concept as relatively new in vision science and useful for visualization because knowing individuals' and populations' preferred proxies could suggest design guidelines, and because proxies can be turned into small programs or "bots" that model them. They note proxies may depend on the visualization type, design, data arrangement, and the viewer's traits and task. They trace proxies back to elementary perceptual processes (Cleveland and McGill), mention Rensink and Baldridge's Weber's-law work on correlation perception, Harrison et al. (as modified by Kay and Heer), and Yang et al., who identified 49 proxies ("visual features") and empirically derived the small subset people use for correlation. They criticize Jardine et al.'s data as not designed to be adversarial, so many proxies were highly correlated with each other and with the true answer (their Appendix B), which made proxy effects hard to separate. They contrast Yuan et al., who varied the number of bars across charts and found summed bar area (called "amount of ink" here) a likely proxy for averages, with their own design, which keeps the bar count equal. On adversarial visualization, they cite Wickham et al.'s graphical-inference lineups, Pandey et al.'s hand-built deceptive visualizations, Correll and Heer's "black hat visualization," and Correll et al.'s lineups with spikes, gaps and outliers, and they position their approach as fully automated with no prescribed data-quality errors.

Sections 3 and 4 give the overview and the dataset-generation machinery. The two experiments are theory-driven (in "proxy space") and data-driven (in "data space"). The dataset generation is similar in both, in that existing data is perturbed to create adversarial datasets, and the difference lies in how a new dataset is judged better or worse: by a model of a proxy (Experiment 1) or by asking participants (Experiment 2). Experiment 1's optimization is off-line, run before the experiment, and uses simulated annealing (inspired by Matejka and Fitzmaurice) to produce pairs of datasets with specified ratios for a proxy and a summary statistic. The cost function is the sum of squared differences between the objective ratios and those of the dataset under consideration. The adversarial series has a lower mean or range (the incorrect answer), and how much lower sets the trial's difficulty. Experiment 2's optimization is on-line. Human perception of the statistic is treated as a black-box function and optimized with Dueling Bandit Gradient Descent (DBGD), which estimates a gradient from pairwise rankings only. The search is in "bar space," seven dimensions for the seven bar lengths, serialized across epochs (one per participant) and parallelized across different random initializations. The algorithm details are said to be in Appendix A.

Section 5 gives the common method. The stimulus is a lineup of two horizontal bar charts side by side, each with seven bars of uniform color and thickness, in orange (#ff7f0e) and blue (#1f77b4). The tasks are MaxMean and MaxRange, both global in scope and taken from Amar and Stasko's low-level task taxonomy. Participants saw instructions and practice trials with feedback, and had to get three correct in a row to proceed. Each trial began with a countdown, then showed the lineup for a brief impression (1000 ms for MaxMean and 1500 ms for MaxRange, chosen from extensive piloting), then replaced it with two colored buttons that the participant clicked to name the chart with the larger mean or range. Sessions took 8 to 27 minutes (mean 15.24, SD 4.75). There were 65 MTurk participants per task, recruited separately for each task (MaxMean: 22 female, 42 male, 1 unspecified; MaxRange: 31 female, 34 male). They were limited to the United States because of IRB tax and compensation restrictions, screened for working English, allowed to participate once, and paid at a rate consistent with at least $10 per hour. A screen resolution of at least 1280 by 800 was required, the browser was put in full screen, and the software was JavaScript and D3.js with a Perl/CGI backend. Both experiments ran simultaneously with the same participants, with trials interleaved.

Experiment 1 (Section 6) tests specific proxies. The authors picked proxies using three heuristics: they preferred proxies that best aligned with participants' judgments in Jardine et al., eliminated those highly correlated with already-chosen ones so each could be modulated independently, and added any new proxy satisfying those constraints. The result was four proxies per task (Table 1). For MaxMean they are hull area (area of a convex hull around the bars, ignoring differences in bar thickness), centroid (centroid of the area occupied by the bars along the relevant x-axis), max bar (length of the longest bar), and min bar (length of the shortest bar). For MaxRange they are hull area norm (convex hull area cropped to the shortest bar), slope neighbor (the largest slope from the tip of one bar to the tip of an adjacent bar), slope range (the slope from the tip of the minimum bar to the tip of the maximum bar), and slope (the slope of a regression line fit to all the bars), where slopes are based on the horizontal slope. The authors say the chosen proxies stand for broader classes, for example hull area is highly correlated with the horizontal position of the hull's centroid, so evidence for one implies that proxy "or a similar proxy" is at play. The proxy correlations from Jardine et al.'s data are given in Appendix B.

The experiment also removes confounding proxies (Table 2). For MaxMean, ink area (the total number of colored pixels) would reduce to the sum, and thus the mean, if bar thickness were the same, so one randomly chosen chart per trial gets skinnier bars, with a fixed skinniness chosen so the skinny-bar chart always has the least ink even for a large mean difference. For MaxRange, min bar and max bar are tied to range, so the smaller range was made to span either the minimum or the maximum of the larger range, which makes min bar or max bar correspond to the larger range only 50% of the time. Whether the proxy was deceiving and whether the correct answer sat on the left or right were randomized and balanced. The hypotheses were H1 (adversarially manipulating proxies will mislead participants into worse comparisons) and H2 (individuals will be affected differently).

The key measure is the titer, defined as max(S_left, S_right) divided by min(S_left, S_right) for S the mean or range; it is at least 1, and a titer of 0.1 would mean one chart's statistic is 10% larger. The text and Equation 1 as extracted do not make the exact offset clear (the equation extracts as a ratio of at least 1 while the prose describes a titer of 0.1 as a 10% difference), so the headline numbers below are reported as the paper gives them rather than interpreted; 0.5 is described as a very large titer, hard for participants to discriminate (Appendix C, not in the PDF). The logic is that if participants need a large titer to select the correct answer under a proxy manipulation, they are likely deceived by it and probably use it. Titer thresholds are found by a staircase method starting at 0.25 for MaxMean and 0.40 for MaxRange: an error increases the titer (easier), a correct answer decreases it (harder). In the first four trials the step was 0.03 (MaxMean) or 0.06 (MaxRange) in both directions, and after that the decrement was 0.01 (MaxMean) or 0.02 (MaxRange). This is meant to align with a 75% just-noticeable difference. Design: within-subjects across four proxy conditions plus a control with no proxy manipulated (intended to replicate Jardine et al.), 20 trials per condition, and different participants for each task to avoid practice, fatigue and carryover. Analysis used 6,400 trials for MaxMean (20 trials x 5 conditions x 64 participants, one participant excluded for an assignment error) and 6,500 for MaxRange (65 participants). As a replication check, the mean titer over the last ten trials of the control was 0.19 [0.17, 0.21] for MaxMean versus 0.21 [0.19, 0.24] in Jardine et al., and 0.46 [0.41, 0.51] for MaxRange versus 0.32 [0.30, 0.33]. MaxRange thus appeared more difficult here, which the authors suggest may be because the control was mixed with adversarial trials and Experiment 2 trials.

The analysis is Bayesian, in two steps. Step 1 fits a Bayesian logistic regression for each participant and proxy on the 20 correct/incorrect responses, takes the titer at which the correct-response probability is 75% as the titer threshold, and derives a measurement error from the posterior using the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD). Step 2 is a robust Bayesian mixed-effects linear regression with a Student's t likelihood, titer threshold as a response with its measurement error propagated, proxy as a fixed effect and a random intercept and random proxy slopes per participant (titerThreshold|se(titerError) ~ proxy + (proxy|participant)), with weakly informative priors, using brms, ggdist, tidybayes, rstan and tidyverse. Results are medians with 50% and 95% quantile credible intervals.

For H1 (Fig. 4), the paper reports evidence that participants are likely deceived by some manipulated proxies. In MaxMean, all four proxies had posteriors surrounding and similar to the control; weak evidence suggests centroid might lead to a larger average titer threshold, while hull area, max bar and min bar are less likely to have a large effect on average. The figure annotation for min bar says an average participant needs a titer of about 0.29 (the digits in the extracted figure text are partly garbled, so treat this value as unconfirmed) to select the larger mean 75% of the time. In MaxRange, the authors found no strong evidence of an effect for hull area norm or slope neighbor, though slope neighbor is the most likely to lead to larger thresholds, and found slope and slope range likely to yield smaller thresholds than the control. One figure annotation says that under slope manipulation there is a 95% chance an average participant's threshold lies between 0.27 and 0.39. The authors call a smaller-than-control threshold "selecting against" a proxy. The logic (Table 3): if participants use some other proxy Y that is negatively correlated with slope (such as slope neighbor), then the chart with the larger slope but smaller range has the smaller Y, so Y users pick the correct chart at a smaller titer and the slope condition looks easier than the control. The smaller thresholds therefore suggest participants use some other proxy negatively correlated with slope and slope range.

For H2 (Fig. 5), individual differences are reported as each participant's predicted median threshold, with the caveat that no measurement error is assumed. In MaxMean most participants are self-consistent across conditions (those with larger thresholds in the control tend to have larger thresholds elsewhere) and cluster near the overall median, with a few having larger thresholds. Most seem deceived by the adversarial trials, except in min bar, where participants consistently and slightly select against the manipulation. A handful appear to follow no manipulation, different participants are deceived by different proxies to different extents, most participants are deceived most by centroid but centroid is also where behavior differs most, and participants are most similar within and across min bar and max bar, which the authors read as consistent with using max bar making one less likely to use min bar. In MaxRange participants are self-consistent but less so than in MaxMean, are most similar to each other in slope neighbor and least similar in hull area norm, and most select against both slope and slope range. Different participants may ignore one manipulated proxy, be deceived by a second, and select against a third. The paper concludes that the evidence supports H2.

Experiment 2 (Section 7) searches for adversarial data. It has two hypotheses: H3 (optimized charts will show identifiable characteristics corresponding to the proposed proxies) and H4 (optimized charts will be adversarial, appearing to have larger summary statistics than random charts with the same statistics). In its trials the two charts have an identical summary statistic, so there is no correct answer (titer 0 in the paper's framing), and participants experienced these as simply very difficult trials interleaved with Experiment 1 trials. The same task-integral confounds (ink area, min bar and max bar) were controlled and balanced. For each task, eight participants started from random initializations, and each of the subsequent 35 (MaxMean) and 34 (MaxRange) participants built on a previous result by adding an epoch of optimization, forming threads of up to 5 epochs. From these 43 (MaxMean) and 42 (MaxRange) results, 20 per task were chosen for evaluation, selecting the participants who performed best in Experiment 1 (lowest final control titer). The remaining 21 (MaxMean) and 23 (MaxRange) participants were shown the final chart of each of these 20 against random charts, seeing each once and only once. The analysis focused on four charts per task optimized across 5 epochs and validated by other participants, called M1 to M4 and R1 to R4. The authors computed the Experiment 1 proxies on these charts and compared them to a random-guessing simulation (the same algorithm as initialization, run 1,000 times with 100 guessing trials, simulating 20 trials per participant for 5 participants), using median and MAD, and also computed the rate at which each final chart was chosen in validation trials.

The Experiment 2 results (Fig. 6) are suggestive but not confirmatory. For MaxMean, M1 and M2 were at least one MAD from the simulation median for centroid and hull area, and half that for max bar. In validation, none of the final charts was selected higher than chance (50%), though the figure lists win percentages for the four charts (the extracted figure text gives these as M1 52.4%, M2 38.1%, M3 52.4%, M4 61.9%, which is a reading of garbled figure text and should be checked against the figure; the paper's prose says none beat chance, which is not consistent with 52.4% and 61.9% at face value, so the exact claim should be confirmed in the PDF). In M1 and M2 the bars were pushed toward the extrema, which the authors conjecture makes the larger bars carry more weight and raises the perceived mean; M3 and M4 show staircase patterns that might suggest a slope-related proxy. For MaxRange, R3 and R4 seem to suggest slope range, but R4 is about two MADs from the median of hull area norm in the negative direction, and R1 seems to suggest against most proposed proxies. In validation R1 and R4 were well above chance and the very similar R2 and R3 slightly below (the figure text lists R1 69.6%, R2 47.8%, R3 47.8%, R4 69.6%, again from garbled text), a discrepancy the authors attribute possibly to individual differences. R3 and R4 show an inverse motif, with the maximum flanked by the minimum or bars close to it, which the authors expect to correspond with slope range and slope neighbor. R1 and R2 show "notches" where the shortest bar is flanked by bars near the maximum, which the authors conjecture makes range easier to estimate rather than larger, making it "more attractive in a forced-choice task."

The discussion (Section 8) says both experiments point to centroid as a proxy for MaxMean, and they connect the two as the same problem approached from different directions. On implications, the authors suggest that a visualization precisely designed for one task may mislead "simply by virtue of the data," a corollary to Anscombe's quartet and related to Correll and Heer's black hat visualization and computer-security framing. They add that skewing perception this way was "quite difficult" in practice and likely subtle if successful, so a malicious designer would have easier paths. In the short term, establishing preferred proxies for individuals and populations may pinpoint situations in which data configurations lead to incorrect perceptions; in the longer term proxies might become building blocks of frameworks that assess any visual representation and dataset and report the data loss for different subsets of the population. They also offer the methodological framework (crowdsourced staircase titration plus simulated-annealing dataset generation plus a reactive, on-line optimization loop) as a contribution. The limitations: the list of proxies is not exhaustive, there are no strong motivations for testing these particular proxies, proxies directly tied to the summary statistic or highly correlated with chosen ones were omitted because they are hard to control independently, and the data-driven approach ran relatively few iterations with only rudimentary analyses. The conclusion says the combined experiments show "interesting, if preliminary" signs that proxy theories can be observed in unstructured perceptual response data, but "we cannot support the overall claim that the optimized datasets actually are adversarial," though several did better than chance. The authors argue that continuing to explore the "cube" of visualization, task and arrangement point-by-point would be inefficient and that it is unlikely the visual system has specialized programs for every representation, so future work should look for proxies that generalize across visualizations and tasks, and for individuals' affinities for particular proxies.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The perceptual-proxy idea is a clean, teachable framing for why charts work or mislead: the visual system seems to use cheap spatial features (centroid, convex hull, slopes between bar tips) rather than computing a statistic, so a chart can be "correct" yet pull the eye toward a wrong answer.
- In this study's bar-chart setting, centroid was the best-supported proxy for judging the larger mean, and slope-type proxies mattered for range, but the evidence is modest, and for several proxies the posterior was close to the control.
- Individuals differ. Some participants are deceived by a proxy, some ignore it, and some behave as if they "select against" it. A single population-level proxy account therefore describes an average that few people necessarily match.
- The adversarial-example method is a reusable idea for evaluating designs: instead of asking whether a design works on typical data, construct data where a hypothesized heuristic and the true answer disagree, and measure how much real difference is needed to overcome the heuristic.
- The "selecting against a proxy" argument (Table 3) is a good example of how correlated proxies muddy interpretation; reading it carefully teaches why controlling confounds (ink area, min bar, max bar) matters in perception experiments.
- The paper is candid that deceiving viewers was "quite difficult" in practice, which tempers any claim that bar-chart judgments are easily manipulated by data alone.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The definition: "A perceptual proxy is a heuristic shortcut for how the visual system extracts data from images using simple features, such as a shape's outline, center of mass, area, or color."
- Tasks: MaxMean (which chart has the larger mean) and MaxRange (which chart has the larger range), on pairs of seven-bar horizontal bar charts in orange (#ff7f0e) and blue (#1f77b4), shown for 1000 ms (MaxMean) or 1500 ms (MaxRange).
- Proxies tested, MaxMean: hull area, centroid, max bar, min bar. MaxRange: hull area norm, slope neighbor, slope range, slope. Confounds neutralized: ink area (via randomly skinnier bars on one chart) for MaxMean, and min bar and max bar (via the smaller range spanning the min or max of the larger) for MaxRange.
- Sample: 65 MTurk participants per task (different people for each task), 20 trials per condition, 5 conditions (4 proxies plus control), so 6,400 MaxMean trials (one participant excluded) and 6,500 MaxRange trials.
- Control replication: MaxMean 0.19 [0.17, 0.21] versus Jardine et al.'s 0.21 [0.19, 0.24]; MaxRange 0.46 [0.41, 0.51] versus 0.32 [0.30, 0.33].
- Staircase start values 0.25 (MaxMean) and 0.40 (MaxRange); step sizes 0.03 and 0.06 for the first four trials, with decrements of 0.01 and 0.02 afterward; aimed at a 75% threshold.
- Experiment 2 design: 8 participants per task from random initialization, threads of up to 5 epochs, 20 charts per task validated by 21 (MaxMean) or 23 (MaxRange) further participants against random charts.
- Quotable: "we cannot support the overall claim that the optimized datasets actually are adversarial."
- Quotable: "in practice, this is quite difficult, and likely to be subtle if successful. A malicious designer would thus have many paths of lower resistance."
- Named motifs from Experiment 2: bars "pushed toward the extrema" (M1, M2), staircase patterns (M3, M4), the maximum flanked by the minimum or by bars close to it (R3, R4), and "notches" where the shortest bar is flanked by bars near the maximum (R1, R2).
- Figure 1 shows four real trial examples with annotations such as "The blue chart has a larger mean, but max bar suggests the orange chart."
- The paper's phrase for the method family: "theory-driven" (off-line simulated annealing, in proxy space) versus "data-driven" (on-line Dueling Bandit Gradient Descent, in data space).
