# Munzner, "A Nested Model for Visualization Design and Validation" (IEEE TVCG, 15(6), 2009)

**What it is:** A conceptual framework paper (no empirical study, no new technique) by a single author that proposes splitting visualization design into four nested levels (domain problem and data characterization, operation and data type abstraction, visual encoding and interaction design, algorithm design) and attaches to each level its own characteristic "threat to validity" and its own set of appropriate validation methods. The paper's stated impetus was dissatisfaction with flat lists of evaluation methods; it positions itself against earlier pipeline-style visualization models (which were not coupled to evaluation) and against evaluation literature that explains how to run methods but not when to choose them. Its secondary contribution is three recommendations for authors and venues. The PDF is the 8-page TVCG version (manuscript received 31 March 2009, accepted 27 July 2009); its Figure 1 extracts poorly as text (see Caveats).

**AI summary:** The introduction argues that existing visualization models guide creation and analysis of systems but are not tightly coupled to evaluation, while the evaluation literature mostly enumerates methods without advice on when to use which. The paper presents four levels: characterize the tasks and data in the vocabulary of the problem domain; abstract into operations and data types; design visual encoding and interaction techniques; and create algorithms to execute those techniques efficiently. The author conjectures that many designers did carry out these steps implicitly and not necessarily in order, and wants to make them explicit so the model can be used either to analyze existing systems or papers or to guide design. The main contribution is guidance on which validation methodology suits each level. Threats are summarized in four short slogans, with "they" as users and "you" as designer: wrong problem, wrong abstraction, wrong encoding/interaction, wrong algorithm.

Section 2 describes the model itself (Figure 1). The levels are nested: the output of the level above is the input to the level below, so an upstream error cascades to every downstream level. A poor abstraction means that even perfect encoding and algorithm design will not solve the intended problem. The three inner levels are each design problems, though a different problem at each level. Section 2.1 untangles vocabulary: "task" is overloaded in the literature, and the paper uses "problem" for a task described in domain terms, "operation" for an abstract task, and "task" only when talking about both. Section 2.2 covers the domain level: the designer must learn the target users' tasks and data, in the domain's own vocabulary and workflow. The paper warns that designers often cut corners by assuming rather than engaging users, that introspection and interviews alone are insufficient, and that the output should be detailed (it contrasts "cure disease" with "investigate microarray data showing gene expression levels and the network of gene interactions"). Section 2.3 covers abstraction: mapping domain problems and data into generic operations (citing several task taxonomies such as Amar and Stasko, Amar, Eagan and Stasko, Wehrend and Lewis, Valiati et al.) and into data types (tables of quantitative, ordered or categorical columns; node-link graphs and trees; fields of values in space), possibly by transforming the raw data into a derived type. This level is described as often the hardest to get right and frequently done implicitly; the example is early web visualization work that assumed a picture of hyperlink topology would cure "lost in hyperspace" when people do not need that mental representation. Section 2.4 treats encoding and interaction together because they are mutually interdependent, and notes that interaction theory is less developed than encoding theory. Section 2.5 notes that algorithm design is not unique to visualization and is covered extensively by computer science.

Section 3 turns the levels into threats and validations (summarized in Figure 2). The author borrows "threats to validity" from computer security by way of software engineering, and prefers "validation" over "evaluation" to stress that every level needs it and that it extends beyond user studies to complexity analysis and benchmark timings; the usage covers both validation and verification in the software-engineering sense. A key distinction is between immediate validation (can be done at that level) and downstream validation (requires results from the levels nested inside). Most validation of outer levels is downstream, so a poor result may be caused by a poor choice at an inner level (a bad encoding casts doubt on a legitimate abstraction; a slow algorithm casts doubt on an interaction technique). Section 3.2 says the four stages are rarely carried out in strict temporal sequence; the value of separating them is that each can be analyzed separately, and rapid prototyping (paper prototypes, wizard-of-oz) can move downstream validation earlier. Per level: domain threats (problem mischaracterized) are validated immediately by observing and interviewing target users (ethnographic field studies, semi-structured interviews; Isenberg et al.'s "grounded evaluation"), and downstream by adoption rates, which the paper calls a weak signal with false negatives and false positives. Abstraction threats (chosen operations and data types do not solve the characterized problem) require testing by target users doing their own work, either through anecdotal evidence from a target user trying the tool or through longer-term field studies of the deployed system; the paper laments how rare field studies are and conjectures the reason is the two levels of downstream dependency. Encoding and interaction threats (design does not communicate the abstraction) are validated immediately by justification against perceptual and cognitive principles (heuristic evaluation, expert review, free-form discussion), and downstream by laboratory studies measuring time, errors and preference, by qualitative discussion of result images or video (strongest when it explicitly points out the desirable properties), or by quantitative measurement of result images (graph-drawing aesthetic criteria such as edge crossings). Informal usability studies appear in Figure 2 but are deliberately not called validation, following Andrews, and are placed there because they should precede a lab or field study. Algorithm threats (too slow or too memory-hungry, or incorrect) are validated by complexity analysis (immediate) and wall-clock and memory measurement (downstream); correctness is implicitly shown by result images. Section 3.7 names a common weakness, a mismatch between the level of the claim and the level of the validation, for example validating a new encoding with algorithm timings, or addressing a mischaracterized task with a lab study whose tasks were dictated by the study designers.

Section 4 analyzes six previous works through the model, each with a small validation-methods figure. Genealogical graphs (McGuffin and Balakrishnan) is called an exemplary paper covering all four levels, shining at abstraction (the data is not really a "family tree"). MatrixExplorer (Henry and Fekete) addresses all four levels with validation at three. Flow maps (Phan et al.) is heavily algorithmic but also covers abstraction and encoding, with domain characterization only implicit. LiveRAC (McLachlan et al.) uses four validation methods including a longitudinal field study, and the paper notes it is a good example of the iterative process, since some "immediate" interviews took place after a prototype existed. LinLog (Noack) looks like an algorithms paper but the author argues its contribution is at the encoding level (the energy model is a visual encoding choice), with no algorithm-level contribution. Section 4.6 treats lab studies (Robertson et al. on animation, Heer et al. on horizon graphs) as validations of encoding and interaction choices.

Section 5 compares the model to others. It is influenced by pipeline models (the Abstraction level corresponds to the Data and Visualization Transformation stages and the Visual Encoding level to the Visual Mapping Transformation stage), but those models do not tie to evaluation. The paper also relates its levels to formative, summative and exploratory evaluation, and argues, extending Ellis and Dix's point that lab studies are often used formatively, that expert reviews and heuristic evaluations can be used summatively as immediate validation of encoding choices, though stopping there would be dangerous. It credits Andrews and Ellis and Dix as closest in spirit, and distinguishes itself from Shneiderman and Plaisant's MILCs by drawing clearer lines between levels, and from Andrews and Ellis and Dix by including algorithm-level threats.

Section 6 gives three recommendations. First, authors with contributions at multiple levels should distinguish them, so that readers can see what is an open problem, a partial solution, or a closed problem (the TopoLayout paper is the example of murky entanglement between encoding and algorithm levels). Second, authors should state upstream assumptions, which can be very brief, and the abstraction level is the one most often neglected. Third, visualization venues should encourage domain problem characterization papers, since the paper knows of only one in visualization venues and argues the level is difficult and time-consuming and should not be assumed trivial.

Section 7 discusses limits. The model applies to technique-driven as well as problem-driven work; it deliberately oversimplifies; it assumes complex sensemaking can be broken into low-level components, which is "unlikely to be completely true"; it can be combined with van Wijk's requirements-then-solutions process; it omits how to carry out validation methods and omits psychophysical-style studies; it is cast in infovis terms, and adapting to scivis would take significant work (spatial position is typically intrinsic to the data there). The author concedes the list of threats and validations is not exhaustive and that others might split or merge levels, for instance asking whether domain characterization and abstraction should be one level. The conclusion offers the model to spur discussion of the interplay between design and evaluation.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The central idea is a way of locating where a design decision lives. The four levels (domain problem, abstraction, encoding/interaction, algorithm) let you ask what a given system or paper is really claiming and at which level.
- Nesting means errors cascade downward: no amount of encoding polish rescues a wrong problem or a wrong abstraction. This is a strong argument for spending time on the upper levels.
- Each level has its own threat, and a validation method only counts against the threat at its own level. A lab study with designer-specified tasks cannot show that you solved the right problem; benchmark timings cannot validate an encoding.
- The immediate versus downstream distinction explains why outer-level validation is hard and why a poor test result can blame the wrong level.
- The abstraction step (domain tasks and data into generic operations and data types) is highlighted as the hardest and most often skipped; the genealogical-graphs example (a "family tree" is not really a tree) and the "lost in hyperspace" example make it concrete.
- Practical habit: state your upstream assumptions and say which level your contribution sits at. This is a useful lens for critiquing visualization papers and for writing about one's own work.
- The paper deliberately oversimplifies, treats the levels as analytically separable even though real design is iterative, and says so; read it as a lens, not a procedure.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read Section 1, Section 2 (especially 2.1 vocabulary, 2.2 and 2.3), Section 3 (at least 3.1 to 3.3 and 3.7), and the recommendations in Section 6; these carry the contribution. Figure 1 (the four nested layers) and Figure 2 (threats and validation methods with red dependency lines) are the two figures that matter; study Figure 2 closely, since it is the paper's compact summary. Section 4 (six worked analyses) can be skimmed: read one or two (genealogical graphs, and LinLog for the "an algorithms paper is really an encoding paper" move) and skip the rest unless you know those systems. Figures 3 to 7 are each a small per-paper validation-methods diagram and add little beyond the text. Section 5 (comparison to other models, including the formative/summative/exploratory discussion) is mainly for readers placing the work in the evaluation literature. Section 7 is short and worth reading for its candid limitations. The reference list (about 53 entries, pages 7 and 8) can be skipped.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The four threats in slogan form: "wrong problem: they don’t do that;" "wrong abstraction: you’re showing them the wrong thing;" "wrong encoding/interaction: the way you show it doesn’t work;" "wrong algorithm: your code is too slow."
- The cascade claim: "an upstream error inevitably cascades to all downstream levels."
- Vocabulary definition: "In this paper we use the word problem to denote a task described in domain terms, and operation to denote an abstract task."
- On abstraction: "This abstraction stage is often the hardest to get right."
- Domain-level detail example: "cure disease" is not detailed enough, whereas "investigate microarray data showing gene expression levels and the network of gene interactions" is appropriate (and the paper notes even that is a drastic paraphrase of the full design study).
- The "lost in hyperspace" example: early web visualization papers implicitly assumed showing the topological structure of the hyperlink graph would help, but "people do not need an internal mental representation of this extremely complex structure to find a web page of interest."
- Figure 2's red lines show how many levels must be addressed before a given validation can be done (the longer the line, the further downstream the dependency).
- Adoption rate is described as "a weak signal with a large rate of false negatives and some false positives."
- Informal usability studies are shown in Figure 2 but are "specifically not called a validation method," quoting Andrews that formative methods "lead to better and more usable systems, but neither offer validation of an approach nor provide evidence of the superiority of an approach for a particular context."
- Worked-example claims: genealogical graphs is "This exemplary paper explicitly covers all four levels"; LinLog's energy model is, in the model's vocabulary, "a visual encoding design choice"; LinLog also uses mathematical proof, which the author classifies as quantitative image analysis.
- The model's own limitation: it "errs on the side of oversimplifying the situation," and the assumption that complex sensemaking can be broken down into low-level components "is unlikely to be completely true."
- The three recommendations: distinguish between levels, state upstream assumptions, and encourage problem characterization papers at visualization venues; the author knows of only one such paper in visualization venues (Tory and Staub-French).
- Concluding claim: the model "classifies validation methodologies for use at only one of four separate levels."

This is the 2009 journal paper, so its vocabulary is the paper's own ("visual encoding and interaction technique" as one level, "domain problem characterization," "data/operation abstraction"); the PDF alone does not say how later work revised this terminology, and none is asserted here. The paper explicitly scopes itself to information visualization rather than scientific visualization, offers an admittedly non-exhaustive list of threats and validations, and does not explain how to carry out any of the validation methods. Its examples and the infovis literature it compares against are of 2009 and earlier.
