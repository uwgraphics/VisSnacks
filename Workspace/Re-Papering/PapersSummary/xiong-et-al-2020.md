# Xiong, Van Weelden & Franconeri, "The Curse of Knowledge in Visual Data Communication" (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, vol. 26, no. 10, pp. 3051–3062, 2020; DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2019.2917689)

**What it is:** An empirical, human-subjects paper from the perception/cognition side of visualization research. It reports four lab experiments (three on a line graph, one on a stacked bar graph) testing whether the psychological "curse of knowledge" (an informed person cannot simulate what an uninformed person perceives) extends to what looks visually salient in a chart. The claim is that once a viewer has background knowledge making one pattern in a chart pop out for them, they predict that naive viewers will find the same pattern most salient, even when told explicitly to ignore what they know. The paper positions itself, in its own words, as the first examination of the curse of expertise in data visualization, and as joining the recent line of work on cognitive biases in visualization (attraction effect, anchoring, priming, narrative framing, memorability, embellishment). It ends with speculative, untested mitigation advice for presenters and designers.

**AI summary:** The introduction opens with experts (a scientist at a colloquium, an analyst presenting a dashboard) who overwhelm audiences with overly complex visualizations "oblivious to the fact that others do not see what they see." It reviews the curse of knowledge as a general phenomenon: informed decision makers failing to predict the judgments of less-informed ones, people who know an ambiguous sentence's intended reading assuming it is unambiguous to others, sarcasm assumed to be heard by everyone, and the "tapping" study in which tappers of well-known songs estimated that listeners would identify about 50% of them while listeners identified about 3%. The consequence for communication is that people do not convey information they assume is already shared. The authors argue that a graph, like a paragraph, can be read in many ways depending on what a viewer selects and interprets, so a presenter fixated on one pattern may miscommunicate. Section 2 (related work) covers children's failure on the Sally-Ann false-belief task, a variant showing adults err too, and hindsight bias as a within-person version of the curse. It then notes visualization work on decision biases (attraction effect), memorability and embellishment, storytelling and framing, and the influence of previously seen visualizations.

Section 3 gives the shared method. Participants completed a Qualtrics survey. They first read a backstory about political events affecting polling data. They were told the experimenters would show the same graph to 100 other people, with only this description: "in the months before the elections of 2014 in a small European country, a polling organization asked citizens about their voting intentions on a daily basis." They then wrote down what they predicted uninformed viewers would find most visually salient, second most salient, up to a fifth. Participants were deliberately given no definition of "features or patterns" and no examples, and "saliency" was defined as "the most noticeable and important feature." For each prediction they circled the region on a paper copy of the unannotated graph, rated how salient each predicted feature was to themselves (1 = not at all, 5 = very), and finally matched their five predictions to five pre-determined features (A to E) or marked N/A. The design is within-subject. The independent variable is whether a feature was primed (highlighted in the story the participant read) or not, the dependent variable is the salience rank given to features, and a second dependent measure is the self-rated salience. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three stories to counterbalance which features were primed. The three hypotheses were that participants identify different features as salient, that they predict their story's highlighted features to be more salient to uninformed viewers, and that features predicted as most or least salient for others are also most or least salient for themselves.

Experiment 1a (Section 4) used 18 introductory-psychology students (10 women). The stimulus was a line graph of four parties' polling over time (Labour, Conservatives, Alliance, United). Each story described a two-party competition: one party has a lead, loses it to the less popular party after an initial debate, and regains it after a later debate and an opponent's bad performance. The three versions ascribed the story to the top two parties (Top-Prime, features A and B, the two near-intersections of the top two lines), the top and third party (Middle-Prime, feature E, the vertical mirror-image of the two green lines, Labour and Alliance), or the bottom two parties (Bottom-Prime, features C and D, the two intersections of the bottom two lines). In Experiment 1a participants saw a graph annotated to emphasize the primed features after the story, but made their predictions on an unannotated graph (Figure 4) that they were told was all uninformed viewers would see. The authors tried to balance baseline salience by darkening the bottom two lines, adding two intersections to them, aligning the green lines' change points to strengthen the mirror pattern, and adjusting appearance across several pilots (for example making a peak less sharp). Only 48% of participants' free-form predictions matched one of the five pre-determined features (56% for their top three), so the statistics cover only matched predictions, and the free drawings are shown qualitatively (Figure 5). Matched features were ranked, with ties getting the best rank, N/A predictions occupying a rank slot, and unranked features receiving rank 6 (a saliency prediction of 0); Figure 6 reverse-codes this into a 0 to 5 "saliency prediction." Qualitatively, participants' drawings tracked their story: those who read the top story marked the top two lines more, those who read the middle story marked global, mirroring, paired features spanning a larger area, and the top and bottom stories yielded local features. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test found primed features (Wilcoxon mean score 59.77, rank mean 2.63) ranked significantly higher than non-primed features (38.37, rank mean 0.87), Z = 4.03, p < 0.01. Per-story means in Figure 6 show, for example, the Top-Prime group giving the top features M = 2.92 versus 1.50 for non-primed. Some participants' individual lines show no priming effect, which the authors say may indicate immunity but cannot be separated from noise in this design. A Spearman correlation between a feature's self-rated salience and its predicted salience for others was moderately strong (rs = 0.55, p < 0.001; Figure 7). The authors note that Experiment 1a's qualitative and quantitative analyses were done post hoc, which motivated the follow-ups.

Experiment 1b (Section 5) removed the visual annotation of primed features, leaving only the story, with otherwise identical procedure and analysis. Twenty-nine students (23 women) took part, and matching rates rose to 66% (78% for the top three). The primed-versus-not-primed difference remained significant but weaker: primed Wilcoxon mean score 83.15 (rank mean 2.48) versus 67.98 (rank mean 1.79), Z = 2.13, p = 0.035. The Spearman association fell to rs = 0.31, p < 0.01. The authors note the middle-prime effect weakened from 1a to 1b and speculate that the spatially separated mirror feature needs annotation to be seen, so that annotation matters more for spatially separated features. They conclude that story alone suffices to bias predictions, and that the effect roughly halved without annotations, so both story and annotation contribute.

Experiment 1c (Section 6) is a conceptual replication with new participants (21 students, 10 women) and a changed instruction: participants were told to describe only features involving two or more parties, to reduce uninterpretable responses (one participant circled the whole graph, another the y-axis). Matching rose to 64% (83% for the top three, which the paper reports as a 16% increase over 1a for the overall rate and 27% over 1a for the top three). Primed features were again ranked significantly higher (Wilcoxon mean score 65.93, rank mean 3.26, versus 46.54, rank mean 1.80; Z = 3.17, p < 0.01), with rs = 0.43, p < 0.001. The authors note overall ratings of both primed and non-primed features went up, the effect size dropped from 1a to 1c though not robustly, and suggest the two-party instruction may have made unprimed features more likely to be matched, shrinking the gap. Comparing the "Everyone" rows across Figures 6, 9, and 11 shows similar overall salience for top and bottom features and slightly lower salience for the middle feature, again attributed to its spatial separation. Participants who read the middle story still rated that less-salient feature as most salient to both themselves and others. Figure 13 summarizes the three line experiments: Experiment 1a, 18 participants, Z = 4.03, effect size r = 0.95, primed saliency 2.75, not-primed 0.81, difference 1.94; 1b, 29 participants, Z = 2.13, r = 0.40, 2.48 versus 1.79, difference 0.69; 1c, 21 participants, Z = 3.17, r = 0.69, 3.29 versus 1.62, difference 1.67.

Experiment 2 (Sections 7 and 8) tests generalization with a different chart type and stories. Seventeen students (9 women) did the same within-subject procedure with a stacked bar graph (Figure 14): bars for four issues (education, defense, immigration, crime), segments for Liberal, Undecided, and Conservative voters, and bar length representing number of voters. Five pre-determined features were built so areas balance (for instance, the purple and orange areas in the top two bars are equal and total purple area equals total orange area). Three stories about a presidential election between Liberal and Conservative parties primed three features: a crime story about police brutality (feature A, the Liberal majority on crime), an immigration story about a bus-system terrorist attack (feature B, the Conservative majority on immigration), and an education story about a debate and a female expert third candidate (feature C, the large undecided share on education). Features D (equally undecided on defense, immigration, and crime) and E (defense balanced between Liberal and Conservative) were unprimed fillers. Matching was high, 82% (94% for the top two predictions). Qualitatively, a heat map of marked features (Figure 18) shows participants marking the features primed by their story. Quantitatively, primed features had a Wilcoxon score mean of 34.74 (rank mean 4.29) versus 21.63 (rank mean 3.38) for not-primed, Z = 3.09, p < 0.01, and the Spearman correlation between predicted and self-rated salience was strong (rs = 0.65, p < 0.001). The authors conclude the effect generalizes to bar graphs, other data, and other visual features.

Section 9 concludes that across four experiments and two graph types, participants are susceptible to the curse of knowledge when simulating what others see, despite explicit instructions to ignore their knowledge and take a naive perspective, and calls this the first empirical demonstration in visualization and "even in the broader realm of visual perception." Limitations (9.1): free-form drawings had to be categorized, creating a tension between open-ended response (realistic) and constrained response (analyzable); all participants were college students; wording was a struggle ("features," "trends," "patterns," "salience" each bias participants, so wording may matter); stimuli were balanced so no single pattern dominated, which is needed to isolate the effect but means baseline salience without a story prime was not tested; a single dominant pattern might mask the effect; and the experiment studied experts who did not create the visualization, so the authors predict, without testing, a stronger curse for designers. They also ask why some participants appear immune and whether the curse extends to memorability, context, or impact. Section 9.2 offers speculative mitigations that the authors say need empirical testing: view data from new angles (for example swapping rows and columns in a Tableau bar graph, or very different formats), use critique as a feedback loop (illustrated by a bus-schedule visualization in the style of Mondrian hung in a school cafeteria that many viewers took for artwork), and rely on the wisdom of the crowd by merging different people's percepts.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Knowing what a chart is "about" changes what you see in it, and you cannot easily turn that off to imagine a first-time viewer. A presenter's sense that "it's obvious" is unreliable evidence about what the audience will notice.
- This is a bias about predicting others, shown by priming: the authors told people a story that made one pattern salient and asked what uninformed viewers would notice. Their predictions followed the story, despite instructions to ignore it.
- The effect was found with both line and stacked bar graphs and with different stories, and was statistically significant in all four experiments, though it was weaker (about half) without visual annotation and varied in size across experiments.
- Annotation plus story primed more than story alone, which suggests that highlighting and annotating a chart makes the author even more confident the audience will see it. It also suggests that spatially separated features, such as the mirror-image pair of lines, may depend more on explicit visual cues.
- The paper's mitigation advice (re-arrange the chart to see it afresh, get critique from naive viewers, merge multiple people's readings) is explicitly speculative and untested, so it should be presented as hypotheses rather than findings.
- Design lesson in the stimuli: the authors balanced baseline salience across features (darkening lines, adding intersections, adding a salient female candidate) so that an effect of priming could be isolated. This is a useful model of controlling competing factors in a perception study.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The introduction and Section 2 are short and readable, and worth reading for the framing (the tapping study, the Sally-Ann task, hindsight bias). Section 3 (General Method) is essential for understanding what participants did. For Experiment 1a, read the story/graph description (4.2, 4.3), Figure 3 (the three stories and primed features A to E), Figure 4 (the unannotated graph), Figure 5 (qualitative free drawings, the most intuitive evidence), and Figure 6 (per-story rank detail). **Memorable specifics:**

- The tapping study as reported: tappers "estimated that listeners would identify around 50% of the songs, but in reality, listeners could only identify around 3%."
- The stimulus: a daily-polling line graph for a "small European country" before its 2014 elections, with parties Labour, Conservatives, Alliance, and United, and five pre-determined features (A and B: top lines' near-intersections; C and D: bottom lines' intersections; E: the vertical mirror-image of the Labour and Alliance lines).
- Participants were randomly assigned to one of three stories (Top-Prime, Middle-Prime, Bottom-Prime) and, despite instructions to ignore the story, predicted that others would find their story's features most salient.
- Sample sizes: 18 (1a), 29 (1b), 21 (1c), 17 (Experiment 2), all students; the participants' pool was an introductory psychology course (1b also offered money).
- Wilcoxon signed-rank results: 1a Z = 4.03, p < 0.01 (rank means 2.63 versus 0.87); 1b Z = 2.13, p = 0.035 (2.48 versus 1.79); 1c Z = 3.17, p < 0.01 (3.26 versus 1.80); bar graph Z = 3.09, p < 0.01 (4.29 versus 3.38).
- Spearman correlations between self-rated and predicted-for-others salience: 0.55 (1a), 0.31 (1b), 0.43 (1c), 0.65 (bar graph).
- Free-form matching rates to the five features: 48% (56% top three) in 1a; 66% (78%) in 1b; 64% (83%) in 1c; 82% (94% for top two) in the bar graph experiment.
- Effect sizes (r) in Figure 13: 0.95 (1a), 0.40 (1b), 0.69 (1c).
- Participants' unconstrained answers sometimes were uninterpretable: "one participant circled the entire graph as being visually salient, another circled the y-axis."
- The authors' stated conclusion: "the curse of knowledge also plagues the visual perception of data, explaining why people can fail to connect with audiences when they communicate patterns in data."
- The bar graph stories: crime (police brutality), immigration (a terrorist attack on the country's bus system), and education (a debate and a female expert third candidate).
- The bus-schedule anecdote: a visualization "in the style of Mondrian painting" hung in a school cafeteria, which "many viewers didn't realize ... was a bus schedule visualization at all, instead assuming that it was artwork."
- Stated mitigations: view data from new angles (for example "hitting the 'swap rows and columns' button" in Tableau), critique ("Critique is critical"), and "Rely on the wisdom of the crowd."

**Caveats:** The PDF supplied is an author's version with the byline stripped (it shows only the DOI and a "© 2020 IEEE" note, and the review-stage website link is "omitted for review"); the author list, volume, issue and pages in the H1 come from the Crossref record for the DOI, not from the document. All participants were psychology-course students, sample sizes were small (17 to 29 per experiment), and Experiment 1a's qualitative and quantitative analyses were described by the authors as post hoc, with 1b and 1c as follow-ups. Only part of the free-form responses (48% to 82%) matched the five pre-determined features and only those were analyzed statistically. The authors did not test baseline salience without a story, instructions that restrict the type of feature (1c) may have changed the effect, and the study examined experts who did not design the chart, not designers themselves. The prose has small slips that could mislead on a quick read (for example Section 4.4 refers to the "middle-prime version" twice where the bottom-prime version is meant; Section 6.3 says the effect "decreased by half"), and the mitigation section is speculation. Per-participant figure details (Figures 5, 6, 9, 11, 18, 19) were read only as extracted text, not as images.
