# Mackinlay, "Automating the Design of Graphical Presentations of Relational Information" (ACM TOG, 1986)

**What it is:** The paper that formalized visualization as **encoding**: graphical presentations are treated as *sentences of graphical languages*, judged by **expressiveness** and **effectiveness** criteria, and generated automatically by a prototype system, **APT (A Presentation Tool)**.

**AI summary:** Mackinlay's goal is an application-independent **presentation tool** that automatically designs effective 2-D static presentations (bar charts, scatter plots, connected graphs) of relational data, so application builders needn't be graphic-design experts. Two problems follow: codifying graphic design knowledge, and generating enough design variety to cover arbitrary inputs (he estimates over **17,000** possible design problems for just two binary relations, over 21 billion for four). His fundamental move is linguistic: a chart is a sentence with precise syntax (arrangements of graphical objects) and semantics (an *Encodes* relation between graphical properties and facts).

**Expressiveness** is a two-part criterion: a set of facts is expressible in a graphical language if some sentence "(1) encodes **all the facts** in the set, (2) encodes **only the facts** in the set." Both halves get worked examples: a single position axis provably *cannot* express a one-to-many relation (a mark can't be in two places), and a bar chart of cars' *nation* — a nominal attribute — fails the "only" condition because bar lengths inevitably encode an ordering that isn't in the data ("as if the USA cars were longer or better"). **Effectiveness** he treats as explicitly conjectural, since "there does not yet exist an empirically verified theory of human perceptual capabilities." Focusing on accuracy of interpretation, he builds on **Cleveland and McGill's** ranking of perceptual tasks for quantitative data (position most accurate, down through length, angle, slope, area, volume, to color/density) and — the paper's most-reused contribution — **extends it to ordinal and nominal data** with separate rankings per data type: position tops all three columns, but color, near the bottom for quantitative data, "is a very effective way of encoding nominal sets." He flags plainly that this extension "has not been empirically verified."

The generative machinery is a **composition algebra**: a basis set of primitive graphical languages built from **Bertin's** vocabulary (marks — points, lines, areas — with positional and **retinal** properties: color, shape, size, saturation, texture, orientation), combined by three operators — **double-axes**, **single-axis**, and **mark composition** — under the **Principle of Composition** ("Compose two designs by merging parts that encode the same information"). Ties among expressive designs are broken by the **Principle of Importance Ordering**: "Encode more important information more effectively." APT implements all this as generate-and-test search in a logic program (~200 rules, 1–2 minutes per design on a Symbolics LISP machine), with backtracking, and is sensitive to the output medium — given color, it produces a scatterplot with color-coded repair records; restricted to monochrome, it reasons its way to an aligned bar chart instead.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The two criteria, as a pair of design questions you can ask of any chart: does it show *all and only* the data (**expressiveness**)? and does it use the most accurately-read channels for the most important data (**effectiveness**)?
- Expressiveness comes *first* — effectiveness only ranks designs that already express the data correctly. The nominal-bar-chart example is the canonical illustration of a chart that lies by construction.
- The effectiveness ranking *depends on data type* — one ranking per quantitative/ordinal/nominal, not one universal list. This is the table that echoes through Munzner's textbook and beyond.
- Why this paper is in Tableau's ancestry: encode-by-rules plus rankings is exactly what automatic chart pickers (Show Me, and every "recommended chart" feature since) operationalize.
- Charts aren't primitives; they're *compositions* of encodings — a scatterplot is two position encodings; a chart with sized, colored points is a mark composition. This is the "charts as encodings" idea in 1986 form.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the abstract, Section 3 (the price/mileage example — scatterplot vs. labeled scatterplot vs. aligned bar charts sets up all three concerns), Section 4, the *prose and examples* of Sections 5–6 (the two expressiveness conditions, the Nation bar chart, Figures 14–15 and the area analysis in Figure 16), and Section 9 (media sensitivity — a satisfying trace of the whole system reasoning). Skim or skip the formal machinery: the predicate-logic definitions, Theorem 1's proof, the formal composition-operator definitions (7.2), and the implementation details (Section 8). The ideas survive intact without the notation.

**Caveats:** Mackinlay himself labels the effectiveness theory "conjectural" and the ordinal/nominal extension unverified — treating Fig. 15 as settled perceptual law is the classic over-claim (and precisely what Bertini pushes back on). Scope is narrow: 2-D, static, accuracy-only ("dealing with multiple, perhaps conflicting, effectiveness criteria is beyond the scope"); no interaction, animation, 3-D, or aesthetics/engagement. The "all and only the facts" standard is stricter than real practice (annotation, redundant encoding, and embellishment all technically violate it). And the AI framing (logic programming, generate-and-test) is period technology — the *criteria* are the durable contribution, not the search engine.
