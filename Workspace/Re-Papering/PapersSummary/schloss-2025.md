# Karen B. Schloss, "Perceptual and Cognitive Foundations of Information Visualization" (Annual Review of Vision Science, vol. 11, 303–330, 2025)

DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-vision-110323-110009> (CC BY 4.0).

**What it is:** An invited survey (review) article from a vision-science venue, not an empirical study and not a design framework. It reviews research on how observers interpret visualizations, organized around three kinds of processing drawn from a designer-to-observer communication model: perceiving (graphical perception), mapping (decoding what visual features mean, with color as the main worked case), and comprehending (the takeaway messages and conclusions observers construct). Its central claim is that observers do not passively receive information but actively construct interpretations from the visual input plus their heuristics, biases, knowledge, and beliefs, so informationally equivalent designs differ in how interpretable they are. It is written through the lens of constructivism, and it deliberately focuses on the observer side and on simple, controlled, low-level-task studies. The author's own work (the color inference framework, semantic discriminability) features prominently, and the acknowledgments thank Michael Gleicher among others.

**AI summary:** The introduction defines information visualization as a representational system in which a designer maps information from a represented world onto a graphical representing world, and an observer uses visual reasoning to infer properties of the represented world. Figure 1 presents this as a transmission system: the designer encodes, the observer decodes through perceiving, mapping, and comprehending. The paper stresses that infinitely many visualizations are informationally equivalent, but "not all informationally equivalent visualizations are equally interpretable" because of constraints on perceptual and cognitive processing; misalignment imposes processing costs that slow judgments and decisions. It notes that most reviewed studies use simple visualizations and low-level tasks (comparing, correlating, finding extrema), on the argument that these tasks still recruit a rich range of processing and that principles found there may scale with visualization and task complexity.

The Perceiving section covers graphical perception. It opens with Stone et al. (2014) and Szafir (2018) on how color discriminability falls as mark size shrinks (a practical issue when a figure is rescaled), with the model's effects smallest along L* (lightness), then a* and b*. The first theme is that visualizations are not one-size-fits-all. Cleveland & McGill's (1984) channel ranking (position best, then length, direction and angle, area, volume and curvature, and finally shading and saturation) has been replicated for the same kind of task, but rankings shift with the task. Albers et al. (2014) found position-based time-series displays better for point comparisons (max, min, range) and color-based displays better for summary judgments (average, spread, outlier amount). Arrangement matters too: stacked bar charts made comparing dataset means easier (Jardine et al. 2020), while overlaid charts made finding the most-different bar pairs easier (Ondov et al. 2019). The second theme is heuristic processing. Observers appear to use perceptual proxies, easily computable features that approximate an abstract quantity (for example, ensemble statistics over bar lengths as a proxy for the mean). Researchers define candidate proxies and test which best predict human judgments, which the paper calls more principled than testing every encoding, arrangement, and task combination. Correlation perception in scatterplots follows Weber's law across a variety of visualizations, with evidence that the proxy is the width of a probability density function over element locations (Rensink 2022). An open challenge is observers who need several tasks supported by different proxies (for example, differences versus overall magnitudes). The third theme is graphical illusions: extraneous features bias judgments (bar width biasing bar-height estimates, 3D depth, categorical repulsion from the scale midpoint, word length in word clouds, point lightness and size when estimating scatterplot means, high-variability line segments when estimating line-graph means). The paper frames these as potentially informative in the way visual illusions are. It also covers cases where extraneous information does not interfere: Gleicher et al. (2013) found mean estimates in multiclass scatterplots unaffected by redundant encoding, irrelevant shape variation, or extra classes, explained by feature selectivity, while Boger et al. (2021) show attention to one color can lead observers to miss a dinosaur formed by another, and Tseng et al. (2023) found performance drops beyond the three classes studied earlier.

The Mapping section is about decoding the encoded mapping. It first treats perceptual organization of verbal labels using Wickens & Carswell's Proximity Compatibility Principle: perceptual proximity (Gestalt grouping, common region, connectedness) should be compatible with task or mental proximity. Direct labeling beat a separate legend for line charts (Milroy & Poulton 1979), and legends whose order matched the order of the data were faster than mismatched ones (Huestegge & Philipp 2011), with the congruency effect specific to complex charts. Default legend orders in plotting software (alphabetical or data-entry order) are named as a source of mismatch. The section then turns to visual semantics, using color as the example. Observers have expectations about what features mean even when a legend is present: Lin et al. (2013) found bar charts faster to read when bar colors matched the objects represented (blue for blueberries, yellow for bananas). The author's color inference framework distinguishes color–concept associations from color meaning. In the recycling-bin example (Schloss et al. 2018), participants associated trash more with white than purple, yet when shown a purple and a white bin they inferred purple meant trash. The explanation is assignment inference: observers compare the total merit of the possible assignments (purple–trash plus white–paper versus white–trash plus purple–paper) computed over the activated part of a color–concept association network. The framework predicts individual, cultural, and temporal differences that should be predictable if the network is well specified. Semantic discriminability (how well colors can be told apart by meaning) has metrics for two and for n colors and concepts, and semantic discriminability theory holds that capacity depends on how different the concepts' association distributions are (grape and eggplant low; sleeping and driving higher). This challenges the view that colors must be strongly associated with concepts to encode them; any concept can be meaningfully encoded if the colors are semantically discriminable. Designers can use these metrics, tools such as Colorgorical and Palettelint in Color Buddy are cited as routes to applying them, and designers should keep an encoding consistent across plots. The section then covers relational associations, especially for colormaps: structure preservation, the dark-is-more bias (shown by Schloss et al. 2019 with a legend and alien-sightings task, and persisting on dark backgrounds when colors do not appear to vary in opacity), opaque-is-more, saturated-is-more, hotspot-is-more, and high-is-more. These obey an applicability principle (a bias activates only if applicable to the display) and a combination principle (active associations combine, cooperate, or conflict). Schoenlein et al. (2023a) found direct associations dominated the dark-is-more bias under strong conflict, but a weighted combination predicted inferred mappings best. Whole-visualization effects, such as the affective connotation of light, saturated palettes versus dark, desaturated ones, are noted briefly.

The Comprehending section covers the messages observers take away. Perceptual grouping steers comparisons: Shah et al. (1999) found observers missed trends over time when year varied between groups, but caught them when year varied within groups (by proximity in bars or connectedness in lines). Fygenson et al. (2024) had participants match sentences to charts and found they chose charts whose grouping put the compared items in one group. Boger & Franconeri (2024) found improbable relationships (such as a child shrinking from age 10 to 12) were less often reported when they occurred between groups, which they attribute to capacity limits and slow, sequential comparison, while Bearfield et al. (2024) report observers may start with between-group comparisons of aggregates before drilling down. The paper notes that grouping can be used benevolently or to obscure patterns. On uncertainty, the hurricane cone of uncertainty is misread as risk of damage rather than trajectory uncertainty, and the assumption that risk is low outside the cone; ensembles (hurricane path ensembles, animated risk trajectories, ensembles of COVID-19 forecasts, hypothetical outcome plots) support more accurate interpretations than cones, confidence bands, or error bars. On embellishment and annotation, the paper moves from Tufte's critical-ink view to evidence that strategic additions help: Ajani et al. (2022) found a "decluttered+focused" version (reduced extraneous elements plus semantically rich color and text highlighting) best for recall and reporting the intended message and rated most appealing, while embellishments can hurt children's math learning and have little effect on adults' insights in other studies. Stokes et al. (2023b), using Lundgard & Satyanarayan's four semantic levels (L1 elements and encodings, L2 statistical or relational comparisons, L3 overall patterns, L4 contextual insights), found observers mostly report L3 and rarely L1, report L2 and L4 far more often when annotated, and report each level more when the text is placed near what it describes (L3 text works better in the title). Finally, prior knowledge and biases: a brief meteorology lesson improved wind-direction inference and shifted gaze to relevant map regions (Canham & Hegarty 2010), and salience mattered only after knowledge was acquired (Hegarty et al. 2010). Beliefs bias correlation estimates in scatterplots (Xiong et al. 2023), and the curse of knowledge (Xiong et al. 2020) leads to the "as you can see" fallacy in presentations. The author states that no visualization design is objective, including those produced by machine designers, that designers are responsible for helping observers find key insights, and that observers should read critically; interactive approaches (deleting less relevant data points) may protect against cognitive bias better than visual highlighting (Dimara et al. 2019).

The conclusion names five directions: models that abstract across visualization types, extension from low-level tasks to complex visualizations and interactive displays, culturally aware research, accessibility for people with disabilities or medical conditions, and links to other modalities (sonification, tactilization, olfaction). It suggests such principles could be built into freely accessible web-based design tools (for example Colorgorical). The Summary Points and Future Issues boxes restate these.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- A clean vocabulary for the observer's job: perceiving, mapping, comprehending. It is a useful way to sort why a chart fails: the viewer cannot see the difference, cannot work out what features mean, or draws the wrong conclusion.
- "Informationally equivalent" does not mean "interpretively equivalent." The choice of encoding or arrangement has to be matched to the task, and there is no single best chart for a dataset. The Albers and the stacked-versus-overlaid results are the concrete evidence.
- Perceptual proxies offer a mechanism-level explanation for why tasks favor particular designs, and a more generalizable route than a lookup table of chart type by task.
- Color meaning is inferred, not just read from a legend: association strength is not the same as meaning (the purple-means-trash example), semantic discriminability matters more than strong association, and legend order and direct labeling affect speed. These are directly usable design checks.
- Dark-is-more and related biases are expectations that a legend can contradict at a cost, even for experts in fields where light-is-more is conventional (the paper flags neuroimaging as an example).
- Grouping and annotation shape what readers take away, and the same tools can persuade or obscure. Annotation placement near the thing described matters.
- Uncertainty displays: ensembles and outcome plots outperform cones and error bars in the studies cited.
- The "as you can see" fallacy and the curse of knowledge are a useful warning for anyone presenting charts.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The article is about 20 pages of text plus a long reference list (pages 303–323 are the review, summary boxes, and future issues; the remainder is literature cited). It reads as a survey, so it can be read straight through, but the Mapping section's color material is the most specialized and can be skimmed by readers uninterested in color semantics; the Perceiving section's Weber's-law and proxy passages are the densest. The Comprehending section is the most immediately usable. Figure 2 (Albers and Jardine task-dependence examples) and Figure 6 (grouping and annotation studies) carry the clearest design lessons; Figure 4 is the way into the assignment-inference idea, and Figure 5 illustrates the dark-is-more bias. The Summary Points box (item list on page 323) is a good one-minute digest. The reference list can be skipped except as a source of citations.

**Memorable specifics:**

- Figure 1: the designer-to-observer communication loop with three interpretive processes (perceiving, mapping, comprehending), illustrated with a choropleth of 2018 per capita CO2 emissions from domestic aviation.
- Quotable line: "These results emphasize that informationally equivalent visualizations are not interpretively equivalent—different processing mechanisms may be involved depending on the visual channel."
- Albers et al. (2014), Figure 2a: position-based displays better for point comparisons, color-based (colorfield) displays better for summary judgments, "opposite of the ranking in Cleveland & McGill 1984."
- Figure 2b: the same bar data stacked or overlaid; stacked helps comparing means (Jardine et al. 2020), overlaid helps finding the most-different pairs (Ondov et al. 2019).
- Perceptual proxies are defined as "easily computable features that provide a reasonably accurate estimate without direct calculation of the (often abstract) quantity at hand."
- Correlation perception follows Weber's law: the JND for correlation "increases linearly with distance from a perfect correlation (r = 1 or −1)."
- Graphical illusions: bar width biases bar-height estimates (Ceja et al. 2021); larger and darker points get more weight in scatterplot mean estimates (Hong et al. 2022); in word clouds, font-size percepts are biased by word length (Alexander et al. 2018).
- Boger et al. (2021): attending to purple points in a scatterplot caused observers to not notice that green points formed an image of a dinosaur.
- Figure 3: direct labeling versus legend (Milroy & Poulton 1979) and congruent versus incongruent legend order (Huestegge & Philipp 2011).
- Figure 4: Lin et al. (2013) congruent and incongruent fruit palettes; the recycling example in which purple bin means trash although white is more strongly associated with trash; assignment inference illustrated with merit totals of 1.1 (T–Pu/P–W: 0.1 + 1.0) versus 0.5 (T–W/P–Pu: 0.2 + 0.3).
- Semantic discriminability theory examples: grape and eggplant have relatively low capacity; sleeping and driving have higher capacity.
- Figure 5: alien animal sightings by time of day (Schloss et al. 2019) and ocean-water colormaps (Schoenlein et al. 2023a) showing dark-is-more combining or conflicting with direct associations. Named biases: dark-is-more, opaque-is-more, saturated-is-more, hotspot-is-more, high-is-more.
- Figure 6: Shah et al. (1999) year varying across versus within groups; Fygenson et al. (2024) charts of one group of 6, two groups of 3, three groups of 2 matched to sentences; Stokes et al. (2023b) annotations at L1–L4 on a median-household-income line chart, nearer text drawing more takeaways.
- Hurricane cone: observers "misinterpret larger width as meaning greater risk of damage rather than greater uncertainty" and assume risk is low outside the cone's edges (Ruginski et al. 2016).
- Hypothetical outcome plots (Hullman et al. 2015) cited as an alternative to error bars; the paper also cites professional designers' aversion to showing uncertainty (Hullman 2020).
- Ajani et al. (2022): three versions of a chart (cluttered, decluttered, decluttered+focused); the last gave best recall and was rated most appealing, professional, and clear.
- Xiong et al. (2020): the curse of knowledge, which the paper links to the "as you can see" fallacy.
- Quotable line: "No visualization design is objective because any visualization is subject to the design decisions and potential biases of the designer, regardless of whether it is a human designer or a machine-automated designer that was created by humans or was trained on visualizations produced by humans."

**Caveats:** The text was read with `pdftotext -layout`; the multi-column figure text came out interleaved and partly garbled (axis labels, legends, and caption fragments), so figure descriptions above rest on the captions and the body text, not on viewing the figures. The Annual Reviews download banner was ignored. Page numbering in the PDF (303–330 in print; 28 pages in the file) was not cross-checked page by page; the claim that the review proper ends at page 323 comes from the Summary Points and Future Issues boxes appearing there. Scope limits that the paper itself states: observer-side only, mostly simple visualizations and low-level tasks, and color as the main worked example for semantics, with extension to other features described as ongoing research. The paper also lists open gaps (cultural differences, accessibility, whether dark-is-more varies with domain expertise or visualization literacy, trade-offs among proxies for different tasks). Several findings are single-study results presented in a review without the original effect sizes, which the review does not report. The review's cited studies reach 2025, but it is a selective rather than exhaustive survey, and heavy emphasis on the author's own framework should be kept in mind.
