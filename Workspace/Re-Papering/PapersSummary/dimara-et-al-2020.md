# Dimara, Franconeri, Plaisant, Bezerianos & Dragicevic, "A Task-based Taxonomy of Cognitive Biases for Information Visualization" (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, vol. 26, no. 2, 2020, pp. 1413-1432)

**What it is:** A literature-survey and taxonomy paper (not an empirical study) that collects 154 cognitive biases from the psychology and decision-making literature and sorts them into seven categories according to the experimental task in which each bias was observed, rather than by the psychological explanation of why it occurs. Its claim is that a task-based organization is more usable for visualization researchers than the explanatory taxonomies that dominate the bias literature, and that very little of this territory has been tested in visualization. It sits between cognitive psychology (Kahneman and Tversky and successors) and visualization evaluation and decision-support work, and it is framed as a map of the problem space, a source of hypotheses, and a guide for future studies. The PDF summarized here is the HAL deposit of the authors' version (headed "AUTHORS' VERSION - SEPTEMBER 2018", 22 pages including a HAL cover page); the HAL citation line gives the published reference as TVCG 2020, 26 (2), pp. 1413-1432.

**AI summary:** The introduction argues that visualization designers must consider the limits of computers, displays, and humans, and that for humans this means both vision and reasoning; the paper focuses on the reasoning side, where judgment and decision making rely on "approximations, heuristics, and rules of thumb" whose imperfections show up as cognitive biases. The authors note growing interest in the visualization community (two IEEE VIS workshops on cognitive biases), but say empirical work is limited: most visualization evaluations do not include decision tasks, those that do tend to assume decision making is rational, studies that confirm or disprove a particular bias in visualization are rare, and most bias experiments in psychology use text and very small datasets or no data at all. The goal is to lay out the problem space, facilitate hypothesis generation, and guide future studies that will help designers "anticipate – and possibly alleviate – limitations in human judgment."

Section 2 supplies background. It defines cognitive biases against normative models of rationality such as expected utility theory, then uses Kahneman and Tversky's violations of those norms as the origin of the concept. Two canonical examples are given: the framing effect (a program described as "33% chance of saving a life" is preferred over one described as "66% chance of death" although they are identical) and confirmation bias. The paper adopts Pohl's five criteria: a bias reliably deviates from reality, occurs systematically, occurs involuntarily, is difficult or impossible to avoid, and appears rather distinct from the normal course of information processing. People subject to a bias are unaware of it and it often persists even when they are informed and trained. Section 2.2 describes the difficulty of the concept: unlike a visual illusion, where reality is physically defined, "reality" for a cognitive bias rests on normative models that are not universally accepted and can change, and the cost of an "incorrect" decision is hard to assess. The authors note a long controversy between those who hold that biases are real and consequential and those who hold that heuristics that err in experiments can be effective in the real world, and conclude "The answer likely lies in-between." Section 2.3 reviews existing taxonomies. Explanatory ones (Tversky and Kahneman's representativeness and availability heuristics, Baron's 53 biases, dual-process system 1/system 2 groupings including Padilla et al.'s extension to visualization) organize biases by why they occur, are often not exhaustive, and rest on mechanisms that remain contested. Decision-support taxonomies (Remus and Kottemann 1986, about 20 biases; Arnott 2006, 37 biases), Heuer's intelligence-analysis biases, and Pohl's memory/thinking/judgment classes consider high-level tasks but do not group biases by lower-level task. In visualization, the authors found no comprehensive review; Zuk and Carpendale organize uncertainty-related biases by how design could mitigate them, and Ellis and Dix briefly discuss seven biases. Most existing classifications cover only 10-40 biases, whereas a Wikipedia page lists 176.

Section 3 describes the method in three stages: gather a list, find a representative experimental paper per bias, and card-sort by task. The starting list was the Wikipedia "List of Cognitive Biases" (retrieved 20 November 2017; 176 entries), chosen because it was the longest list found, with the stated caveat that it is not curated by researchers. For each bias, Step 1 searched Google Scholar for the bias name plus "information visualization" and collected visualization papers mentioning it; Step 2 (if no eligible source resulted) searched the Wikipedia pages and then Google Scholar for the bias name plus "experiment", taking the first eligible paper by decreasing citations on the first results page; Step 3 removed any bias for which no source could be found (21 biases from the Wikipedia list). A source was eligible if it was peer reviewed, accessible, and either reported a human-subject study of the bias or cited one and described its task in detail. Experiment reliability (design, statistics, effect size) was explicitly not examined. After this process the paper says 151 of the 176 remained, noting that 13 Wikipedia entries were duplicates, and then three biases found in the literature were added (the ballot names bias, the phantom effect, and the compromise effect), giving 154. Two borderline perceptual items were deliberately kept: the Weber-Fechner law (illustrated by a fifty-dollar upgrade seeming cheap on a 20,000-dollar car but large on a 300-dollar phone) and pareidolia. Biases that appear similar were kept separate if researchers treat them as different.

For categorization, the authors identified the task participants performed in each representative experiment and gave it a short label (one coder labeled, two others reviewed until all three agreed), then grouped similar tasks into seven categories by open card sorting (one person proposed, four co-authors revised). They deliberately did not base the categories on existing visualization task taxonomies, to avoid constraining them to visualization, and they contrast the result with those taxonomies later. The categories are ESTIMATION, DECISION, HYPOTHESIS ASSESSMENT, CAUSAL ATTRIBUTION, RECALL, OPINION REPORTING, and OTHER. Because categories are large, the authors added subcategories called "flavors" that capture other similarities: Association, Baseline, Inertia, Outcome, and Self perspective. They say plainly that flavors were developed intuitively and are not a primary contribution. Figure 1 shows all 154 biases as dots grouped by task; Figure 2 redistributes them by flavor.

Section 4 gives the taxonomy (Table 2 lists every bias with its flavor, a representative reference, a "relevance to InfoVis" code, and a one-line description) and then reviews each category with a psychology subsection, a visualization subsection, and research opportunities. The "relevance to InfoVis" column uses an eight-level code (Table 1): from #1 "Relevance to visualization currently unclear", through #2 "Potentially relevant", #3 "Probably relevant", #4 "Not discussed in visualization but likely relevant", #5 "Discussed in visualization research as important, but not yet studied", #6 "Studied in visualization, but no clear evidence of existence or alleviation", #7 "Evidence for the existence of the cognitive bias in visualization", up to #8 "Evidence for the alleviation of the cognitive bias in visualization". The gray-shade relevance ratings (#1-#4) are, by the authors' own description, "a subjective rating only meant to help the reader get started."

ESTIMATION (33 biases) covers tasks where people assess a quantity. The psychology discussion distinguishes probability estimates with a calculable ground truth (base rate fallacy, conjunction fallacy, conservatism), those without ground truth judged by consistency with rationality (optimism bias; anchoring, shown by a fortune wheel influencing estimates of the number of African countries in the UN), frequency estimation (words starting with "R"), time prediction (planning fallacy), and confidence/performance estimation (overconfidence, with hard-easy and expertise effects). The authors also include Goldstein and Gigerenzer's finding that the recognition heuristic improved city-population comparisons for foreign cities, as a warning that heuristics do not necessarily lead to bias. In visualization, they say there is little work on cognitive-level estimation biases beyond Bayesian reasoning studies (Euler diagrams and frequency grids against the base rate fallacy, where visualizations did not show clear benefits; Micallef et al. conjecture that participants ignored the visualization and calculated from numerals in the text, and their last experiment suggests visualizations can help when numerals are omitted). Two studies examine anchoring in visualization (Valdez et al. on scatterplot class-separability judgments influenced by the first scatterplot; Cho et al. on a Twitter-data visual analytics system), and Xiong et al. offer preliminary evidence for the curse of knowledge in visualization communication. Position papers propose alleviation strategies for the planning fallacy and availability bias but no tool has been built or tested. The authors warn that self-reported confidence, a common evaluation metric, is itself subject to bias, and tentatively recommend minimizing the estimations users need and encoding quantities visually rather than with numerals that prompt miscalculation, while admitting there is too little data for strong guidelines.

DECISION (33 biases) covers tasks requiring selection among alternatives. Examples include uncertainty-related biases (ambiguity effect, zero-risk bias, loss aversion, framing), context effects (attraction effect, less-is-better effect, compromise effect, phantom effect, mere-exposure effect), and biases involving rewards or ownership (hyperbolic discounting, IKEA effect, endowment effect, status quo bias). In visualization, the authors mention a "multi-attribute choice task" definition and systems built for it, but say empirical studies of decision biases are very limited. Exceptions: Dimara et al. showed the attraction effect exists in scatterplots and that correctly visualized and understood data can still yield an irrational decision; later work showed visualizations can mitigate it by letting users remove irrelevant information; Zhang et al. found loss aversion among startups shown star-rating tables; the identifiable victim effect was studied in visualization, but Boy et al. found anthropomorphized icons did not increase empathy, which the authors say shows how hard it is to predict what happens when bias findings meet visualization designs. Wood et al. used visualization to find evidence of a bias, the effect of ballot-name position in 5000 candidates of the 2010 Greater London local elections.

HYPOTHESIS ASSESSMENT (11 biases) covers judging whether a statement is true or false; the central example is confirmation bias, with the illusory truth effect, congruence bias, illusory correlation, and the experimenter effect. The authors quote Nickerson on how pervasive it is. They link this category to confirmatory analysis tasks (Keim et al.; Amar and Stasko's confirm-hypotheses task) and say they know of no empirical study assessing these biases in visualization. They suggest a first step of confirming the biases occur with visualizations and discuss possible mitigation: analysis of competing hypotheses and evidence marshalling and the software that supports them, showing what data has already been examined and what has been ignored, imposing analytic workflows, and temporarily hiding labels to emulate a "blind test" (the example is replacing ethnicity values with labels A, B, and C when first reviewing income or crime data). They also note that researchers are themselves prone to these biases and recommend risky hypothesis tests and strategies against experimenter effects.

CAUSAL ATTRIBUTION (12 biases) covers explaining the causes of events or behaviors, mostly from social psychology: egocentric bias, self-serving bias, actor-observer bias, hostile attribution bias, ultimate attribution error, group attribution error. For visualization, the authors connect it to Amar and Stasko's formulate-cause-and-effect task, observe that any analytic task can involve causal attribution when users explain peaks or outliers, and say the area is "potentially ripe for research." They give two illustrations: the hormone replacement therapy case, where an apparent protective correlation was later attributed more likely to socio-economic differences in diet and exercise, and a GPS example in which a graph shows new-system drivers having more accidents (1 percent per year against 2 percent per year for older systems as the paper states it), although risk-taking drivers disproportionately adopted the new system and safety improved for both driver groups taken separately; they describe anecdotal evidence that dashboard users make unwarranted causal claims and call for empirical data.

RECALL (39 biases, the largest category) covers recalling or recognizing previous material (misinformation effect, picture superiority, Google effect, cryptomnesia, false memories). The authors argue that visualizations can decrease but not eliminate biased recall, since not all information can be displayed or processed and viewers still rely on a limited memory to link data to examples, context, and emotions. They suggest annotation systems that record observations and later organize automated comparisons of user preferences, note that memorable visualization properties (real-world images, distinctiveness) and linking data patterns to real-world objects (the "hockey stick") can improve memory, and suggest an automated system giving suggestions for data shape mnemonics.

OPINION REPORTING (21 biases) covers explicitly reporting beliefs on political, moral, or social issues (bandwagon effect, naive cynicism, third-person effect, stereotyping, moral luck). The authors distinguish it carefully from the other categories using the same example of "Americans are smart" (opinion reporting reports it; causal attribution explains with it; hypothesis assessment tests it; estimation predicts with it). They concede this category is "inherently linked to people's attitudes, moral beliefs and behavior" and "may be less useful to visualization researchers," while noting that connections to visualization remain unexplored. OTHER (5 biases) holds biases observed through behavior rather than responses (unit bias, ostrich effect, risk compensation), with speculative visualization relevance: the unit bias may affect judgments of quantity from white space around scatterplot points, the ostrich effect suggests systems could highlight information viewers would consider negative, and risk compensation may affect how thresholds are set.

Section 5 discusses benefits and limits. The authors argue a task-based organization assumes only that a task analysis has been done (standard practice), may reveal biases with the same root by showing similar tasks, preserves pointers to original experiments to help build evaluations using established methods, and is "by far the largest in the literature." They acknowledge that their categories do not align with visualization task taxonomies (look-up, explore, identify, compare), which tend to be lower-level building blocks of the higher-level tasks here, though some overlap (hypothesis assessment, cause and effect). They state all categories are highly relevant: researchers on multi-attribute choice should start with DECISION, memorability work with RECALL, confirmatory analysis with HYPOTHESIS ASSESSMENT, and uncertainty visualization with ESTIMATION. Because few visualization studies exist, there are many research opportunities: pick a bias, test whether it persists with standard visualizations, then test improved designs. Methodological advice from earlier work includes using a continuous error metric instead of "correct/incorrect," including conditions without numerals, and presenting the same information in all conditions so improvement can be attributed to the visualization. The limitations section admits that more biases will be found, that only a subset is discussed in the text (chosen for importance to visualization, establishment in psychology, and clear rationality violation), that each bias got a single category even though the same bias may appear across task types (the attraction effect in product choices versus visual judgments of rectangles or circle-and-line pairs, which the authors prefer to treat as perceptual), that initial coding was done by one person (mitigated by review from all five authors), that the representative source was the first eligible one rather than an exhaustive search, and that "a cognitive bias assumes by definition a 'deviation from reality'" that is complex and controversial, so visualization researchers should verify that erroneous responses reflect irrationality and not an optimal strategy under another interpretation. The conclusion repeats that only one empirical study alleviating a cognitive bias with visualization design was found, and very few even show that biases exist in visualizations.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Cognitive biases are a different kind of problem from perceptual illusions: the "ground truth" is a normative model of rationality, which is contestable. The paper models the caution to take when calling a response "wrong" and asks researchers to check that errors reflect real irrationality and not a sensible alternative interpretation of the task.
- The central organizing idea is to classify biases by the task in which they were observed (estimate, decide, assess a hypothesis, attribute cause, recall, report an opinion), so that a designer who knows what users do with a visualization can look up which biases might matter. That is a practical way to enter a very large literature.
- Visualization does not automatically cure bias. The attraction effect persisted in correctly read scatterplots, and Bayesian-reasoning visualizations often gave no clear benefit; the paper argues numbers shown next to visualizations can prompt miscalculation. It also gives a counterexample to assuming visual "empathy" tricks work (anthropomorphized icons did not raise empathy).
- Evaluation itself is exposed to bias: self-reported confidence, a common visualization-evaluation metric, is subject to bias, and researchers can be subject to confirmation bias and experimenter effects.
- The paper's own scorecard of the field is stark: most of the 154 biases are marked as not yet discussed or not yet studied in visualization, only one alleviation study was found, and the authors present that as open research space.
- Design ideas offered as hypotheses (not findings): show what data has been examined and what has been ignored, hide or recode labels for "blind" first looks, provide annotation and comparison support for memory, highlight negative information, and reduce the estimations a viewer must make.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read the Introduction and Section 2.1-2.2 (what a bias is and why the concept is contested) for framing, and Section 3 for the method if you want to judge how far to trust the list. Section 2.3 (existing taxonomies) can be skimmed unless the explanatory-versus-task-based contrast matters. Section 4 is a reference: read the opening of each category and the visualization subsections of ESTIMATION, DECISION, and HYPOTHESIS ASSESSMENT, which hold the most concrete visualization findings, and treat OPINION REPORTING and OTHER as skippable. Section 5 (discussion, limitations) is short and worth reading in full. Figure 1 (page 1, dots grouped by task) and Figure 2 (flavors) give an at-a-glance sense of category sizes. Table 1 (the 1-8 relevance scale) is needed to read Table 2, which is the paper's real deliverable: a lookup table of all 154 biases, each with a flavor, a representative reference, a visualization-relevance code, and a short description, and is best used as a reference to search. The long reference list (several hundred entries, on the pages after Section 6) is where the original experiments live. Table 3 (synonym names for some biases) is referred to in the text; it is reference material.

**Memorable specifics:**

- Taxonomy size and structure: "a task-based taxonomy of 154 cognitive biases organized in 7 main categories" (Abstract). Category counts as stated in Section 4: Estimation 33, Decision 33, Hypothesis Assessment 11, Causal Attribution 12, Recall 39, Opinion Reporting 21, Other 5 (these sum to 154).
- The five flavors: Association, Baseline, Inertia, Outcome, Self perspective.
- Starting list: the Wikipedia list of 176 cognitive biases, retrieved 20 November 2017; 21 removed for lack of an eligible source, 13 duplicates noted, 3 added (ballot names bias, phantom effect, compromise effect).
- Pohl's five criteria for a cognitive bias, as numbered in Section 2.1 (reliably deviates from reality; occurs systematically; occurs involuntarily; is difficult or impossible to avoid; appears rather distinct from the normal course of information processing).
- The framing example in Section 2.1: "33% chance of saving a life" preferred over "66% chance of death" even though the two programs are the same. The Linda example illustrates the conjunction fallacy and the representativeness heuristic.
- The relevance code in Table 1 runs from #1 "Relevance to visualization currently unclear" to #8 "Evidence for the alleviation of the cognitive bias in visualization". Only the attraction effect is coded #8 among the Decision rows visible in Table 2 (with references [6], [91]); anchoring, conservatism, curse of knowledge, and loss aversion (and mere-exposure) are coded #7 among the first Estimation and Decision rows.
- Quote on confirmation bias (Nickerson, as cited): the bias "appears to be sufficiently strong and pervasive that one is led to wonder whether the bias, by itself, might account for a significant fraction of the disputes, altercations, and misunderstandings that occur among individuals, groups, and nations."
- The ballot study: Wood et al. analyzed 5000 candidates in the Greater London local elections of 6 May 2010 and showed that candidate-name position on the ballot influenced votes; an alphabetical tabular representation can lead to biased election results.
- The GPS example: a graph showing new-GPS users with more accidents (1 percent per year) than users of older systems (2 percent per year), as the paper writes it, in which the confound is that mostly risk-taking drivers adopted the new system.
- The "blind test" idea: show labels A, B, and C instead of ethnicity values on first review of income or crime data.
- Explicit definition of the scope: "Our taxonomy focuses on the tasks where biases occur, instead of why they occur - as previously done."
- Closing finding: "we only found one empirical study [91] that alleviates a cognitive bias using visualization designs."
- Methods advice for experiments (Section 5.3): use a continuous error metric rather than "correct/incorrect"; include conditions with no numerals; present the same information in all conditions.

**Caveats:** This is the HAL authors' version (September 2018), not the typeset journal version, so page numbers and any late wording may differ from the published TVCG 2020 article. The core content is the taxonomy table (Table 2), which the paper itself describes as a starting point: one representative paper per bias, chosen as the first eligible source rather than by exhaustive search, with the reliability of the experiments not examined, and each bias placed in a single category. The flavors were assigned intuitively, the 1-4 relevance ratings are subjective, and the starting list (Wikipedia, 2017) is not research-curated. The count reconciliation in Section 3.3 is stated in the text as 151 of 176 remaining after source selection plus 3 additions; the text mentions the 21 removals and 13 duplicates separately and I did not verify that these figures reconcile arithmetically. The text-extracted PDF was read in full for the body (Sections 1-6) and the start of Table 2 (through bias #66 in the Decision category); I did not read the rest of Table 2 (hypothesis assessment through "other", biases #67-154), Table 3, or the reference list in detail, and Figures 1 and 2 were read only through their captions, since the dot layouts do not survive text extraction. The empirical claims about visualization (attraction effect, anchoring, and so on) are reported as the paper presents them and are dated to 2018; the field has presumably moved since then, but the PDF cannot say how.
