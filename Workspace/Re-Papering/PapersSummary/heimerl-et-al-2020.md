# Heimerl, Chang, Sarikaya & Gleicher, "Visual Designs for Binned Aggregation of Multi-Class Scatterplots" (arXiv preprint, arXiv:1810.02445v2 [cs.HC], 14 Jan 2020)

**What it is:** A design-space and task-taxonomy paper (Michael Gleicher's group at UW–Madison) about how to visualize large, multi-class 2D point sets by binning them into a regular lattice rather than plotting every point. It compiles the space of existing and newly-extended visual designs for showing per-bin class/frequency summaries, derives a set of abstract analysis tasks that binned scatterplots need to support, and connects the two with design guidelines (a task-by-design suitability table) plus a web-based tool for trying out design combinations on real data. This copy is the arXiv preprint (v2, dated 14 Jan 2020); a footnote in the text ("to be published with camera ready version of this submission") indicates the paper had been accepted somewhere by the time of this revision, but no journal/conference name appears anywhere in the PDF's front matter, body, or footnotes, so it's cited here as the arXiv preprint.

**AI summary:** The motivating problem is overplotting: a scatterplot showing many points with a categorical class attribute (color- or shape-coded) becomes illegible once point density gets high, because points occlude each other and class membership becomes impossible to read. Binned aggregation addresses this by turning the scatterplot into a two-step abstraction pipeline: first, a *density field* is computed by dividing the 2D plane into a regular lattice of bins and counting per-class frequencies in each bin (the paper deliberately restricts itself to regular, binned aggregation rather than continuous methods like kernel density estimation, citing binning's computational efficiency and the perceptual advantages of regular grids for comparison); second, that abstract per-bin scalar field is transformed into a visual representation. The paper's contribution is to survey and extend the menu of choices for that second step, and to tie those choices back to a systematic list of tasks so that a designer can pick a defensible combination rather than guessing.

The task list (Table 1, §4) is derived from an earlier scatterplot task taxonomy by Sarikaya & Gleicher, which had grouped tasks into object-centric, browsing, and aggregate-level categories. Because binning abstracts away individual data items, the authors argue object-centric tasks (which target single points) no longer apply, leaving browsing tasks (explore neighborhood, search for known motif, explore data) and aggregate-level tasks (characterize distribution, identify anomalies, identify correlation, numerosity comparison, understand distances) — eight base tasks in total. They then add a second dimension specific to multi-class binned data: *scope*, i.e. whether a task is targeted at bins (bin-centric) or at classes (class-centric). Crossing the eight base tasks by this bin/class scope produces the paper's 16 numbered tasks (e.g., task 1 "explore properties of bins in a neighborhood" vs. task 2 "explore properties of classes in a neighborhood"; task 13 "compare density in different regions of the space" vs. task 14 "compare class density in different regions of the space"). This task list is grounded concretely rather than left abstract: §2 and §4 walk through three example datasets. An NBA shooting-position dataset (Figure 1) tracks four point guards (guards 1–4, color-coded blue/orange/green/red) to illustrate how a traditional overplotted scatterplot (Fig. 1a) obscures shot-distribution differences that a hexagon-binned design with grayscale density plus per-bin pie charts (Fig. 1b) and a per-player juxtaposed color-weaving design (Fig. 1c) both recover — the paper walks through this example bin by bin, e.g. noting that guard "green" attacks unusually from the left side of the court. The Early Modern Drama Collection (Figure 2) plots 1,242 dramas from 1576–1700 across two topic-model dimensions, categorized into nine genres (tragedy, tragicomedy, comedy, history, masque, interlude, entertainment, dialogue, non-dramatic), and is used to contrast a raw scatterplot (2a) against color-weaving (2b), rectangular color-blended bins (2c), and a class-frequency small-multiple design (2d) — showing, for instance, that "history" is the one genre whose density center sits in the middle of the plot with heavy overlap onto every other class. The Colorado Tree Coverage dataset (Figure 3, from the UCI repository, roughly 600,000 trees across seven species) plots elevation against horizontal distance to water and is used to show correlation- and threshold-style findings a binned design surfaces easily — e.g. ponderosa pines essentially never grow above 2,800m, krummholz doesn't appear until about 3,300m, and cottonwood/willow, douglas fir, and ponderosa pine all need water within about 700m while the other species are far less water-dependent.

Section 5 is the heart of the paper: the design space itself (summarized visually in Figure 4). It splits into "representing bins" (§5.1) and "composition/distribution" for classes (§5.2–5.3). Bin representation has three sub-choices. Shape: only triangles, rectangles, and hexagons tile a 2D plane without gaps; the paper recommends against triangles (citing Scott's finding that triangulating the space produces higher expected per-point position error than the alternatives), notes rectangles give the best contrast along orthogonal/diagonal neighbors and are the only shape with constant interval along both axes (useful when reading precise interval values matters), and notes hexagons have the least expected distortion of density counts and are best for bin-centric neighborhood/gradient tasks (tasks 1, 2, 9, 10, 11, 12). Size: homogeneous vs. heterogeneous bin sizing trades off spatial fidelity against reducing aliasing, and heterogeneous sizing lets each juxtaposed class use its own optimal resolution at the cost of complicating cross-plot mapping. Bin boundaries: explicit boundaries (drawn outlines, as in the rectangular bins of Fig. 2b) reduce visual clutter's opposite problem — they make it easy to read off exact bin extents and align regions across juxtaposed plots — while implicit/boundary-less designs (glyphs floating in space with no drawn cell) reduce clutter and free up room for smaller, denser glyphs, at the cost of making exact bin-to-bin comparison harder.

The second major decision, composition (§5.2), is how per-class information from potentially many bins/classes gets combined into one view: juxtaposition (a separate coordinated plot per class, as in Fig. 1c, so each class can even have its own coordinate system/bin resolution) versus superposition (all classes overlaid into a shared set of bins, as in Fig. 2c, mixing class information at the bin level). The paper is explicit about the tradeoff: juxtaposition is well suited to comparing within-class distributional features across classes (its class-centric tasks 4, 6, 10) because each class gets full, undiluted visual bandwidth, but it's poor at any task requiring readers to compare classes within one bin, since that comparison now spans multiple plots — echoing Livingston et al.'s finding that tasks requiring readers to combine information across separate plots have higher error rates and slower response times than single-plot alternatives. Tied to composition is *normalization* — three options for scaling raw per-bin, per-class frequency counts: bin-internal (normalized to the bin's own maximum, which favors bin-centric/odd-numbered tasks by preserving cross-bin comparability of the whole bin), class-internal (normalized within a class across all bins, which favors class-centric/even-numbered tasks and reveals class-specific density centers), and global (normalized against the single overall maximum across all bins and classes, needed for genuinely comparable raw-frequency tasks like task 14, numerosity comparison – class).

Section 5.3 catalogs specific visual encodings for conveying density and class distribution within bins, organized into three tiers. Single-color designs occupy the whole bin background: luminance/grayscale conveys pure item density and, per Padilla et al., helps viewers identify distribution maxima better than continuous color scales; color-of-majority-class colors each bin by whichever class is most numerous there (with bin- or class-internal normalization producing different variants, shown in Fig. 2b); color blending (Fig. 5a) averages class colors by weighted proportion but becomes hard to read once more than roughly two or three classes overlap in one bin. A second tier, "color plus additional variables," packs more information into the bin background at the cost of losing the ability to layer another visualization on top: color weaving (Hagh-Shenas et al.; Fig. 5b) interleaves proportionally-sized colored fragments within a bin, which the paper credits with being more discriminable and scalable for showing class diversity than blending, though it is weak at conveying small class proportions since a handful of tiny fragments is hard to discern; attribute blocks (Miller; Fig. 2d) assign each class its own fixed sub-region of the bin and encode a summary value via luminance/position, trading some ease of reading for very clean class-centric overlap analysis; hatching (Livingston et al.; Fig. 5c) encodes class identity via both color and line angle, remaining perceptually efficient even when line thickness must shrink to fit many classes, though overlapping line-drawing order can visually bias which class appears more prominent. A third tier is glyphs added on top of (or in place of) a bin's background color: pie charts (the most common choice, borrowed from choropleth-map GIS practice, used with bin-internal normalization since the part-whole metaphor requires it) are good for bin-centric search/explore/anomaly tasks (3, 5, 9) but scale poorly once class counts get high, since a pie slice for a small class is essentially invisible; bar charts (regular or stacked, also borrowed from choropleth practice, shown in Fig. 3d) support both bin- and class-internal or global normalization and are specifically singled out as good at comparison tasks across bins and classes, such as task 14, numerosity comparison – class, because they share a common zero baseline that pie wedges lack; point glyphs (Bertini & Santucci; Chen et al.; Fig. 3c) sub-sample a handful of representative points per bin (ensuring at least one sample per present class, nudging overlapping samples slightly toward the bin center) to preserve some sense of spatial distribution within the bin while still resolving overdraw.

Section 5.4 briefly covers interaction: designs can support selecting/filtering classes (via a legend, or by clicking distinct glyph elements where a design has them) and interacting with bins (zooming until items no longer overlap, at which point the visualization can switch to a full per-item detail view). Section 6 describes the accompanying artifact: a web-based, open-source implementation built as a reusable vue.js/vuetify component with D3.js-based rendering, released as a running demo (the paper gives a URL, graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Vis/binning/) that lets users upload their own multi-class 2D data and interactively try out combinations of the paper's design dimensions, rendering svg-based visualizations that scale to datasets of many thousands of points directly in the browser. Table 2 is the paper's payoff artifact: an 11-design-by-16-task grid (rows: juxtaposition, superposition, grayscale, majority-color, blending, weaving, tone+position/attribute blocks, color+angle/hatching, pie charts, bar charts, point glyphs; columns: the 16 tasks) using checkmarks, circles, and X's to indicate whether a design fully supports, partially supports, or fails to support each task, assuming the best-configured version of that design (correct normalization, etc.) for the task at hand.

The discussion (§7) is candid about scope limits. The design space and task list explicitly exclude irregular lattices (Voronoi tessellations, quadtrees), which the authors acknowledge exist and can adapt bin shape/size to underlying data distributions, but which they set aside because the paper's goal is uncovering and preserving statistical/distributional properties of the data, which irregular, distribution-adapted bins would themselves distort. The paper distinguishes two separate facets of "scalability": scalability to number of data items (governed by bin resolution/aliasing tradeoffs, addressable via zooming/panning interaction) and scalability to number of classes, which is bottlenecked by color as the primary channel for encoding class identity — the authors state color-based designs are effective for roughly up to about 10 classes, and flag scaling to larger class counts (via added interaction to select subsets of classes, or via additional non-color-encoding glyphs) as open future work. The paper also flags that Table 2 shows real overlap between which designs support a given task, but that the paper does not evaluate whether one checkmarked design is empirically more effective than another checkmarked alternative for the same task — that comparison is explicitly left to future user studies. The conclusion restates the three contributions: a compiled task list for binned multi-class scatterplots, a survey/extension of visual designs for binned aggregation, and a task-based assessment (guideline table) connecting the two, plus the open-source component as a practical takeaway.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- Binning is not one design but a bundle of independent decisions — bin shape, bin size, boundary visibility, composition (juxtaposition vs. superposition), normalization (bin-internal, class-internal, global), and the specific visual encoding of class distribution within a bin (color-only, color+extra-channel, or glyph) — and a "binned scatterplot" recommendation is meaningless without pinning down all of these.
- The bin-centric/class-centric task distinction is the paper's most reusable idea: the same underlying question ("what patterns are here?") is a fundamentally different task depending on whether the analyst's unit of interest is a spatial region (a bin) or a category (a class), and a design that serves one scope well can actively fail the other.
- Juxtaposition vs. superposition is a general tradeoff, not specific to binning: separating data into coordinated small multiples (juxtaposition) maximizes per-series visual bandwidth and within-series comparison but makes cross-series/cross-plot comparison slow and error-prone; overlaying into one shared view (superposition) does the opposite. This generalizes well beyond scatterplots.
- Normalization choice is a semantic decision, not a cosmetic one — bin-internal, class-internal, and global normalization each answer a different underlying question and support different tasks; picking the wrong normalization silently breaks the comparison a reader is trying to make, even though the chart still "looks fine."
- Color's practical ceiling for encoding categorical class identity (roughly 10 classes, per this paper's assessment) is a concrete, citable number for a common design conversation about "just color-code the categories."
- The paper's guideline table (Table 2) models a useful practice for any design-space paper: crossing a task list against a design list and being explicit (checkmark/circle/X) about partial versus full support, rather than declaring one design category "the best."

**Skim/skip guidance:** This is a short, dense conference/journal-length paper (14 pages including references and author bios), and unlike a review article it does not have long stretches that are safe to skip — nearly every section is load-bearing for the paper's core contribution. If time is limited, read §4 (task list, Table 1) and §5 (design space, Figure 4, plus the subsections on composition/normalization and on class-distribution encodings) in full — these are the paper's actual content. §2 (motivation/example data) and the worked examples embedded throughout §4–5 (Figures 1–3, 5) are worth skimming for the concrete before/after scatterplot comparisons, which make the abstract task/design language legible, but the play-by-play analysis of each example dataset can be skimmed once the pattern is clear. §3 (related work) and §6 (the web tool) are the most skippable for a reader who just wants the design guidance — §3 is a fairly standard literature review, and §6 is a short pointer to a demo tool whose live URL should be treated as unverified this many years after publication. §7/§8 (discussion and conclusion) are short and worth reading for the explicit scope caveats (irregular lattices excluded, no comparative user study between designs, class-count scalability ceiling).

**Caveats:** This is the arXiv preprint (v2, 14 Jan 2020); the PDF itself never states a publication venue, though an in-text footnote ("to be published with camera ready version of this submission," attached to the open-source component citation) indicates the paper had been accepted for publication by the time of this revision — a reader who needs the final published venue/year for citation purposes should verify it independently rather than relying on this PDF. The paper is a design-space and taxonomy contribution, not an empirical evaluation: Table 2's design-task ratings are the authors' own reasoned assessment (informed by cited perceptual literature for individual claims, such as Padilla et al. on color-scale maxima detection or Livingston et al. on cross-plot comparison error rates) rather than the product of a new controlled user study comparing the designs against each other, and the authors explicitly flag comparative evaluation of same-task-supporting designs as future work. The design space is deliberately restricted to regular, fixed-size lattices; irregular tessellations (Voronoi regions, quadtrees, distribution-adaptive bin sizing) are explicitly out of scope, as is computational scalability (the paper addresses only visual/perceptual scalability, not runtime performance of aggregation at very large data volumes). The stated ~10-class ceiling for color-based class encoding is presented as a practical assessment rather than a value derived from a dedicated experiment in this paper. Finally, the web tool's live URL is now several years old and was not checked as part of this summary (no web fetches were used, per the summary process); treat it as likely stale until verified.
