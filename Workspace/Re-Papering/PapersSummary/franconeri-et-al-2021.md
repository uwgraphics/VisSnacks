# Franconeri, Padilla, Shah, Zacks & Hullman, "The Science of Visual Data Communication: What Works" (Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2021)

**What it is:** A field-spanning review — commissioned as a "Psychological Science in the Public Interest" report — that assembles evidence-backed design guidelines for communicating data to nonspecialist audiences (students, coworkers, the public), synthesizing work from visual perception, information visualization, data-based reasoning, uncertainty representation, and health-risk communication that the authors argue "too rarely connect."

**AI summary:** The paper opens by framing visualization literacy as a matter of public consequence, not just design taste: science standards require students to reason with visualizations, evidence-based policy on climate and vaccines depends on communicating data credibly, and low graphical literacy plus ineffective design can lead viewers to draw conclusions "opposite the one suggested by the depicted data." The authors deliberately ignore disciplinary boundaries — a psychologist studying color-to-magnitude mapping, a cognitive psychologist studying working-memory limits, an education researcher studying textbook graphics, and a computer scientist building interactive tools are all treated as contributing to one evidence base — and the review's structure tracks a viewer's pipeline: first whether values can be read off a chart accurately (perceptual accuracy), then whether that reading is fast enough to be efficient (perceptual efficiency), then whether the reader's conceptual schema for the chart type lets them connect visual pattern to real-world meaning (understandability), and finally the special problems of communicating uncertainty and health risk.

The first major argument is that vision does two very different jobs at very different speeds. Visualizations let viewers extract broad statistics from an entire two-dimensional array almost instantaneously — Figure 1 reproduces Anscombe's quartet (four x/y datasets sharing identical means, standard deviations, and correlation coefficients to two decimal places) plus Matejka & Fitzmaurice's more extreme "Same Stats, Different Graphs" set (nine scatterplots, including one shaped like a dinosaur, all matching the same summary statistics), to argue that "relying on statistics turns out to be dangerous" and that a new dataset should always be plotted — histograms and scatterplots for every interesting pairing of columns — before summary statistics are trusted. But making *comparisons* between subsets of that data is a slow, serial, capacity-limited process: "extracting global statistics is fast, but comparisons between subsets of values are slow," with each individual comparison taking hundreds of milliseconds and viewers able to process only a handful per second, so a bar graph with 12 bars (66 possible pairwise comparisons) can be genuinely hard to reason about even though nothing about it looks confusing at a glance.

The section on perceptual accuracy works from Figure 2's now-canonical ranking (following Cleveland & McGill) of visual channels by how precisely a viewer can verbally state the ratio between two encoded values: position is the clear winner, followed by length, area, angle, and intensity (color/luminance) trailing badly. This ranking motivates a long catalog of ways designs mismap ratios back to numbers even on high-precision channels: truncated y-axes (the Fox News Obamacare bar chart showing a 6:7 real ratio as a visually 1:3 one; a deleted National Review climate graph using −10°F as a lower bound to flatten a "hockey stick" trend; a September 2019 Economist cover mapping a narrow temperature range to the full extent of a blue-red color scale), confusion between circle area and diameter, angle-vs-2D-area confounds in donut charts, and failures to recover 3D geometry from 2D projections. A separate cluster of illusions is catalogued in Figure 4: a line-graph illusion where two identical curves offset by a constant appear to converge because viewers measure vertical rather than orthogonal distance between lines; an "illusory contrast" effect where identical luminance values look different depending on surrounding context, making it inadvisable to ever plot intensity encodings on top of other intensity encodings; and categorical-perception effects, where continuous color scales are perceived as discrete bins (as in the visible rainbow, or in fMRI activation maps), artificially exaggerating differences that straddle a category boundary. The paper devotes a subsection to color-vision accessibility: an estimated 4% of the global population (roughly 300 million people) has some form of color-vision impairment, and Figure 5 walks through a scatterplot as seen with normal vision versus a simulated protanopia deficiency, showing how a single-hue encoding collapses two visibly distinct groups into one, and recommending double-encoding (redundant shape or texture) or colorblind-safe palettes such as those from Harrower and Brewer's ColorBrewer as remedies. Crucially, the paper notes the position-is-best ranking is itself task-dependent: for extracting a single precise value position wins, but for judging an *average* across many values, intensity coding can actually outperform position — a dissociation the authors say "is not well understood." Similarly, when the task shifts from single-value comparison to distinguishing categorical groups in a scatterplot, color reliably beats shape for speed, and Figure 6 shows an experimentally-derived perceptually-spaced shape set (open vs. closed contours matter most) that clearly outperforms Excel's default shape palette for a second, less important nominal variable — but redundantly encoding both color and shape for the same variable gives little additional benefit except for color-blind viewers.

The paper's second major thread is perceptual *efficiency* — not just whether a value can be read correctly, but how much cognitive effort it costs. Section "Avoid a visual processing limit: making comparisons" argues that the "sluggishness" of comparison, not accuracy, is often the real bottleneck: viewers asked to find which of several paired bars decreased (Figure 7's "Test Scores" bar graph) must serially process each pair, and in a striking demonstration (Boger et al., 2021, "Jurassic mark"), 93% of viewers tracking one color of dots in a scatterplot completely failed to notice a dinosaur shape formed by a second color of dots layered on top. The prescribed fix is *visual grouping*: Figure 8 ranks grouping cues from most to least powerful — connecting lines, then spatial proximity, then color/hue, then shape — and shows how the same four-way dataset can be made to emphasize entirely different comparisons (within-category vs. within-time-period) purely by choice of grouping cue, with a worked bar-graph example (Social Security/earnings/pensions data from 1962 vs. 2007) and a word-cloud example where proximity grouping makes semantic clusters ("restaurant," "baseball," "graduation" words) far easier to spot than color grouping does. Beyond grouping, designers can guide viewers to the *single* most important comparison through salience (bright/distinct color highlighting, as in an "unemployment is higher than stated goals" line-graph redesign in Figure 10) and direct annotation, addressing what the authors call the *curse of knowledge* — a documented inability of presenters to simulate a naive viewer's perspective, empirically demonstrated in Figure 11 (Xiong, Van Weelden & Franconeri, 2020): participants told a story about specific patterns in a line graph, then asked to predict what a naive viewer would find salient in the same unannotated graph, systematically over-attributed their own story-driven attention to that hypothetical naive viewer. The same visual-grouping and highlighting toolkit doubles as a persuasion mechanism — Figure 12 shows the identical unemployment dataset framed to support opposite political narratives purely through which pattern (a downward slope vs. an area exceeding a goal line) is emphasized — and the paper connects this to Hullman & Diakopoulos's "visualization rhetoric" framework, plus studies showing that scatterplot correlation judgments shift when the same points are labeled as "personality variables" (assumed correlated) versus left unlabeled, and that social pressure (seeing others' ratings, even manipulated ones) biases individual judgments. Working memory receives its own subsection: Figure 13 demonstrates that reading four line-graph labels straight off the lines ("direct labeling") is markedly faster than looking them up in a color legend or shape legend, because legend lookups require holding a symbol-to-referent mapping in working memory while re-scanning the chart — hence the guideline to prefer direct labels over legends wherever the layout allows it. Animation gets a skeptical treatment: capacity for tracking moving objects is limited to roughly one or two objects at a time, producing a "transient information effect" (a Kriz & Hegarty study found students shown *animated* diagrams of a toilet-flushing mechanism made 20% errors about individual stages, versus 5% for those shown static diagrams, despite reporting higher confidence and engagement) — with the notable exception that animation is genuinely effective for conveying sampling processes and probabilistic draws (hypothetical outcome plots), because that use case doesn't require retaining precise object trajectories. The rich-vs-minimalist aesthetic debate (Tufte's "data-ink ratio," "chartjunk") is treated as empirically unsettled: some studies find preferences for decluttered charts, others find the opposite, and pictorial embellishments — while showing "little to no" improvement in data recall in some studies — do reliably improve long-term memory for the fact that a chart was seen at all (Borkin et al.'s memorability work).

The third pillar, understandability, argues that even accurate, efficient perception is useless if the viewer lacks the *graph schema* needed to map visual variation onto meaning. Figure 16's bubble chart of GDP-by-population (nested, colored, unevenly positioned circles for the ten most populous countries) is presented as deliberately hard to parse — "if you are having trouble extracting the data from this visualization, it is not your fault — you do not have the needed schema" — and Figure 17 catalogs four novel-but-real chart types (connected scatterplots, parallel coordinates, treemaps, adjacency matrices) against their more "intuitive" familiar cousins, illustrating that expert communities routinely trade intuitiveness for information density. Some schema violations are near-universal and simply confuse people when broken: Figure 18 shows that viewers expect "more" to map to higher vertical position, darker (on light backgrounds) or more opaque colors, and rightward horizontal position in left-to-right reading cultures, and that a real Florida gun-deaths graphic with an inverted, smaller-at-top y-axis caused readers to see an actual increase in deaths as a decrease. A related failure mode is choosing a chart type that mismatches the data's conceptual type: mapping nominal categories (car manufacturer) to bar length implies a false metric ordering, while the choice between bars and connected lines for the same metric variable can flip the take-home message entirely — the paper's memorable example (from Zacks & Tversky, 1999) is that plotting height against nationality as a *line* graph leads a meaningful fraction of viewers to conclude "one tends to become more Dutch as one becomes taller," because the connecting line implies a continuous trend where none exists. Finally, even correct within-chart reading can fail to connect to real-world claims: in a study of 673 high school students given a climate graph paired with an *incorrect* caption claiming CO2 and temperature "are not related," only 4% of students noticed the contradiction (Farrar, 2012); in a graph-construction task, when asked to draw a position-vs-time graph of a runner who sprints, rests, then walks back, only 1% of middle/high schoolers produced a correct graph, with most instead drawing a literal top-down "map" of the running path.

The paper's fourth major section, on uncertainty and risk, opens by arguing that lay audiences routinely misunderstand the statistical constructs behind common uncertainty displays — even trained researchers frequently misinterpret a frequentist 95% confidence interval as "there is a 95% probability the true value lies in this interval" (a frequentist illegal move; Hoekstra et al., 2012) and mistakenly treat non-overlapping error bars as necessary and sufficient evidence of significance. Error bars specifically mislead lay viewers into treating them as depicting the discrete range of the underlying data rather than parameter uncertainty around a summary statistic; a striking demonstration (Hofman et al., 2020, Figure 20's fictional "boulder-sliding" intervention study) found that viewers shown a standard-error interval (a narrower band) were more willing to pay for an intervention and more likely to overestimate its effect size than viewers shown the wider, more conservative predictive interval representing actual outcome variability — a difference the corrigendum to this article specifically revises the description of. Figure 21 shows a parallel illusion for bar graphs themselves: viewers rate a data point falling just inside a bar's height as more likely to belong to the distribution the bar represents than an equidistant point falling just outside it, treating the bar as a literal container. The National Hurricane Center's "cone of uncertainty" gets singled out as a real-world case study of exactly this failure: viewers wrongly believe the cone depicts a hard boundary (dangerous inside, safe outside) or read its width as reflecting the *storm's* physical size rather than the range of its possible paths. Proposed remedies converge on making uncertainty *discrete and frequency-based* rather than abstract and interval-based: quantile dot plots (Figure 22, and FiveThirtyEight's 2020 election "ball swarm" of 100 sampled outcomes) let viewers literally count dots to extract a probability rather than visually integrating under a density curve; value-suppressing uncertainty palettes (Correll, Moritz & Heer, 2018, Figure 23) collapse color-value distinctions for highly uncertain map regions using a tree metaphor so uncertain areas read visually as more ambiguous; icon arrays (Figure 24, Galesic et al., 2009's aspirin/stroke example) let viewers compare a treatment's absolute risk against control using literally countable filled-versus-empty icons instead of abstract percentages; reconstructed hurricane-path ensembles (Figure 25) reduce the "spaghetti plot" clutter of raw sampled paths while preserving the true spread; and hypothetical outcome plots (HOPs; Figure 26) animate a sequence of individual random draws from a distribution, exploiting the visual system's automatic ability to extract frequency statistics from a temporal sequence without requiring any training in statistics. The closing health-risk section applies these lessons concretely: it recommends absolute risk over relative risk framing (citing a study where 41.3% of practicing physicians were more likely to recommend a treatment when its benefit was framed as a relative rather than an equivalent absolute risk reduction), documents "denominator neglect" (people weighing the numerator of a risk ratio, e.g., 1,286 deaths, more heavily than the full ratio, making 1,286/10,000 feel riskier than the equivalent 24.14/100), and recommends icon arrays with matched denominators as the most effective remedy, while flagging that anthropomorphic icon variants (Figure 27's blocks vs. ovals vs. faces vs. head outlines vs. photographs, from Zikmund-Fisher et al., 2014) interact with a viewer's own graph literacy and numeracy in ways that are still only partly understood. The paper closes with an explicit bulleted "Summary of Key Guidelines" distilling every section into practitioner-facing prescriptions, plus a "Recommended Reading" box (Box 1) pointing to practitioner guides by Camões, Evergreen, Knaflic, Schwabish, and Cairo, and an accessibility reference (Kim et al., 2021).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The paper's organizing pipeline is itself a useful mental model for critiquing any chart: can the value be read *accurately* (channel precision, illusions), can it be read *efficiently* (comparison limits, working memory, grouping/highlighting), and can it be *understood* (does the viewer have the right graph schema, does the chart type match the data type)? A chart can fail at any one of these three stages independently of the others.
- Internalize the channel-precision ranking (position > length > area > angle > intensity) as the default answer to "which encoding should carry my most important variable" — but hold it loosely: the ranking is itself task-dependent (intensity can beat position for judging averages; color reliably beats shape for grouping).
- The distinction between "vision is fast for global statistics, slow for comparisons" is the single most load-bearing idea in the paper and generalizes far beyond any one example — it explains why dashboards with many bars feel exhausting even when each bar is perfectly legible, and why grouping/highlighting/annotation exist as a design toolkit at all.
- The "curse of knowledge" (Figure 11) is a sharp, quotable warning against trusting your own reaction to a chart you built — a presenter who already knows the story cannot simulate what a naive viewer will notice.
- Uncertainty and risk communication is not solved by "just add error bars" — lay viewers routinely misread confidence intervals, error bars, and cones of uncertainty as literal ranges rather than as probabilistic summaries, and frequency-based, discrete depictions (icon arrays, quantile dot plots, hypothetical outcome plots) consistently outperform abstract statistical depictions for nonexpert audiences.
- Chart-type choice carries an implicit conceptual claim independent of what's technically plotted: bars imply discrete comparison, connected lines imply continuous trend, and picking the wrong one (nominal data as bars, metric data as connected lines) can lead viewers to draw an incorrect real-world inference even when every individual value is read correctly.

**Skim/skip guidance:** This is a long synthesis review (about 40 pages of main text plus ~450 references) organized as a sequence of short, self-contained subsections each anchored by one or two figures — it reads more like an illustrated field guide than a single sustained argument, so it's well suited to targeted reading. The introduction and "Summary of Key Guidelines" (the final section, just before Transparency/References) are worth reading in full as bookends — the guidelines section alone is a fast, quotable checklist. Within the body, the strongest, most citable sections for a visualization course are "Visual channels translate numbers into images," "Avoid a visual processing limit: making comparisons," "Control comparison with visual grouping cues," and the uncertainty-communication section — these carry the paper's most original synthesis and its best figures (2, 8, 11, 20–26). The color-vision-impairment subsection, the shape-perception subsection (Figure 6), and the health-risk subsection are narrower and can be skimmed unless color accessibility or health communication specifically is the topic at hand. The reference list itself (pp. 151–161) is a genuinely useful bibliography of the empirical vis-cognition literature circa 2021 and worth keeping as a citation lookup even if not read line by line.

**Caveats:** This is a review/synthesis article, not new primary research — its evidentiary weight rests on the underlying studies it cites (many single lab experiments with modest sample sizes, as is typical of the vis-cognition literature), and the authors themselves flag several open dissociations rather than settled facts (e.g., why intensity beats position for average judgments "is not well understood"; the rich-vs-minimalist aesthetic debate is explicitly called mixed evidence, not resolved). The review deliberately narrows its scope to quantitative visualizations for nonspecialist communication, explicitly excluding qualitative/text visualizations, diagrams, and process visualizations, and explicitly does not systematically cover visualization literacy measurement or teaching effectiveness (both flagged as out of scope, with pointers to Börner et al., 2019 and Lee et al., 2016 instead). Guidelines drawn from narrow laboratory tasks (e.g., the position/length/area/angle/intensity precision ranking, based on ratio-estimation judgments) are repeatedly shown, within the paper itself, not to generalize cleanly to other tasks (correlation judgment, mean judgment, group differentiation) — treating Figure 2's ranking as a universal law rather than a task-specific finding is the paper's own explicitly flagged over-generalization risk. Note also that this specific PDF bundles a 2022 corrigendum (two pages, prepended ahead of the article proper in this file) correcting Figures 5 and 23's captions and color palette, and revising the wording describing the Hofman et al. (2020) boulder-sliding results in Figure 20 and its surrounding text — the summary above reflects the corrected figures and captions, not the original 2021 print version.
