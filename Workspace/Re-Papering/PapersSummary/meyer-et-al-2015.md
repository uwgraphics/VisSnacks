# Meyer, Sedlmair, Quinan, and Munzner, "The nested blocks and guidelines model" (Information Visualization, 14(3):234–249, 2015)

**What it is:** A conceptual/framework paper (a design-decision model, not an empirical study) that proposes the nested blocks and guidelines model (NBGM) for the design and validation of visualization systems. The paper presents the NBGM as an extension of the four-level nested model that Munzner proposed in 2009: it keeps the four levels and adds finer-grained structure inside each level (blocks) and an explicit vocabulary for relationships between blocks (guidelines). It then applies the model post hoc to three InfoVis 2012 papers, situates it among other kinds of design models, and uses it to derive implications for visualization evaluation and to name two knowledge gaps. The paper states that it builds on an earlier workshop article; the added material is the fuller block definitions, the three analyses, and the survey of design-model types. The article was published online first in 2013 (the PDF's copyright line reads "The Author(s) 2013") and appeared in the 2015 issue.

**AI summary:** The paper opens by noting that creating visualizations is a design process, and that many design models exist across visualization, software engineering, and design itself. In visualization, the authors say, the work has mostly been on process models (stages with concrete actions, such as the nine-stage design study framework) and architecture models (the structure of a software system, such as the visualization pipeline). They claim little attention has gone to design decision models, which capture and describe design decisions. The original nested model provides guidance on choosing validation techniques at each level and stresses the cascading effects of poor decisions. The authors report that, although they and others have used it extensively, it "falls short" in capturing design decisions at the level of detail needed for thorough analysis. Their remedy is to extend it rather than replace it.

The core definitions come in the section on adding blocks and guidelines. A block is the outcome of the design process at a specific level: an algorithm, a visual encoding and/or interaction technique, a data or task abstraction, or a specific domain situation. Blocks exist at different granularities and nest hierarchically; a treemap technique block, for instance, includes finer-grained color and size encoding blocks, which can also appear in other blocks such as a bubble chart. Fine-grained blocks therefore have one-to-many relationships with the blocks that contain them, and complex blocks that share a fine-grained piece overlap. The authors chose the name as an allusion to building blocks: some fit together and others do not, and structures can be broken down into the same base pieces. A key distinction is between designed and identified blocks. At the lower levels (algorithm, technique) blocks are designed, meaning created or invented. At the upper levels they are identified, as the outcome of the designer's understanding of users' requirements.

Each level is then characterized. At the domain level, blocks are "situations": a group of target users, their field of interest, their questions, and their measurements or data (examples are a computational biologist in comparative genomics, and members of the public making medical decisions under uncertainty). Situation blocks are identified through interviews, observation, or research into the users, and they nest from whole domains such as bioinformatics down to specific questions (the MizBee design study's table of low-level genomics questions is the example). The authors prefer "situation" to the original "domain characterization" because it has a broader scope than a formal field of study, and tie this to the holistic view in the design-thinking literature. The abstraction level holds both task blocks and data blocks, with different status: task blocks (finding outliers and trends, for example) are identified, while data blocks (table, network, text, categorical/ordered/quantitative attributes) are designed, because the designer often transforms the data from the form found in the domain situation. Even keeping the data unchanged is described as an active design decision. The ABySS-Explorer study illustrates a data transformation: the designers swapped nodes and edges in the genome assembly graph because the domain experts reasoned that way, and the NBGM treats the two graphs as different data abstraction blocks. The authors say this lets them separate a failure to identify the right task abstraction from a failure in designing the data abstraction. Technique blocks combine visual encoding and interaction and are not split apart (the word tree is the example). Algorithm blocks concern how to draw something programmatically (ray casting, splatting, and texture mapping for volume rendering; the GEM force-directed layout), and are often inseparably connected to technique blocks.

A guideline is defined as a statement about the relationships between blocks. Within-level guidelines compare blocks on one level (the fastest algorithm of several, say) and often carry upstream dependencies that go unstated. For example, Tory et al.'s finding that simple points beat landscapes is stated for dimensionally reduced data, while the choice of the newer Voronoi treemap algorithm over the older one is cited as a guideline with little dependence on upstream blocks. Between-level guidelines map blocks at adjacent levels, such as hue colormaps to categorical data. The paper folds the literature's terms "characterization" (moving up) and "guideline" (moving down) into one concept, bidirectional. Guidelines can also be many-to-one or many-to-many and then are not trivially invertible: matrix alignment suits both tables and networks, but node-link diagrams do not suit tables. A system is a stack of blocks, one or a few per level, with higher choices constraining lower ones; real systems usually contain several stacks because the domain problem decomposes into multiple abstract subtasks. The paper links guideline types to research types: within-level guidelines tend to come out of technique-driven validation, between-level guidelines out of design studies, and evaluation articles can produce either. It argues that contributing a new block without any guidelines about its use is "a lost opportunity". The authors say the NBGM can be used in all three of Beaudouin-Lafon's ways: descriptive, evaluative, and generative. It also clarifies the eighth stage of the nine-stage design study framework (reflect), which the authors say should involve both between-level and within-level guidelines, and the paper borrows that framework's four verbs (propose, refine, confirm, reject) to describe contributions.

The analysis examples cover one design study, one technique paper, and one evaluation paper from InfoVis 2012; system and model papers were skipped as not focused on these kinds of decisions. In the diagrams, solid outlines mark contributions and dashed outlines mark existing blocks or guidelines that were simply used. For the RelEx design study (in-car network traffic optimization for automotive engineers), the stack is broad and deep: situation blocks for automotive engineering and in-car network specification, a task abstraction of optimizing network traffic, and a data abstraction of an overlay network shown with a matrix for the dense logical network and node-link for the small sparse base network. The matrix and node-link choices follow existing guidelines by Ghoniem et al., which the study confirms through field observation of post-deployment use. The RelEx domain and abstractions are also compared to those of other network visualization studies, which the authors read as new within-level contributions at the abstraction level. For the large-graph LOD rendering technique (Zinsmaier et al.), the contribution is new algorithm blocks (GPU node density estimation and edge aggregation) and a hierarchical technique block mixing existing and new sub-blocks, with the abstractions (large graphs that fit in video memory but overplot; exploration) taken as upstream assumptions. Because technique and algorithm are tightly woven, the comparisons with MINGLE and KDEEB can only be between whole stacks. For the Bayesian reasoning evaluation (Micallef et al.), the task and data abstraction blocks are existing ones, medicine and law are existing situations, and the general public is identified as a new situation block. The first study compared seven existing encoding blocks and could not replicate previously reported effects, so the authors rejected the earlier within-level guidelines for this new situation and proposed new ones. They then refined two blocks combining text and visualization and compared them in a second study, proposing further guidelines.

In the section on types of design models, the authors classify three kinds found in software engineering, cognitive science, visualization, and design: process models (the how of design, in stages), architecture models (including design patterns and the visualization pipeline), and decision models (capturing the rationale behind decisions). The NBGM is a decision model, as is the original nested model, which the authors call a coarse-grained one. The context of a decision is modeled by the upstream assumptions and downstream implications as well as the known alternatives. The authors warn that viewing the NBGM as a cookbook, where practitioners trace paths through known blocks and guidelines, breaks down because design is continually innovating and its problems are wicked or ill-structured; they invoke satisficing and say the NBGM's contribution is helping designers make satisficing decisions through the formalization of context. They also report that a thread of research-through-design work was examined and judged not directly relevant to their question.

The discussion draws two implications for evaluation. First, comparing domains must happen implicitly at the abstraction level: domain situation blocks differ by definition, so the useful move is to map them to abstractions. If the same abstraction block serves several situations a common solution is feasible. The authors use RelEx to illustrate this, and they call the establishment of a complete set of task and data abstraction blocks the "long-term grand challenge of applied visualization research". Second, within-level guidelines depend on both upstream assumptions and downstream decisions, so a block comparison should be understood in the context of a stack. Comparing data abstractions requires choosing a visual encoding, and evaluating an interaction technique usually requires algorithm blocks; some comparisons are therefore between stacks that span levels. The knowledge-gaps section names two open problems. First, there is a dearth of identified blocks at the abstraction level, especially task blocks at the middle level: Amar et al.'s low-level task taxonomy and Amar and Stasko's very high-level taxonomy exist, but the middle is empty (Brehmer and Munzner's work is cited as a first step), and the authors observe that task identification and data derivation form an interwoven cycle that the model may need refinement to capture. Second, guidelines mapping up from algorithms to techniques are sparse or subtle; different force-directed layout algorithms can produce quite different visual encodings, yet few authors discuss this (Noack is an exception). The limitations section says blocks and guidelines may not capture all the complexity of visualization design, that taxonomies at every level may suggest new or refined levels, and that field validation could refine the model. The conclusion restates the extension, the identified-versus-designed distinction, the contributed-versus-used distinction, and the two evaluation implications.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The vocabulary: a block is the outcome of a design decision at a level, and a guideline is a statement about a relationship between blocks, either within a level (comparing choices) or between adjacent levels (mapping choices). That gives a precise way to say what a design claim is about.
- The identified-versus-designed distinction is a useful lens: domain situations and task abstractions are understood and identified, while data abstractions, techniques, and algorithms are designed. Choosing a data abstraction (including choosing not to transform the data) is a real design decision, not a given.
- A guideline is only as general as its upstream assumptions. The Bayesian reasoning example shows previous guidelines failing when the situation changes to the general public, and the Tory et al. example shows a guideline that holds for dimensionally reduced data.
- Fair comparisons are often between stacks, not single blocks, because visual encodings are bound to the algorithms that produce them and evaluating a data abstraction requires committing to an encoding.
- Different situations cannot be compared directly; they are compared through the abstractions they map to, which is why rich data and task abstractions matter for generalizing across domains.
- The paper offers a template for reading research papers: for each, ask which blocks were proposed, refined, confirmed, rejected, or merely used, and which levels they sit at.
- The paper itself says the NBGM is not a cookbook and does not claim there is a best solution; it supports reasoning about decisions in context.
- The two named gaps (middle-level task taxonomies; algorithm-to-technique mappings) are stated as open problems.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read the abstract, the introduction, and the "Adding blocks and guidelines" section (pp. 235–239) in full; this is the model itself and the part to cite. Figures 2, 3, and 4 matter most: Figure 2 is the signature picture of blocks as shapes with within-level and between-level arrows, Figure 3 shows invertible versus many-to-many guidelines, and Figure 4 shows stacks of blocks. Figure 1 is just the original nested model for reference. The "Analysis examples" section (Figures 6 to 8, pp. 240–243) is useful for seeing the model applied, and a reader who has the idea can skim one or two of the three examples; the RelEx and Bayesian reasoning ones are the most instructive for design studies and evaluation respectively. Figure 5 (the nine-stage framework) only needs a glance. "Types of design models" is the place to go for how the NBGM relates to process and architecture models, and can be skimmed otherwise. The "Discussion" (evaluation implications and knowledge gaps) is short and contains the paper's secondary claims, so it is worth reading. The reference list (about 70 items, starting on p. 247) can be skipped.

**Memorable specifics:**

- Definitions: "A guideline is a statement about the relationships between blocks." The abstract says "Blocks are the outcomes of the design process at a specific level, and guidelines discuss relationships between these blocks."
- Concrete block examples from the introduction: a network as a data abstraction block, a node-link diagram as a visual encoding technique block, and the GEM force-directed layout as an algorithm block. Concrete guidelines: a node-link diagram is a good visual encoding of small graphs, or one specific force-directed algorithm is faster than another.
- On characterization versus guideline: "we consider these concepts to simply be two sides of the same coin." The paper's example is that "hue-based colormaps are appropriate for categorical data" restates as "if your data is categorical, then hue-based colormaps are appropriate."
- The many-to-many guideline example (Figure 3b): matrix alignment suits both table and network abstractions, but node-link does not match the table abstraction.
- Figure 2 shows levels ordered vertically rather than nested, "for visual clarity"; arrows between individual blocks are guidelines, whereas the arrows in the original Figure 1 are dependencies between whole levels.
- Analysis diagram conventions: solid lines for a contribution, dashed for use of an existing block or guideline, gray background for a stack, containment for hierarchy.
- Three analyzed papers (all InfoVis 2012): RelEx (Sedlmair et al.), large-graph GPU LOD rendering (Zinsmaier et al., compared against MINGLE and KDEEB), and the Bayesian reasoning evaluation (Micallef et al.). In the last, seven encoding blocks were compared in the first study, and the authors "could not replicate previously reported effects", so they rejected previous guidelines and proposed new ones.
- On satisficing: "there is no best solution, only verification of whether a solution is sufficient or good enough."
- On missing guidelines for new blocks: not contributing guidelines is called "a lost opportunity".
- On the gaps: "Without blocks, we cannot have guidelines"; "we lack task blocks at the middle level." The grand challenge phrase is "the long-term grand challenge of applied visualization research."
- Funding statement: NSERC Strategic Grant, a Microsoft Research Faculty Fellowship, and NSF grant IIS-1212806.

**Caveats:** This is a conceptual paper; its claims for the model's descriptive, evaluative, and generative power rest on three post hoc analyses done by the authors, none of which is a test with other analysts or designers. The authors themselves list limitations: blocks and guidelines may not capture all the complexity of design, and field validation is future work. The analyzed papers are all from InfoVis 2012, so the examples and the "recent work" and "very recent study" references (for example, Brehmer and Munzner's task typology) are dated by years and the mid-level task gap may have been partly addressed since; the paper says nothing about later work.
