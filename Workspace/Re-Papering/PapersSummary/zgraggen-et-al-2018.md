# Zgraggen, Zhao, Zeleznik & Kraska, "Investigating the Effect of the Multiple Comparisons Problem in Visual Analysis" (CHI, 2018)

**What it is:** An empirical HCI study that imports the statistical *multiple comparisons problem* (MCP) into interactive visual data exploration, and directly measures how often "insights" people report while browsing visualizations with a real tool are actually false — the answer, in their experiment, is over 60% of the time.

**AI summary:** The paper opens with a thought experiment that reframes MCP intuitively: rolling a pair of dice once, the chance of "snake eyes" is low, but keep rolling and eventually you're guaranteed to see it — the probability of *eventually* observing an unlikely-looking event approaches certainty as the number of trials grows. The authors argue visual analysis has the identical structure: as an analyst looks at more and more visualizations of the same dataset, the probability of stumbling on an interesting-looking pattern that is actually just random noise (a *false discovery*, statistically a Type I error) rises sharply, even though each individual visualization looks like an honest, careful look at the data. A worked scenario (Jean, a nonprofit worker comparing yearly donor-retention charts across 11 years and "discovering" that a USB-drive thank-you gift correlates with a jump in repeat donors) walks through why this specific insight is spurious — the underlying data was sampled from a single uniform distribution with no real year-to-year difference at all; ten implicit comparisons (one per year) inflated the chance of finding an apparently interesting pattern in at least one of them. Three colleagues in the scenario illustrate three different confirmatory follow-up strategies with different costs and validity: testing the insight on the *same* dataset (cheap, but reintroduces exactly the bias being tested for — data dredging/p-hacking); testing on a fresh, held-out *validation* dataset (statistically sound, catches the false insight, but requires new data that's often expensive or impossible to get); and *mixing exploration and confirmation* by having the analyst reconstruct and formally account for every comparison made during the exploratory session (achieves validity similar to a held-out dataset, without needing new data — but requires the analyst to accurately recall/log everything they looked at).

The core contribution is an experiment quantifying this problem directly, rather than just arguing it exists. The authors define an "insight" narrowly and operationally — an observation, hypothesis, or generalization directly extracted from the data, deliberately excluding claims based on prior knowledge or domain expertise — precisely so that each reported insight can be scored true/false against a known ground truth. They built synthetic datasets (shopping and sleep domains, at two realistic sizes, 300 and 1000 records) generated from correlated and uncorrelated bivariate-normal variable pairs with known ground-truth relationships, so any insight a participant reports about a relationship between two variables can be checked against the actual generating model. 28 participants (all with some data-analysis-tool experience and at least one stats course) explored one dataset each for up to 15 minutes using a Tableau-like drag-and-drop visual analysis tool (derived from Vizdom/PanoramicData), thinking aloud, with screen and eye-tracking recorded, and wrote down "reliable observations" using a note-taking feature; a follow-up review session with the experimenter clarified ambiguous insights.

Results: participants reported an average of 5.5 insights each; using a standard true-positive/false-positive/true-negative/false-negative breakdown against the known ground truth, the average accuracy across users was only 0.375 (37.5%) — meaning **over 60% of self-reported, confidently-written insights were false** — with a false discovery rate of 0.738 (when a user claimed a positive finding, it was wrong nearly three-quarters of the time) and a much lower false omission rate of 0.018 (when users said "no relationship," they were almost always right — the errors ran overwhelmingly in the direction of seeing patterns that weren't there, not missing ones that were). Neither dataset type nor dataset size significantly affected accuracy (ANOVA, p > 0.05 for both). The second half of the paper (not needed for the course's purposes, per the reading note) converts insights into formal statistical hypotheses and re-runs the three confirmatory strategies from the opening scenario at scale, showing quantitatively that confirming on the same dataset dramatically inflates false-discovery rate relative to a held-out validation dataset, while the "mixing exploration and confirmation" approach (accounting for all comparisons made) achieves comparable protection to a true validation dataset without needing fresh data.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The headline number is the paper's whole argument in one fact: in a controlled experiment with a real interactive visualization tool, **more than 60% of the insights people confidently wrote down turned out to be false** — a strong, quotable empirical anchor for "exploratory visual analysis produces false discoveries at an alarming rate if nothing corrects for it."
- The dice-rolling framing is the cleanest possible intuition-builder for *why*: looking at enough visualizations of the same data is structurally the same as running enough statistical tests — the chance of a false-looking "win" approaches 100% with enough looks, even on pure noise.
- This paper is the direct, concrete, visualization-specific companion to Leek & Peng's "What is the question?" — Leek and Peng name the abstract error (exploratory findings mistaken for inferential/confirmed ones); Zgraggen et al. show it actually happening, with a real tool, real participants, and a measured failure rate, in exactly the interactive-visual-analysis setting this course cares about.
- The false-discovery vs. false-omission asymmetry (0.738 vs. 0.018) is itself a teachable point: the risk from visual exploration isn't "missing real patterns," it's "manufacturing fake ones" — people are much better at correctly saying "nothing here" than at correctly judging that something they *did* notice is real.
- The three confirmatory-strategy sketch (same-dataset, held-out validation, or meticulously accounting for every comparison made) is a practical, actionable framework students can actually apply to their own exploratory-analysis habits, not just a warning.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The introduction's dice-rolling framing and the Jean/Noemi/Hasan/Kendra worked scenario carry the paper's entire intuitive argument and are the highest-value section. **Memorable specifics:**

- The headline result: over 60% of self-reported insights were false (mean per-user accuracy 0.375 ± 0.297).
- The false discovery rate vs. false omission rate asymmetry: FDR = 0.738 (nearly 3 in 4 positive claims were wrong) vs. FOR = 0.018 (almost every "no relationship" claim was right) — the errors are almost entirely false alarms, not missed signals.
- The opening dice game: win probability starts at 1/36 per roll but climbs above 94% after just 100 rolls — a crisp numeric anchor for "keep looking long enough and you'll 'win' eventually, on pure chance."
- Jean's USB-drive "insight": a completely spurious finding (the underlying dataset was pure uniform noise with no real year-to-year signal at all) that felt compelling enough to justify sending an expensive gift again.
- Kendra's multiple-hypothesis-corrected re-test of Jean's insight: the seemingly-significant single-year p-value of 0.028 becomes a non-significant 0.306 once all ten of Jean's implicit year-by-year comparisons are properly accounted for with Benjamini-Hochberg correction.
- The paper's own framing of the asymmetry between statistics and visual analysis: in classic MCP an analyst "wins" by finding a false pattern; here, by direct analogy, an analyst effectively "loses" (is misled) by observing an interesting-looking but spurious pattern — the more visualizations examined, the higher the risk.

**Caveats:** This is a controlled lab study with synthetic data, not a study of real analysts working with real data in the wild — the tool used (a Vizdom/PanoramicData-derived prototype) is not the specific tool students may use, and the 28-participant sample (mostly undergraduates from one research university) limits generalizability of the exact 60%/37.5% numbers to other populations, tools, or task framings. The paper's definition of "insight" is deliberately narrow (data-derived observations/hypotheses/generalizations only, explicitly excluding claims drawn from prior knowledge or domain expertise) — the authors flag this themselves as a simplification; real insights in professional settings are often broader and harder to score against a binary ground truth. Neither dataset type nor size significantly affected accuracy in this study, which is a null result worth noting rather than over-interpreting (the study wasn't necessarily powered to detect such effects). The proposed remedy that's most practical without new data — reconstructing and formally correcting for every comparison an analyst made — depends on the analyst accurately remembering or logging everything they looked at, which the paper itself notes is a real practical burden, not a free lunch.
