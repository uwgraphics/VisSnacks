# Borgo, Kehrer, Chung, Maguire, Laramee, Hauser, Ward & Chen, "Glyph-based Visualization: Foundations, Design Guidelines, Techniques and Applications" (Eurographics 2013 State of the Art Report, pp. 39–63, DOI:10.2312/conf/EG2013/stars/039-063)

**What it is:** A Eurographics STAR (State of the Art Report) — a comprehensive survey/tutorial, not a single study or system paper. It grew out of a 2012 Workshop on Glyph-based Visualization (keynoted by Colin Ware and Sine McDougall) and is explicitly written to fill gaps its authors see in the existing literature: no prior survey connected glyph design to the broader theory of signs (semiotics), collected the scattered design guidelines into one place, or surveyed applications across the full breadth of domains where glyphs are used. The report has five substantive parts, in this order: (2) a grounding in the history and theory of signs, semiotics and visual-variable "grammars"; (3) design criteria and guidelines, organized around a three-stage glyph-creation pipeline (data mapping → glyph mapping/instantiation → rendering) and codified into 14 numbered design guidelines (DG1–DG14); (4) a domain-by-domain survey of applications (medical, event, multi-field, geo-spatial, flow, tensor, and uncertainty visualization); and (5) a summary of findings plus an agenda for future work. A large bibliography (roughly 90 references) closes the paper and is itself a resource, since the design-guideline and application sections are essentially annotated pointers into it.

**AI summary:** The Introduction opens by defining "glyph" two ways, which the abstract flags as the paper's own organizing tension. In the **narrow** interpretation: (a.1) a glyph is a small independent visual object that depicts attributes of a data record; (a.2) glyphs are discretely placed in a display space; (a.3) glyphs are a type of *visual sign* but differ from other sign types such as icons, indices and symbols. In the **broad** interpretation: (b.1) a glyph is a small visual object that can depict attributes of a data record *or* a composition of a set of records, used independently or constructively; (b.2) glyphs can be placed independently of one another, but in some cases are spatially connected to convey topological or geometric relationships in the underlying data space; (b.3) a glyph is a type of visual sign that *can* make use of visual features of other sign types (icons, indices, symbols). The paper treats glyphs fundamentally as **dictionary-based encoding schemes** — akin historically to maritime semaphore or signal flags — and frames the whole survey around the design of glyphs as visual channels (shape, colour, texture, size, orientation, aspect ratio, curvature) that let a viewer perform "visual fusion" of multiple data attributes into one recognisable, individually distinguishable mark.

Section 2 (History and Related Concepts) traces "glyph" to the Greek *glyphē* ("carving"), historically tied to etymology, archaeology, topography and graphonomics, but shares attributes with the modern visualization usage: small, visual, meaningful, requiring learning, often metaphoric. §2.1 gives a compressed history of sign systems — Paleolithic cave paintings, Neolithic petroglyphs (incised in rock; *petra* = stone + *glyphein* = to carve), pictograms/pictographs (visual representations of objects, places, activities), ideograms (graphical symbols representing "ideas," e.g. wayfinding signage, Arabic numerals), and logograms (the grapheme unit of a written language, e.g. cuneiform, Chinese characters, Egyptian hieroglyphs, which the paper notes mix phonetic glyphs, logographs and ideograms). §2.2 (Functional Space) grounds the discussion in **Peirce's theory of signs**: a sign is a *stimulus pattern* associated with a *meaning*, and depending on how that meaning is associated with the pattern, a sign is classified as an **icon** (physical correlation/resemblance to its object — pictures, diagrams, metaphors), an **index** (space/time correlation — smoke as a sign of fire, a clock, a fuel gauge, a medical symptom), or a **symbol** (metaphysical/conventional correlation only — words, trophies, medals, phrases; symbols alone require an interpreter but need no physical or spatiotemporal link to their referent). The functional domain of icons further splits into images, metaphors, and diagrams (sharing sensory, representative, or structural/relational similarity respectively); indices into tracks, symptoms and designations. §2.2.4 discusses **codes** — the deterministic functional relation between a signifier and signified that lets a sign carry meaning at all, illustrated with the Pioneer 10 spacecraft plaque as an attempt at cross-species pictorial communication — and Chandler's three-way classification into **social codes** (verbal language, bodily codes, commodity codes, behavioural codes), **textual codes** (scientific, aesthetic, genre/rhetorical, mass-media codes), and **interpretive codes** (ideological codes; perceptual codes, i.e. visual perception itself as a semiotic code that must be learned through culture and context). §2.3 (Theoretic Frameworks) contrasts the two classic **semiotic models**: Saussure's **dyadic model** (a sign = signifier [sound/form] + signified [concept]) and Peirce's **triadic model** (sign vehicle/representamen + object + interpretant, viewed as "the most fundamental division of signs" by most semioticians). It then covers **semiotic algebra** (Goguen's formal system decomposing a sign into hierarchically-organised "sorts," e.g. colour, location, size, combined via constructor rules into complex signs — used as an example the priority ordering of pollutants by toxicity in a lake visualization) and **semiotic grammar**: **Bertin's** foundational contribution of six visual primitives/"retinal variables" (size, colour hue, colour value, grain, orientation, shape), each rated for appropriateness to numerical, ordinal, or categorical data, followed by **MacEachren's** extension adding clarity/fuzziness, resolution, and transparency (Figure 5 of the paper), with a three-step (good/marginal/poor) rating scheme per data type, and a nod to **Mackinlay's** 1986 APT system as an early demonstration of automating graphical-presentation design from such a visual-variable syntax.

Section 3 (Design Criteria and Guidelines) is the paper's theoretical/methodological core and is organized in two halves: §3.1 "Design Space" (perception, channels, criteria, and process frameworks drawn from the wider literature) and §§3.2–3.7 "General Design Considerations and Usage Guidelines for Glyphs" (the paper's own synthesis into DG1–DG14, organized by pipeline stage). §3.1.1 reviews **Gestalt principles of perceptual organisation** as the basis of a "perceptual code": proximity (closer objects perceived as more related — shown stronger than colour similarity), similarity, continuity (elements on a line/curve perceived as related; stronger than colour similarity), closure (elements grouped into a single recognisable pattern), symmetry, figure/ground (surroundedness, size, symmetry, parallelism, extremal edges determine what reads as figure vs. background), and prägnanz (ambiguous stimuli are resolved to the simplest, most stable interpretation). §3.1.2 defines a **visual channel** (also called retinal variable, visual encoding variable) and gives Cleveland & McGill's classic ranking of quantitative-channel accuracy (position, length, angle, slope, area, volume, colour/density) and Mackinlay's 13-channel extension. It then presents **Table 1**, a synthesis (credited to Chen & Floridi) of over 30 visual channels into four categories: **Geometric** (size/length/width/depth/area/volume, orientation/slope, angle, shape, curvature, smoothness), **Optical** (intensity/brightness, colour hue/saturation, opacity/transparency, texture, line style, focus/blur, shading/lighting, shadow, depth cues, motion blur/animation/flicker), **Topological and Relational** (spatial location, connection, node/terminator, intersection/overlap, depth ordering/partial occlusion, closure/containment, distance/density), and **Semantic** (number, text, symbol/ideogram, sign/icon/logo/glyph/pictogram, isotype) — the paper notes that despite this rich channel vocabulary, only a small subset has actually been exploited in glyph design so far, meaning the design space remains "far from being fully explored." Established pop-out-effect ordering across channels is given as colour ≺ size ≺ shape ≺ orientation (≺ read as "precedes" / is more noticeable than), with colour's dominance flagged as much stronger than the other three.

§3.1.3 (Design Criteria) surveys higher-level frameworks for *why* a design choice is good. Eco's semiotic theory calls for codes governing both how signs are produced/interpreted and how they communicate. Yousef's five criteria for visual metaphors, abbreviated **CARSE**: **C**ontextual suitability (does the sign resemble its source domain given the context of use), **A**pplicability of structure (relevance of the metaphor to the new/unfamiliar concept — related to Tversky & Gati's "Within-Domain Distance" and Lakoff's "Invariance Principle"), **R**epresentability/imagery (how easily the metaphor can actually be depicted), **S**alience imbalance (per Ortony: good metaphors have source-domain traits that are highly salient but subtle in the target domain, and the visual representation should convey those salient source traits), and **E**motional tone (emotions triggered by the metaphor as an indicator of its semantic efficacy). The paper then lists guidelines credited to Maguire et al. and Bertin under explicit "Guideline on..." headers: **Semantic Relevance** (Bertin's four associative/selective/ordered/quantitative criteria for matching a channel to a data type, later shown by other researchers to be modifiable through practice/familiarity); **Channel Composition** (integral vs. separable dimension pairs — integrated channels combine via Euclidean distance √(d²ₐ+d²_b), separable channels combine via city-block distance dₐ+d_b, so separable retinal variables cost less to read and support more independent, non-conflicting encodings); **Pop-out Effects** (the colour ≺ size ≺ shape ≺ orientation ordering above); and **Visual Hierarchy** (top-down/global, bottom-up/local, middle-out, and salient-feature processing — since glyphs are small relative to a whole visualization, top-down and salient-feature detection dominate glyph selection). Maguire et al.'s own contribution beyond Bertin is the importance of a **metaphoric association** between a visual channel and the concept it encodes, i.e. natural mappings that make a visualization easier to learn/remember with less effort — but a cited study (referenced as [BARM*12]) found that *realistic* metaphors (high visual detail) can actually hurt visual-search performance and need higher pixel resolution to keep their discriminating power, a caution against over-realism. Also in §3.1.3, **Chung et al.'s eight criteria** for glyph design (Figure 7 of the paper) are given in detail: **typedness** (does each visual channel match the data type — nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio/directional — being encoded), **visual orderability** (an orderable data variable should map to a visually orderable channel, e.g. size or greyscale, not an arbitrary shape set), **channel capacity** (the number of distinguishable values a channel can encode, limited by glyph size and just-noticeable-difference effects), **separability** (minimising interference when multiple primitive channels — e.g. intensity, hue, saturation — are combined into one compound channel), **searchability** (ease of locating a specific value of a specific channel within a glyph), **learnability** (aided by well-defined constructive rules, memorable metaphors, guessability), **attention-balance** (matching a channel's visual salience to the importance of the variable it encodes), and **focus+context** (the ability to highlight a currently-relevant channel, e.g. when a user selects a sort key). §3.1.4 (Design Processes) cites Petterson's four categories of visual-design principle (functional: focus/structure/clarity/simplicity/emphasis/unity; administrative: accessibility/cost/ethics/quality; aesthetic: harmony/proportion; cognitive: facilitating attention/perception/mental-processing/memory) and Karve & Gleicher's three considerations for complex/compound glyph design (integral-separable dimension pairs, natural mappings, perceptually efficient encoding — with horizontal bars on a shared positional scale found most accurate, then interval length, slope, area, volume, colour, in decreasing order). A subsection on **Measurements and Norms** (§3.1.4.1) covers McDougall et al.'s normative ratings for symbol design along five characteristics — **concreteness** (vs. abstraction; concrete symbols are more visually obvious and easier to comprehend but design guidance often still favours simplicity), **visual complexity**, **meaningfulness**, **familiarity** (frequency of encounter; strongly predicts usability and improves with learning), and **semantic/articulatory distance** (closeness between symbol and referent) — plus three ways these have been measured in the literature: subjective rating, icon-based metrics (summing an icon's components: letters, lines, arrows), and automated visual measurement (via image-analysis features like edge/perimeter detection). Discriminability, distinctiveness and configurality are flagged as harder to quantify since they're only meaningful relative to a whole display's other symbols.

§§3.2–3.7 walk through a three-stage glyph-creation **pipeline** (Figure 8, credited to Lie, Kehrer & Hauser): (a) **data mapping** — each data variable passes through windowing (clamping/rescaling a user-selected data range to [0,1]), optional exponentiation (e(x) = x^γ, γ<1 emphasises small values, γ>1 emphasises large values), and mapping (restricting/transforming the output range, e.g. reversed mapping so important values get an enhanced visual style); (b) **glyph mapping/instantiation** — the windowed, mapped data variables are assigned to specific visual channels of the glyph and used to instantiate individual glyph instances; (c) **rendering** — glyphs are placed into the resulting image, where occlusion, clutter, and depth perception must be managed. Fourteen numbered design guidelines are distributed across this pipeline. Before the pipeline stages proper: **[DG1] Task-based choice of visualization space** — the choice between 2D, 3D, or "2.5D" (glyphs placed on 3D surfaces) should generally follow the task, though volumetric/flow data often inherently calls for 3D. **[DG2] Task-based compromise between complexity and density** — designs span a spectrum from dense fields of simple glyphs (e.g. stick figures forming textural patterns, Pickett & Grinstein) to sparse placements of a few very complex glyphs (e.g. de Leeuw & van Wijk's local flow probe, which can depict 6–9 attributes at once); Table 2 of the paper scores 20 named techniques on this density-vs-complexity spectrum from −3 (individual complex instances) to +3 (dense simple arrangements). **[DG3] Hybrid visualizations** — combining glyphs with other techniques (isosurfaces, volume rendering) to supply spatial context, per Ropinski et al.

At the **data mapping** stage (§3.4), dimension-ordering strategy matters because permuting variable order changes which patterns are salient (illustrated with Figures 10–11: random vs. sorted ordering of a 10-country exchange-rate time series — sorting by first-year value exposes trends and anomalies invisible under random order). Four ordering strategies are given: **correlation-driven** (order by similarity/correlation measures to reveal clusters, outliers, gradual relationship shifts); **complexity/symmetry-driven** (Gestalt-motivated preference for simple, symmetric shapes — Peng et al.'s automatic reordering of star-glyph dimensions to maximise monotonicity and symmetry, shown in user studies to improve performance); **data-driven** (order dimensions by the values of one base record — e.g. sorting countries by relative exchange rate in year one); and **user-driven** (manual ordering by derivative relations, semantic similarity, or task importance). Lie et al.'s own three-step data-mapping process (windowing → exponentiation → mapping, matching Figure 8a) is presented as the concrete instantiation of these ideas.

At the **glyph mapping/instantiation** stage (§3.5), guidelines continue: **[DG4] Perceptually uniform glyph properties** — equal distances in data space should be perceived as equal in the glyph (e.g. box-plot height/position, or size mappings that avoid the classic non-uniform trap of mapping a value directly to a circle's radius, which grows its *area* non-linearly — Li et al.'s study on symbol-size perception, ranking size as the second most dominant channel after colour, and flagging non-perceptually-uniform channels like the rainbow colour map as a related negative example). **[DG5] Redundant mapping of variables** (per Ward): one-to-one (each variable → one channel), one-to-many (redundant encoding across multiple channels to reduce information loss, also useful as a design guideline in its own right), and many-to-one (multiple variables sharing the same channel type, e.g. bar height across a histogram of profile glyphs). **[DG6] Importance-based mapping** (per Ropinski et al.) — more important variables should get enhanced, redundant, or more prominent (colour/size/opacity) encodings to guide the user's focus of attention; Ropinski et al.'s surface glyphs for PET data use an inverse mapping so low-activity regions are rendered thin and high-activity regions thick; Maguire et al. propose building an algorithmic taxonomy from qualitative terms to determine channel strength by term-use frequency. §3.5.1 (Shape Design) distinguishes **basic glyph shapes** (superquadrics such as spheres and tori — perceived pre-attentively, so best for the most important information) from **composite shapes** combining multiple basic shapes (perceived attentively/sequentially, e.g. Chernoff faces mapping data to eye/nose/mouth features, or the Glyphmaker and Kraus & Ertl tools letting users map variables to composite glyph properties). **[DG7] View point independence** — 3D glyph shapes should be unambiguously perceivable regardless of viewing direction (Lie et al. recommend 2D billboard glyphs to sidestep this, except where 3D orientation carries semantic meaning, e.g. flow-field arrows). **[DG8] Simplicity and Symmetry** — Gestalt-motivated; simple/symmetric shapes ease pattern perception and highlight minor changes/outliers (Peng et al.'s reordering for symmetric star glyphs; Lie et al.'s horizontally-symmetric superellipse-based glyphs, chosen to aid mental reconstruction when part of the glyph is occluded). **[DG9] Orthogonality and Normalization** — different glyph properties should be perceivable/reconstructible independently of each other (a challenge, since e.g. mapping a variable to shape inevitably also changes area, which then needs to be normalised out — not always fully achievable, since shape interpretation is usually influenced by size). §3.5.2 covers other visual properties: pre-attentive channels (position, width, size, orientation, curvature, colour/hue, intensity) per Cleveland & McGill's original accuracy ranking (position on common scale > position on non-aligned scale > length > angle/slope > area > volume > shading/colour saturation), Ward's "proximity biases" (adjacent glyph properties are easier to compare than non-adjacent ones) and "grouped" biases (semantically/perceptually grouped properties, e.g. the ears or eyes in a Chernoff face, are easier to distinguish variation in than ungrouped ones), and Healey & Enns's simple multi-dimensional texture patterns ("pexels") using colour and equally-distinguishable texture dimensions (density, regularity, height). **[DG10] Intuitive mapping based on semantics** — incorporate metaphoric/natural mappings (Crawfis & Max's small coloured vector-velocity glyphs combined with cloudiness contour surfaces; a diverging colour map with white at 0°C, blue for negative, red for positive temperatures). §3.5.3 (Glyph Placement) reviews Ward's **data-driven** (glyphs placed directly by data values/dimensions, e.g. scatterplot position) vs. **structure-driven** (placement by ordering/hierarchy/relationships among dimensions) placement, plus **feature-driven** placement (Ropinski et al.'s proposed third category for medical data — placing glyphs on local data features like isosurfaces) and **user-driven** manual placement (de Leeuw & van Wijk's local probe; Treinish's task-specific design). Glyph-packing methods (Kindlmann & Westin's particle-system packing for densely-arranged superquadric tensor glyphs; Meyer-Spradow et al.'s relaxation-based even distribution of surface glyphs) are given as ways to avoid unwanted overlap. **[DG11] Balanced glyph placement** — avoid overlap/aggregation artefacts from naive regular-grid placement, which can be mistaken for real data features (Laidlaw et al.'s random jittering when placing brush strokes as a mitigation).

The **rendering** stage (§3.6) covers three further guidelines: **[DG12] Facilitate depth perception for 3D visualizations** — halos help disambiguate overlapping glyphs and depth ordering (Lie et al.; Piringer et al.; Interrante & Grosch); chroma-depth colour maps can help with non-overlapping glyphs, though the paper flags this as "questionable" given colour is already a dominant, often-overloaded channel. **[DG13] Avoid occlusion by interactive slicing or brushing** — view-dependent slice-based visualization (hiding glyphs in front of a user-controlled plane) or linking/brushing across coordinated multiple views to filter glyphs by criteria (Kehrer et al.). **[DG14] Avoid perspective projections when using glyph size to encode a data variable** — orthographic projection is preferable, since perspective distorts size comparisons across different depths (Ropinski et al.). §3.7 (Glyph Interaction) closes the design section with two worked interactive systems: Legg et al.'s **MatchPad**, an interactive glyph-based sports notational-analysis tool (developed for Rugby) using a scale-adaptive layout and video linking, chosen because the limited encoding capacity of a single glyph makes it impractical to map an entire video clip's worth of tracking data onto it directly; and Chung et al.'s system extending this with **focus+context** interaction — an Interactive, Multidimensional Glyph (IMG) plot arranging glyphs along two primary sorting axes, with brushing, pan-and-zoom, and optional connectivity-line display.

**Table 2** (a full-page table) is a compact cross-reference categorising roughly 20 named papers/techniques (e.g. Crawfis & Max's vector field visualization, Kindlmann & Westin's glyph packing, Pickett & Grinstein's stick figures, de Leeuw & van Wijk's local probe, Kindlmann's superquadric tensor glyphs, McGill et al.'s box-plot variations, Ward & Guo's shape space projections) against which of the 14 design guidelines (DG1–DG13, plus visualization space and complexity/density scoring) and which visual channels (colour, shape, size/height/length, orientation, texture, opacity) each technique actually uses — functioning as a reference grid for "who does what" rather than prose.

Section 4 (Application) is a domain-by-domain survey, opening with the observation that generic glyphs (star glyphs, Chernoff faces) are well-studied but limited for challenging, complex data types (vector, tensor), so many glyph designs are necessarily attribute/domain-dependent. **§4.1 Medical Visualization**: Ropinski & Preim's survey and taxonomy (pre-attentive vs. attentive processing, per semiotic theory) frames the subsection; Westin et al. derive quantitative diffusion-tensor shape measures (c_l, c_p, c_s — linear, planar, spherical) from the Stejskal-Tanner equation and build composite tensor glyphs blended from sphere/disc/rod shapes, colour-coded blue (linear)/yellow (planar)/red (spherical); Oeltze et al. present two glyph designs for cardiac perfusion — a 3D Bull's Eye Plot (BEP) Segment and 3D Time-Intensity Curve (TIC) Miniatures encoding Peak Enhancement, Time To Peak, Integral, and Up-slope, applied to three clinical datasets; Meyer-Spradow et al.'s SPECT myocardial-perfusion glyphs use a supertorus shape characterised by colour, opacity, size, and roundness, positioned via random distribution + relaxation to avoid the unbalanced-placement problem of DG11. **§4.2 Event Visualization**: Botchen et al.'s VideoPerpetuoGram (VPG) stacks temporally-spaced key video frames with colour-filled glyphs encoding geometric, semantic, and statistical (certainty/error-margin) information for surveillance-footage analysis; Pearlman & Rheingans use compound pie-chart glyphs (size/colour mapped to activity amount and service type) as network-security nodes, sliceable into concentric temporal layers; Parry et al.'s video storyboard tackles event classification/selection/illustration for Snooker video, using ball glyphs varying in size/opacity/colour for trajectory, ball type, event importance, plus textured circle and numbered-icon glyphs for shot sequencing; Legg et al.'s MatchPad (detailed above, Figure 12 shows six metaphoric pictogram design iterations for Rugby scrums — from initial stickmen to a final silhouette design) is the subsection's most developed example, explicitly using metaphoric icons because they are quicker to learn/remember/guess than abstract encodings, at the cost of struggling to scale to sport's many categorical event types. **§4.3 Multi-field Visualization**: Barr's superquadrics paper (torus, star-shape, ellipsoid, hyperboloid, toroid, plus angle-preserving bend/twist transforms) is the foundational geometric-primitive reference used across multiple later subsections; Crawfis & Allison combine texture/raster overlay operations with glyph textures for scientific multi-dataset visualization; Shaw et al. extend Barr's superquadrics into a multi-dimensional glyph framework mapping data importance to location/size/colour/shape in decreasing order, demonstrated on solar-wind magnetohydrodynamics simulation; Taylor's survey of scalar-field visualization on 2D manifolds finds a ceiling of about four scalar fields depictable per unified visualization, motivating data-driven spots (DDS, varying dot intensity/height) and oriented-slivers (sliver-shaped texture glyphs with luminance mapped to scalar value) as two techniques that push past that limit; Kirby & Laidlaw borrow oil-painting concepts for multi-layer, minimally-overlapping visualization, extending Laidlaw et al.'s stochastic brush-stroke placement, applied to simulated 2D flow past a cylinder, depicting 6–9 attributes at once. **§4.4 Geo-spatial Visualization**: MacEachren et al. visualize mortality-map reliability via bivariate colour-filled/textured region overlay on a US base map; Healey & Enns's "pexels" (multi-coloured perceptual texture elements, height/density/regularity dimensions) are applied to typhoon data (wind speed, pressure, precipitation); Pang identifies two uncertainty-integration strategies (mapping uncertainty to graphic attributes like hue/opacity, or using animation) and surveys cartographic uncertainty-glyph techniques; Sanyal et al. use glyphs, ribbons and spaghetti plots to visualize ensemble uncertainty (mean, standard deviation, IQR, 95% confidence intervals) in numerical weather models, demonstrated on the 1933 Superstorm simulation. **§4.5 Flow Visualization**: de Leeuw & van Wijk's local flow probe glyph (Figure 9d) depicts six flow characteristics at once — velocity and curvature (via a curved vector arrow's length/direction/curvedness), acceleration (displacement toward the arrow centre), torsion (candy-stripe surface texture), a shear-plane ring, and convergence/divergence (a lens/osculating paraboloid) — interactively probed along a streamline; Dovey extends Crawfis & Max's method to curvilinear/unstructured grids via physical- or parameter-space resampling; Martin et al. empirically validate 2D wind-barb glyphs for hurricane visualization, finding users can estimate both point values and regional averages of magnitude/direction using a real hurricane dataset; Peng et al. present an automatic vector-field clustering algorithm feeding statistical multivariate glyphs — a |v|-range "disc" glyph (inner/outer radius = local min/max vector magnitude) and a θ-range cone glyph (mean direction + variance as a semi-transparent cone), demonstrated on synthetic and real CFD meshes (Figure 13, an engine-flow clustering visualization); Hlawatsch et al.'s "flow-radar" glyphs statically depict unsteady flow by mapping time-dependent vector attributes to polar coordinates (direction→angle, time→radius, magnitude→colour), plus an uncertainty arc for angular variation. **§4.6 Tensor Visualization**: Laidlaw et al. present normalised ellipsoid glyphs for DTI (principal axes/radii mapped to eigenvectors/eigenvalues, normalised to reduce clutter) and, separately, a painting-inspired method layering up to seven tensor attributes as multiple oil-painting-style brush strokes, both demonstrated on healthy and diseased mouse spinal cords; Kindlmann's superquadric tensor glyphs use explicit/implicit superquadric parameterisation plus anisotropy metrics c_l, c_p, c_s and a user-controlled edge-sharpness parameter γ to form a barycentric triangular domain of shapes varying by flatness/orientation, compared against equivalent ellipsoid visualizations on DT-MRI data; Kriz et al. review second-order tensor techniques (Lamé's stress ellipsoids, Haber glyphs, Reynolds tensor glyph, hyperstreamtubes) and introduce a Principal Normal and Shear (PNS) glyph extending stress ellipsoids by mapping shear to surface colour; Kindlmann's later glyph-packing work (particle-system potential-energy placement) improves on regular-grid glyph sampling; Hlawitschka et al. offer a faster Delaunay-triangulation-based alternative for glyph packing; Schultz & Kindlmann extend superquadric tensor glyphs to general symmetric (non-positive-definite) second-order tensors, using convex/concave shape between eigenvectors to indicate sign differences; Chen et al. introduce a hybrid asymmetric-tensor-field visualization combining hyperstreamlines (in simple regions) and elliptical glyphs (in complex regions), mapping tensor magnitude to hyperstreamline density and glyph size. **§4.7 Uncertainty Visualization**: Wittenbrink et al.'s early work evaluates glyphs for wind/ocean-current uncertainty, encoding direction, magnitude and mean-direction/length uncertainty via glyph length, area, colour and/or angle simultaneously; Lodha et al.'s UFLOW system visualizes differences between flow streamlines computed with different integrators/step-sizes, using glyphs to encode shape/size/colour differences; Pang et al. and Verma & Pang compare streamlines/stream-ribbons between a dataset and a sub-sampled version via Euclidean distance-based glyphs; Brown demonstrates animated visual "vibrations" (oscillating vertex displacement, luminance/hue change) as an uncertainty-encoding technique; MacEachren et al. empirically test abstract single-variable symbols (shape, hue, orientation) against more pictorial/iconic symbols for uncertainty, measuring both intuitiveness and task effectiveness in a map-use context (building on Bertin's semiotic framework); Ribicic et al.'s sketch-based system lets users sketch boundary conditions for a flood-management simulation and view resulting uncertainty via vector and "ensemble handle" glyphs.

Section 5 (Summary and Conclusions) restates the paper's contributions (connecting glyph design to sign theory and perceptual studies; consolidating design criteria/guidelines; surveying visual-mapping, layout, rendering, and interaction methods; surveying applications) and lists glyph-visualization's demonstrated **merits**: rapid semantic interpretation (e.g. traffic signs); scalability across many dimensions; suitability for both dense and sparse arrangements; no significant disadvantage for learning/memorization versus other sign forms; capacity to evolve into a standard schematic form (like many diagram conventions); and capacity to evolve into a full visual "language" (grapheme-based, as writing systems did). It also names an explicit gap: the community has expressed doubts about glyphs' encoding capability, largely because of size limits on individual visual channels and the cognitive demand of learning/memorising glyph mappings — reasonable concerns that don't undermine glyphs' relative merits, but that existing perception studies mostly only validate for three or fewer dimensions, leading the authors to call for more empirical studies of *high-dimensional* glyph representations specifically. The closing paragraph reiterates the dictionary-based-encoding framing and suggests visualization researchers could draw more directly on data communication, information theory, data compression, and lexicography, closing with the aspirational note that glyph-based encoding schemes may one day evolve into "the basis of a common visualization language."

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The narrow-vs-broad definition split (a.1–a.3 vs. b.1–b.3) is a genuinely useful vocabulary check before calling *anything* a "glyph": narrowly, glyphs are independent, discretely-placed marks distinct from icons/indices/symbols; broadly, they can represent aggregates of records, be spatially connected to convey topology, and freely borrow visual features from icons/indices/symbols. Most real systems use the broad sense; the paper is explicit that both are in active use.
- Peirce's icon/index/symbol triad (resemblance / causal-spatial correlation / pure convention) is a compact, reusable lens for critiquing *any* visual encoding choice, not just glyphs — worth pairing with Mackinlay's expressiveness/effectiveness criteria as a complementary "why does this mark mean what it means" question.
- The four-category visual-channel taxonomy (Geometric / Optical / Topological-Relational / Semantic, Table 1) is a useful checklist when designing a multi-attribute glyph — a reminder that channel choice extends well beyond the classic Bertin six.
- Chung et al.'s eight glyph-design criteria (typedness, visual orderability, channel capacity, separability, searchability, learnability, attention-balance, focus+context; Figure 7) is the paper's most directly actionable checklist — genuinely usable as a design or critique rubric for a new glyph.
- The three-stage pipeline (data mapping → glyph mapping/instantiation → rendering) and its associated windowing/exponentiation/mapping data-transform sequence (Figure 8) is a clean mental model for *where* in the process a given design decision (e.g. dimension ordering, occlusion handling) actually belongs.
- The 14 design guidelines (DG1–DG14) read well as a punch-list to run any glyph design against — particularly DG2 (complexity vs. density trade-off), DG9 (orthogonality — encoding shape almost always leaks into perceived size), and DG11 (regular-grid placement risks fake-looking aggregation artefacts).
- The application survey (§4) is a good index of *named, citable* glyph techniques per domain — useful for finding a canonical example (e.g. Chernoff faces, superquadric tensor glyphs, the local flow probe, MatchPad) to point to when discussing a particular data type in class.

**Skim/skip guidance:** This is explicitly a long reference document, and different sections reward different reading strategies. Section 2 (semiotics/history) rewards a careful first read once — it's the paper's distinctive contribution (no other glyph survey grounds the topic in sign theory this way) and the icon/index/symbol/code vocabulary recurs throughout the rest of the paper, but it does not need to be re-read afterward; treat it as background to internalize once. Section 3.1 (design space: Gestalt principles, visual channels, Table 1, the CARSE/Chung-et-al./Petterson criteria) is dense and citation-heavy; Table 1 and Figure 7 (Chung et al.'s eight criteria) are worth memorizing or bookmarking, but the surrounding prose citing a dozen named studies per paragraph is better skimmed for the named guideline headers than read word for word. Section 3.2–3.7 (the DG1–DG14 guidelines and pipeline) is the paper's most reusable content and worth reading start to finish once, ideally alongside Figure 8 (the pipeline diagram) and Table 2 (technique-by-guideline matrix) open side by side. Section 4 (applications) is a reference section by design — nobody should read all seven subdomains start-to-finish unless surveying the whole field; instead, jump to the one or two subsections (e.g. flow or tensor visualization) relevant to your own work and treat the rest as an index to return to later. Section 5 (conclusions) is short and worth reading for the honest self-assessment of glyphs' limits. The reference list itself (pp. 20–25 of the PDF) is unusually useful as a glyph-visualization bibliography in its own right.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Narrow-glyph definition (a.1–a.3): small independent visual object depicting a data record's attributes; discretely placed; a visual sign distinct from icons/indices/symbols. Broad-glyph definition (b.1–b.3): can depict a single record or a composition of records; can be spatially connected to convey topology/geometry; can borrow visual features of icons/indices/symbols.
- Peirce's sign triad: icon (physical-correlation resemblance), index (space/time correlation, e.g. smoke as a sign of fire, a clock, a fuel gauge), symbol (metaphysical/purely conventional correlation, e.g. a word or trophy) — symbols are the only sign type needing no physical/spatial/temporal correlation to their referent.
- Bertin's six original visual variables/retinal variables: size, colour hue, colour value, grain, orientation, shape; MacEachren adds clarity/fuzziness, resolution, transparency.
- Pop-out ordering across channels: colour ≺ size ≺ shape ≺ orientation (≺ = "precedes"/more noticeable); colour's dominance over the other three is "much more noticeable."
- Channel-composition distance metrics: integrated (integral) visual channels combine via Euclidean distance √(d²ₐ+d²_b); separable channels combine via city-block distance dₐ+d_b — separable channels are cheaper to read and preferred for effective glyph design.
- Chung et al.'s eight glyph-design criteria (Figure 7): typedness, visual orderability, channel capacity, separability, searchability, learnability, attention-balance, focus+context.
- Fourteen numbered design guidelines, DG1 ("task-based choice of visualization space") through DG14 ("avoid perspective projections when using glyph size to encode a data variable").
- Named worked examples across the report: Chernoff faces (composite, attentively-processed glyph shapes mapping variables to facial features); de Leeuw & van Wijk's local flow probe, depicting 6–9 flow attributes (velocity, curvature, acceleration, torsion, shear, convergence/divergence) in a single glyph; Legg et al.'s MatchPad for Rugby notational analysis, with six iterated metaphoric-pictogram designs shown in Figure 12; Kindlmann's superquadric tensor glyphs with anisotropy metrics c_l, c_p, c_s; Laidlaw et al.'s oil-painting-inspired DTI visualization layering up to seven tensor attributes as brush strokes; Peng et al.'s automatic vector-field-clustering |v|-range/θ-range disc-and-cone glyphs (Figure 13, engine-flow example).
- Taylor's survey finds roughly four scalar fields is the practical ceiling depictable in one unified visualization on a 2D manifold — motivating dedicated multi-field techniques (data-driven spots, oriented slivers) to exceed it.
- The report's central self-description: glyph-based visualization is fundamentally a "dictionary-based encoding scheme," historically continuous with maritime semaphore and signal flags, with the paper suggesting it could draw more on information theory, data compression, and lexicography, and might one day form the basis of "a common visualization language."
- Originated from the Workshop on Glyph-based Visualization, held 7–8 May 2012, keynoted by Colin Ware (University of New Hampshire) and Sine McDougall (Bournemouth University); partly funded by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF) ViMaL project No. P21695.

**Caveats:** This is a 2013 survey, now over a decade old — glyph-visualization research has continued since (newer perceptual studies, new tensor/uncertainty techniques, deep-learning-assisted glyph design), so the applications section should be read as a snapshot of the field circa 2012, not a current-state index; several cited works are themselves marked "to appear" at time of writing (e.g. [BARM*12], [MRSS*12], [CF12]), meaning some references were still in press. The paper is a synthesis and taxonomy exercise, not original empirical research — its own "guidelines" are compiled and paraphrased from dozens of separate sources rather than independently validated by the authors, and the authors are candid about this limit: they explicitly note (Section 5) that most existing perceptual validation of glyph channels only covers three or fewer dimensions, so guidance for genuinely high-dimensional glyph design is asserted more than proven, and they call for further empirical work rather than claiming the guidelines are settled. Scope is bounded to what the authors call glyph-based visualization proper — dense pixel/texture-based multivariate techniques and general iconography are referenced for context but not systematically surveyed. One internal tension worth flagging honestly rather than smoothing over: the paper opens by presenting the narrow and broad glyph definitions as two live, competing interpretations, but the rest of the report (design guidelines and especially the Application section) moves fluidly between both without consistently marking which sense is in play for a given technique — e.g., composite/spatially-connected glyphs (broad sense) and strictly independent discrete glyphs (narrow sense) are discussed side by side under the same guideline numbers, so a careful reader shouldn't assume DG1–DG14 apply uniformly to both interpretations. Similarly, a few guideline recommendations are presented with some internal hedging rather than as firm rules — e.g. DG9 (orthogonality/normalization) is stated as a goal that "is not always easy" to achieve, and DG12's suggestion to use chroma-depth colour maps for depth perception is itself flagged by the authors as "questionable" given colour is usually a heavily-loaded channel already; these are the paper's own caveats, not omissions on this summary's part.
