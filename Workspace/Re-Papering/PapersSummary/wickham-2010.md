# Wickham, "A Layered Grammar of Graphics" (Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics, Vol. 19, No. 1, 2010, pp. 3–28)

**What it is:** A formal grammar/specification paper that reparameterizes Leland Wilkinson's *Grammar of Graphics* (GPL) into a "layered" grammar built around the idea of a plot as one or more layers, each combining data, aesthetic mapping, statistical transformation, and a geometric object. It is not an empirical study — it's the design document for the grammar underlying the R package `ggplot2`, written by ggplot2's author, and it doubles as an explanation of ggplot2's architecture. The paper walks through building a plot by hand (§2) before presenting the grammar formally and contrasting it component-by-component with Wilkinson's grammar (§3); describes the hierarchy of defaults that makes the grammar usable in everyday practice, via a worked diamonds-dataset example and the `qplot()` shortcut (§4); discusses the consequences of embedding the grammar inside a host programming language, R, via a worked reproduction of Minard's Napoleon's-march map (§5); works through three implications of the grammar — histograms, polar coordinates, and the three places transformations can occur (§6); speculates about tools for helping users avoid common graphical mistakes, dubbed "a poetry of graphics" (§7); and closes with a candid list of the grammar's weaknesses (§8).

**AI summary:** The paper opens by framing the whole exercise as answering two questions: "What is a graphic?" and "how can we create the graphic we have described?" Following the OED's definition of grammar as "the fundamental principles or rules of an art or science," Wickham argues a grammar should reveal the composition of complicated graphics and unexpected connections between seemingly different ones (citing Cox 1978), while acknowledging — by analogy to English — that grammatical correctness doesn't guarantee a *good* graphic. He identifies Wilkinson, Anand, and Grossman's *Grammar of Graphics* (2005), itself building on Bertin (1983), as "the most important modern work in graphical grammars," and states his contribution plainly: an alternative parameterization built around layers, differing from Wilkinson's grammar in the arrangement of components, the addition of a hierarchy of defaults, and embedding inside a host language (R). These three differences structure the rest of the article.

Section 2 builds intuition with a trivial four-observation, four-variable dataset (variables A, B, C, D; Table 1). To draw a scatterplot of A versus C with D mapped to point shape, the first step is constructing a new dataset that reflects the mapping — x-position from A, y-position from C, shape from D — and dropping unused variables (Table 2); x-position, y-position, and shape are examples of **aesthetics**, "things that we can perceive on the graphic." The same specification with lines instead of points yields a line plot, with bars a bar plot — points, lines, and bars are all **geometric objects**. Next, data units must be converted to physical/aesthetic units via **scales** (here linear scales into a Cartesian coordinate system), using a formula like `floor((x - min(x))/range(x) * screen width)` — x scaled to [0, 200], y to [0, 300], shape "a"→circle and "b"→square (Table 3). A **statistical transformation** step is also, in general, needed (here it's the trivial identity transform). The final plot combines graphical objects from three sources — the geom, the scales-and-coordinate-system (which generate axes/legends), and plot annotations (background, title) — shown separately in Figure 1 and combined in Figure 2. Section 2.2 extends the example to **faceting**: splitting the previous plot by D produces small multiples (Figure 3), which requires splitting the dataset into per-facet subsets (Table 4) and reveals that scaling actually happens in three parts — transformation, training, and mapping. Scale transformation happens *before* statistical transformation (so a plot of log(x) vs. log(y) on linear scales looks identical to x vs. y on log scales — relying on the location-scale invariance of statistics). Training combines the ranges of the individual per-facet datasets into one shared range, which matters because locally-scaled facets (Table 5, where each facet independently occupies the full range) make cross-facet comparison meaningless; properly trained scales (Table 6) preserve comparability.

Section 3 formalizes the grammar's components. A plot is defined as: a default dataset and set of variable-to-aesthetic mappings; one or more **layers**, each with one geom, one stat, one position adjustment, and optionally its own dataset and mappings; one scale per aesthetic used; a coordinate system; and a facet specification. Figure 4 maps these onto Wilkinson's grammar: GPL's DATA becomes ggplot2's defaults, TRANS has "no correspondence in ggplot2: its role is played by built-in R features," ELEMENT becomes Layer (splitting out data/mapping/geom/stat/position as independent sub-parts), and SCALE/GUIDE/COORD map to Scale/Coord/Facet. The key structural difference from Wilkinson's ELEMENT is that GPL intertwines geom, stat, and mapping into one parameterization, whereas the layered grammar keeps them separate and independently omittable, relying on defaults to fill gaps (Figure 5 contrasts `line(position(smooth.linear(x*y)), color(z))` in GPL against `layer(aes(x=x, y=y, color=z), geom="line", stat="smooth")` in ggplot2). Within a layer: **data and mapping** (§3.1.1) are kept independent of other components so a graphic can be reapplied to different datasets; **statistical transformation/stat** (§3.1.2) must be location-scale invariant — f(x+a) = f(x)+a and f(b·x) = b·f(x) — and Table 7 lists nine built-in stats (bin, boxplot, contour, density, identity, jitter, qq, quantile, smooth, summary, unique), user-extensible; **geometric objects/geoms** (§3.1.3) are classified by dimensionality — 0d (point, text), 1d (path, line), 2d (polygon, interval) — and are abstract enough to be rendered multiple ways (Figure 6 shows the interval geom as a bar, a line, an error bar, or, for continuous x, a ribbon); every geom has a default stat and vice versa, each geom can only display certain aesthetics (a point geom: position/color/shape/size; a bar geom: position/height/width/fill), and geoms are parameterized by location rather than dimension internally, since dimension-based parameters (height, width) only make sense in Cartesian coordinates; **position adjustments** (§3.1.4) — stack, dodge, jitter — handle overlap, called "collision modifiers" in Wilkinson's terminology. **Scales** (§3.2) are a function-plus-inverse-plus-parameters that map data to aesthetic attributes (Figure 7 shows legends for four scale types: continuous→size, continuous→color, discrete→shape, discrete→color); the inverse draws the guide (axes for position scales, legends otherwise); scales usually map one variable to one aesthetic, but exceptions include mapping two variables to hue and saturation to build one color aesthetic, or redundantly mapping one variable to multiple aesthetics (useful for graphics that must work in both color and black-and-white). Scale corresponds to Wilkinson's combined SCALE and GUIDE; ggplot2 doesn't need Wilkinson's separate "annotation guides" because they can be built with creative use of geoms. **Coordinate systems/coord** (§3.3) map position onto the plane — Cartesian is most common in 2D, polar and map projections less so, and higher-dimensional options include parallel coordinates ("a projective geometry") and mosaic plots ("a hierarchical coordinate system"); unlike scales, coordinate systems affect all position variables simultaneously and change the *appearance* of geoms (bars become circle segments in polar coordinates), and coordinate transformation happens after statistical transformation, with consequences worked out in §6.3 (Figure 8 shows Cartesian, semi-log, and polar axes/gridlines, noting polar axes are hard to draw well). **Faceting** (§3.4) generalizes conditioned/trellis/lattice displays; in Wilkinson's grammar it's folded awkwardly into the coordinate system (the facet variable specified inside ELEMENT, with COORD separately faceting by it), whereas the layered grammar makes faceting independent of both layer and coordinate system — simpler, at the cost of always laying out facets in Cartesian space (Figure 9 compares the two parameterizations).

Section 4 demonstrates the "hierarchy of defaults" through a price-vs-carat scatterplot of 1000 diamonds (Figure 10). The fully explicit spec — `ggplot() + layer(data = diamonds, mapping = aes(x = carat, y = price), geom = "point", stat = "identity", position = "identity") + scale_y_continuous() + scale_x_continuous() + coord_cartesian()` — is progressively compressed: only one of geom/stat needs specifying since each supplies a default for the other; Cartesian coordinates are the default so needn't be named; default scales are chosen by aesthetic and variable type (continuous position values get linear scaling, categorical values get mapped to integers; continuous color gets a smooth path through HCL color space, discrete color gets evenly spaced hues at equal luminance/chroma — Wickham notes "the choice of a good default scale is difficult"). This reduces the full spec to `ggplot(diamonds, aes(carat, price)) + geom_point()`. Adding a log-log version with a linear smoother requires a second layer plus log-scale overrides: `ggplot(diamonds, aes(carat, price)) + geom_point() + stat_smooth(method = lm) + scale_x_log10() + scale_y_log10()`. Even with defaults, the grammar syntax stays verbose (spanning multiple lines), which motivates `qplot()` ("quick plot"), a shorthand that assumes shared data/mappings across layers and defaults to a scatterplot, mimicking base R's `plot()` — e.g. `qplot(carat, price, data = diamonds)` and, for the two-layer log-log version, `qplot(carat, price, data = diamonds, geom = c("point", "smooth"), method = "lm", log = "xy")`. Wickham flags qplot's own limitation directly: "to which layer does the method argument apply?"

Section 5 discusses consequences of embedding the grammar in R, illustrated by reproducing the top part of Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's 1812–1813 Russian campaign (Figure 11; troop and city data digitized in Wilkinson 2005, table 20.1, p. 624, and supplied as supplemental material). The reconstruction (Figure 12) proceeds incrementally: a `geom_path` layer on the troops data mapping size to survivors and color to direction (grouped by an "arms of the advance/retreat" grouping variable); a second `geom_text` layer for city labels; then polishing scales — `scale_size(to = c(1,10), breaks, labels = comma(...))`, `scale_color_manual(values = c("grey50","red"))`, and suppressed axis labels. This demonstrates two embedding advantages: building a plot piece-by-piece using host-language variables, and using host-language functions (like `comma()`) or loops/custom functions to encapsulate repeated tasks — ggplot2 itself includes such helper functions for more complex composite graphics like parallel-coordinates plots and scatterplot matrices. Embedding also lets ggplot2 borrow R's statistical routines (loess smoothing, quantile regression, density estimation) and its data-import/manipulation tools. Because of this, ggplot2 drops three components of Wilkinson's grammar entirely: DATA (unnecessary since data are R data frames, not a described grammar component), TRANS (variable transformation is already easy in R), and Wilkinson's ALGEBRA for reshaping data for display (replaced by the separate `reshape` package, which — unlike GPL's algebra — can also aggregate and subset, and keeps data manipulation checkable and reusable across plots). The disadvantage of embedding is subtler: grammatical restrictions from the host language. To preserve the grammar's declarative nature inside an imperative language, ggplot2 uses `+` to add plot components together, and needs a base `ggplot()` object to anchor everything else added to it — a requirement that "is not necessary in a stand-alone grammar."

Section 6 works through three implications of the layered grammar. **Histograms** (§6.1): a histogram is the combination of a binning stat and a bar geom — `ggplot(data = diamonds, mapping = aes(price)) + layer(geom = "bar", stat = "bin", mapping = aes(y = ..count..))` — and Wickham notes this spec "does not contain the word histogram" because a histogram isn't elemental, just a stat+geom combination; ggplot2 adds `geom_histogram()` as a convenience alias with a default bin stat and default count-to-height mapping, further reducible to `qplot(price, data = diamonds, geom = "histogram")`. Bin choice matters enormously (Figure 13 shows 30 default bins vs. a custom $50 bin width that reveals missing data); ggplot2's default heuristic is "very simple" — 30 bins "regardless of the data" — a deliberately "perverse" choice meant to send a message that the user must experiment, reinforced by a warning. The histogram geom is parameterized by bin *width* rather than bin count (unusual in R, but directly interpretable on the graph), with a `breaks` argument for exact control. Separating stat from geom enables variants (Figure 14): a ribbon instead of bars produces a frequency polygon, points produce an unnamed graphic. Because the histogram's y-aesthetic (bar height) isn't in the original data but is computed by the stat, ggplot2 flags it visually with double-dot syntax, `aes(y = ..count..)`, with other available computed variables including `..density..`, `..density../sum(..density..)`, and `..count../max(..count..)` (rescaling the histogram to [0,1], useful for faceted/interactive graphics). A `weight` aesthetic affects the stat rather than the display directly — it sums weights per bin instead of counting observations. **Polar coordinates** (§6.2): parameterizes the plane via angle θ and radius r, with x = r·cos(θ), y = r·sin(θ); either variable can be mapped to angle or radius, and a nonzero minimum radius avoids compression near the origin. Angle mapping suits cyclical data (times of day, wind direction) but risks missing patterns that straddle the arbitrary start/end point. In the grammar, a pie chart is simply a stacked bar geom in polar coordinates — `ggplot(diamonds, aes(x = "", fill = clarity)) + geom_bar(width = 1) + coord_polar(theta = "y")` — and changing only `theta` to `"x"` produces a "bullseye plot," described as the polar equivalent of the spineplot: the pie chart holds radius constant and varies angle by category, the bullseye/radial chart holds angle constant and varies radius (Figure 15, using diamond clarity from I1, worst, to IF, best). Converting an ordinary bar chart (clarity on x instead of a constant) into polar coordinates instead produces the Coxcomb plot, "popularized by Florence Nightingale" (1857) (Figure 16); the caption notes that categories abut in the Coxcomb but are separated by gaps in the Cartesian bar chart — "an example of a graphical convention that differs in different coordinate systems." **Transformations** (§6.3): there are three places a transformation can occur — the data, the scale, or the coordinate system. Transforming data versus transforming the scale look nearly identical (Figure 17: log10(carat)/log10(price) plotted directly, versus raw carat/price on log-scaled axes with a linear smoother) because stats operate on scale-transformed data, but the two are not identical — minor gridlines and axis labeling differ, and Wickham states scale transformation is preferable because axes are then labeled in original data units, "easier to interpret," while noting "the presentation of the labels still requires work." Transforming the coordinate system instead is qualitatively different (Figure 18): it happens at the very end of the drawing process and distorts geom shape — a smoother fit as a straight line on raw data gets stretched into a curve once drawn in log coordinates, which "demonstrates why fitting a linear model to the untransformed data is such a bad idea" when the plot itself is in transformed coordinates. Figure 19 combines scale and coordinate transformation to fit a model on logged data and then back-transform it onto the original scale, equivalent to fitting log(y)~log(x) and back-transforming predictions. Wickham flags an open problem: tick placement currently differs between the two approaches even though "arguably" it shouldn't, since concealing whether a transform was applied to scale or coordinate system "raises an interesting philosophical question: should we be able to uniquely identify the specification of a graphic from its rendering?" — left unresolved.

Section 7, "A Poetry of Graphics?" (title's question mark is Wickham's own), is explicitly speculative discussion, framed by analogy to word-processor writing aids: ggplot2 already behaves like a spellchecker (a malformed spec simply won't run), which raises the question of a "grammar checker" that flags common graphical mistakes — a "helpful first step" that "will not significantly improve your prose" and can sometimes misfire. Named detectable problems and possible fixes: too many variables (hard to read more than three in one panel — suggest faceting); overplotting (suggest density contours or point-density coloring, citing Unwin et al. 1996, Cleveland and McGill 1984, Carr et al. 1987, and Huang, McDonald, and Stuetzle 1997); alphabetical ordering of categorical variables, which Wainer (2004) named "Alabama first!" (suggest ordering by a data property like mean or median, citing Becker, Cleveland, and Shyu 1996 and Friendly and Kwan 2003); and polar coordinates (citing Cleveland and McGill 1987 that people judge length more accurately than angle or area, and noting polar's singularity at the origin makes angle judgment hard for small-radius objects — posing, not answering, "should we always warn users when they choose to use this coordinate system?"). The section also floats automatic exploration aids: scagnostics (Wilkinson, Anand, and Grossman 2005) and projection pursuit (Friedman 1987; Cook et al. 2008) to surface interesting views of scatterplot-projection space; exploring other parameter spaces such as aspect ratio via banking-to-45° (Heer and Agrawala 2006, following Cleveland 1993); and showing several revealing histogram bin widths rather than one "optimal" choice. Wickham concludes candidly that "it seems difficult to see how to do much more algorithmically," pointing instead to education: the grammar's real value may be pedagogical, helping students see the deeper themes connecting different graphics.

Section 8's conclusion revisits Cox's (1978) framing of a grammar's aim — to "bring together in a coherent way things that previously appeared unrelated and which also will provide a basis for dealing systematically with new situations" — and assesses success against it via the paper's own examples: the histogram example decomposed a familiar graphic into independently variable parts; the polar-coordinate examples revealed the shared structure underlying bar charts, pie charts, and Coxcomb plots, and "accidentally" produced the bullseye plot as a new, previously unnamed graphic; the transformation examples showed how to reproduce statistical back-transformation using graphics components alone. Three weaknesses are named explicitly: (1) area plots — bar charts, spineplots, histograms, mosaic plots — are a family the grammar doesn't illuminate well; it can construct them but gives "no great insight into their underlying structure," suggesting "subgrammars" for particular plot families as future work (citing Hartigan and Kleiner 1981 and Hofmann 2003 on mosaic plots); (2) the grammar has no interaction model — "all plots are static and separate" — some interaction (e.g., a slider controlling histogram bin width, zooming/panning) would be easy to add, but linked brushing is fundamentally about interacting with underlying data and "will require deeper thought," and speed is a real obstacle since an interactive graphic "must be updated multiple times a second," whereas "many ggplot2 graphics take over a second to draw"; (3) the grammar gives no guidance for visualizing non-data-frame objects (mixed-effects models, MANOVAs, clusterings, classifications) — existing default visualizations from R packages are inflexible, and hand-assembling a custom ggplot2 graphic "quickly grows tiresome," with partial thoughts pointed to in Wickham's 2009 ggplot2 book. The closing line: the grammar is "powerful and useful, but not all encompassing." **What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The five-part decomposition — default data+mapping, one or more layers (each geom+stat+position, optionally its own data/mapping), one scale per aesthetic, coordinate system, facet specification — is a reusable checklist for describing or diagnosing *any* static statistical graphic, not just ones built in ggplot2.
- Many "named" chart types are not primitives but *compositions*: a histogram is bin-stat + bar-geom; a pie chart is stacked-bar-geom + polar coordinates; a Coxcomb plot is an ordinary bar chart in polar coordinates; a bullseye plot is a pie chart with angle and radius swapped. Seeing chart types this way — rather than as a fixed taxonomy — is the paper's central, most transferable move.
- The location-scale invariance requirement for stats (f(x+a) = f(x)+a, f(b·x) = b·f(x)) is a crisp, checkable criterion for "is this transformation legitimate to compose with a plot's scales" — useful well beyond ggplot2.
- Transformation is not one operation but three, with different consequences: transforming the *data*, transforming the *scale* (nearly equivalent to the data case, but axes stay labeled in original units), and transforming the *coordinate system* (applied last, and it distorts the shape of already-drawn geoms — e.g., a fitted straight line becomes visibly curved).
- Aesthetics are the connecting abstraction between data and geometry — a small, fixed vocabulary (x/y position, color, shape, size, label, etc.) that decouples "what varies in the data" from "how it's drawn," and that any geom can display only a subset of.
- Position adjustment (stack/dodge/jitter) is its own grammar component, separable from the geom itself — a reminder that overlap-handling is a distinct design decision.
- Defaults form a genuine hierarchy (a geom implies a default stat and vice versa; mappings/data can be inherited from plot-level settings) — this is what lets the same grammar support both terse everyday syntax and precise, fully explicit specification when needed.
- Faceting is best understood as a general small-multiples mechanism, independent of the coordinate system — worth contrasting with how conditioning/trellis/lattice displays are usually taught as a separate technique.

**Caveats:** This is a design/formalization paper, not an empirical study — there is no user evaluation, and the comparisons to Wilkinson's grammar are architectural/expressiveness arguments rather than measured results. Scope is explicitly bounded to static, non-interactive graphics on (mostly) the 2D plane; §8 states outright that "all plots are static and separate," and that the grammar offers no guidance for visualizing non-tabular model objects (MANOVAs, clusterings, mixed-effects models). Several things are explicitly left unresolved rather than solved: choosing a good default scale is called "difficult" with no general answer given; tick-mark placement under scale-vs-coordinate transformation is called "suboptimal," and the paper poses, without answering, whether a graphic's specification should be uniquely recoverable from its rendering. Section 7 ("A Poetry of Graphics?") is explicitly speculative — its title carries a question mark, and the text says "it seems difficult to see how to do much more algorithmically" — so its proposals (an automatic "grammar checker," scagnostics-driven view suggestion) should not be read as implemented ggplot2 features. One place the source itself is thin rather than fully worked through: §6.3 asserts that transforming data versus transforming the scale "produce graphs that look very similar" because "statistics operate on data that has been transformed by the scale," yet Figure 17's own caption says "close inspection is required to see that the scales and minor grid lines are different" — the paper illustrates this difference with a figure but does not derive or fully explain it in the prose, which a careful reader should notice rather than take as resolved. On the relationship to today's ggplot2: this paper documents the package's 2009-era explicit syntax (`ggplot() + layer(data=, mapping=, geom=, stat=, position=)`) and the `qplot()` shortcut as living, current features; by now (more than fifteen years later) `qplot()` has been deprecated and removed from ggplot2, default color/scale choices have been revised multiple times, and the terse `geom_*()`/`stat_*()` layer functions shown here as shorthand have long been the standard idiom rather than an abbreviation of a "full" `layer()` call — readers should treat the code listings as historically accurate documentation of ggplot2's design rather than as current usage.
