# Sarikaya & Gleicher, "Scatterplots: Tasks, Data, and Designs" (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, vol. 24, no. 1, 2018)

**What it is:** A design-space framework paper (not an empirical study) that surveys the scatterplot literature and organizes it along three axes — the analysis tasks scatterplots are asked to support, the characteristics of the data being plotted, and the catalog of design choices available to a designer — and then shows how those three axes connect to guide design decisions. The PDF summarized here is a preprint headed "To appear in IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics"; the paper was subsequently published in TVCG vol. 24, no. 1 (January 2018). Structurally it reads as a taxonomy/framework contribution in the tradition of Munzner's nested-model methodology, scoped deliberately narrow — single scatterplots with mono-variate marks, not SPLOMs, linked views, or glyph-based multi-attribute variants.

**AI summary:** The paper opens from a simple problem statement: the traditional scatterplot (one mark per object, positioned on two continuous orthogonal dimensions) is flexible and has a long history as a research sandbox, but its design "fails to scale as the complexity and amount of data increases" — the core failure mode is overdraw, where marks overlap and obscure each other. Many alternative scatterplot-like designs exist to address this (binning, density estimation, sampling, and more), but the authors argue designers and practitioners have little principled guidance for choosing among them. Their stated goal is to build a framework that helps designers select an appropriate scatterplot design for a given scenario by identifying which task and data-characteristic factors make which designs appropriate — modeled explicitly on Munzner's design-study methodology and on Schulz et al.'s design-space goal of "externalizing" implicit design knowledge so it becomes teachable and critiqueable rather than tacit expertise. Section 2 grounds this in prior work: Cleveland's observation that scatterplot design is shaped by three factors (preattentive mark features, individual-object detection, and distances-as-similarity), a review of existing typologies (task abstraction on the high-to-low continuum, per Brehmer & Munzner; Ellis & Dix's clutter-reduction taxonomy; Sedlmair et al.'s taxonomies for dimensionality-reduction and cluster-separation factors), and a survey of related high-dimensional strategies (SPLOMs, scagnostics, dimensionality reduction, direct-manipulation tools like Dis-Function and InterAxis) that the paper deliberately sets aside in favor of concentrating on the single two-dimensional scatterplot case.

Section 3 builds the task taxonomy (Table 1), and the methodology is described in some detail: the authors collected 23 "model tasks" for scatterplots from the literature (empirical evaluations, design-study papers, technique papers, even position papers), then had four visualization researchers (two faculty, two senior doctoral students with 5–10 years of experience) perform an open card sort — no predetermined category names or count — on these tasks. The initial consensus groups were "open-ended browsing and exploring," "cluster rationalization," "density judgments," "dimension rationalization," "multi-scatterplot tasks" (later discarded as out of scope for a single-plot framework), and "trend analysis." Refining and reconciling these against existing taxonomies (notably Casner's task-analytic framework) produced the paper's final list of 12 abstract tasks, organized into three tiers: object-centric tasks (#1 identify object, #2 locate object, #3 verify object, #4 object comparison — reconciling an object's attribute with its spatialization or encoding), browsing tasks (#5 explore neighborhood, #6 search for known motif — find a particular known pattern like a cluster or correlation, #7 explore data — look for unusual or global patterns), and aggregate-level tasks (#8 characterize distribution — do objects cluster, form a manifold, what's the range of values; #9 identify anomalies — find objects that don't match the modal distribution; #10 identify correlation; #11 numerosity comparison — compare density/count across regions; #12 understand distances — interpreting what a given spatialization's distances mean). The paper illustrates how a complex real analysis goal decomposes into these primitives with a worked example: Brehmer et al.'s "match clusters and classes" task composes as search-for-known-motif (#6) plus explore-neighborhood (#5) plus object-comparison (#4).

Section 4 turns to data characteristics (Table 2), identifying five attributes that the authors argue drive design appropriateness: class label (none, 2–4 classes, or 5+ classes), number of points — deliberately quantized into bins (small <10, medium 10–100, large 100–1,000, very large >1,000) tied to the overdraw threshold for a given mark size and plot area rather than to a fixed count, number of dimensions (two continuous dimensions, two dimensions derived via a technique like PCA, or a higher-dimensional space being projected down), spatial nature (whether the plotted dimensions do or do not correspond to real spatial position — relevant to the "first law of cartography," that objects closer together tend to be more related, and whether that intuition transfers to abstract point spatializations), and data distribution, for which the paper settles on five representative categories — random, linear correlation, overlap, manifolds, and clusters — illustrated in Figure 2 with matching sample scatterplots for each. This is explicitly a narrower, more design-focused set than Wilkinson et al.'s nine scagnostics measures; the authors pick categories that plausibly shift which design is appropriate, rather than trying to be a complete statistical taxonomy.

Section 5 catalogs the design space itself (Table 3), built from a separate literature survey: the authors keyword-searched titles and abstracts of InfoVis, EuroVis, PacificVis, and VAST proceedings from 2009–2017 (3,040 papers) for the string "scatter," got 117 hits, and hand-filtered down to 62 papers actually relevant to scatterplot design (the full list and per-paper task/data/design coding lives in the supplemental material; the authors also built a D3-based library called d3-twodim, released publicly, to let programmers experiment with these design variables). They cluster design decisions into four groups. Point Encodings are per-mark visual variables — color, size, symbol, outline, opacity, texture, depth of field, blurriness. Point Grouping decisions aggregate or differentiate marks and split into implicit grouping (relying on the viewer's own gestalt perception of proximity/similarity) versus explicit grouping strategies: positional binning (the canonical example is hexagonal binning, trading individual-mark fidelity for reliable aggregate/numerosity judgments), polygon enclosure (convex hull, majority, or full enclosure — VisIRR's simplified ellipses are one example), and shape abstraction (smoothings like Cleveland & McGill's upper/lower residual bands, or Yates et al.'s trend-emphasizing abstractions). Point Position decisions modify where marks are actually plotted — subsampling (e.g., Chen et al.'s "smart" subsampling to preserve multi-series distributions while reducing overdraw), displacement/jittering, animation, projection (dimensionality reduction), and zooming — trading fidelity to the "true" spatialization for reduced overdraw or added structure (Keim et al.'s subspace-warping generalized scatterplots are the paper's example of packing more into unused screen space). Graph Amenities are the scaffolding elements — grid lines, axis ticks, legends, trend lines, and annotations — that help viewers navigate without changing the marks themselves. A short subsection (5.2) treats interaction as a fifth, closely related axis: intents like changing granularity ("elaborating" for more detail, "summarizing" for less, per Schulz et al.'s vocabulary) or focusing analysis on a relevant subset via direct manipulation, brushing, or linked views, with InterAxis and Hurter et al.'s MoleView (a semantic lens that can locally de-aggregate a dense region) cited as systems that make these intents concrete.

Figure 1, on the paper's first page, is the framework's signature illustration: a 3×3 grid comparing a traditional scatterplot, a hexagonal-binning implementation, and a Splatterplot (thresholded KDE-based aggregation) as columns, against three representative datasets as rows — a randomly distributed set with few points (top), a set with a much larger number of points (middle), and a set of overlapping distributions from different classes (bottom). The point is that no single design wins across all three rows: for the sparse random top row, the plain scatterplot communicates the data plainly and the fancier designs add nothing; for the higher-count middle row, the binning and Splatterplot columns start to usefully communicate spatial density that the raw scatterplot obscures under overdraw; and for the overlapping bottom row, the density-based Splatterplot column can reveal outliers and communicate overlap between the classes that the strictly-binned middle column actually erases, because binning collapses exactly the individual-point information (an isolated point deep inside another class's territory) that signals an outlier. Figure 3, later in the paper, makes a parallel comparison for a 100-item, four-class dataset — traditional scatterplot vs. a colored contour map vs. a Splatterplot — with the observation that the contour map best conveys density gradients while the Splatterplot's thresholded regions better convey which areas are simply "dense" versus not, and that both point-grouping strategies necessarily weaken support for object-centric tasks (locating or verifying an individual point) that the traditional scatterplot still supports even with its overdraw.

Section 6 is where the paper connects the three axes into an explicit "framework." The authors describe the full task/data/design space as a grid: 12 tasks × (4 point-count bins × 3 dimensionality options × 2 spatial-nature options × 5 distribution types, i.e., 4×3×2×5 = 120 data-characteristic combinations) yields over 4,300 discrete scatterplot scenarios, each of which could in principle be rated for which of the five design-decision clusters (point encoding, point position, point grouping, interaction intent, graph amenities) appropriately support it. The authors are candid that fully populating this grid is infeasible — many cells would reduce to the authors' own subjective judgment rather than settled empirical fact — so instead of the full table they provide (a) a representative "slice" in the paper itself and (b) a companion web-based exploration tool at graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Vis/scattertasks for browsing other slices. The illustrated slice (Table 4) fixes data characteristics to a large, unclustered, few-class dataset and cross-tabulates all 12 tasks against the 5 design clusters using a four-symbol appropriateness code (general support, situational support requiring extra work, support requiring a concurrent additional encoding, or no meaningful support). From this slice the discussion pulls out several concrete, quotable patterns: point encodings alone struggle to support the three aggregate-level tasks (identify anomalies, identify correlation, numerosity comparison) because those tasks are inherently about the whole distribution rather than any single mark's attributes; point-grouping strategies that aggregate marks into density regions systematically sacrifice object-centric fidelity (individual outlier and object-comparison tasks) in exchange for that aggregate-level support, which is exactly the trade-off Figure 1's bottom row and Figure 3 visualize; there is a genuine open gap in how interaction and graph amenities should support aggregate-level tasks like correlation or numerosity comparison (direct-manipulation approaches exist but how to specifically prompt a viewer toward a correlation judgment via interaction is unclear); and distance judgments (task #12) depend heavily on whether the plotted dimensions are the object's "true" continuous attributes or a dimensionality-reduced projection, since the semantics of "closeness" differ in the two cases.

Section 7's discussion pulls four cross-cutting themes out of the framework rather than proposing new content: visual complexity/too many points (the central overdraw problem, addressed by the point-position and point-grouping strategies surveyed, though most existing designs still lack graph-amenity support and lensing techniques like Tominski et al.'s survey are proposed as a partial fix); differentiating groups of marks/too many classes (color's fidelity tops out around 12 distinguishable hues per Ware, and the field lacks good techniques once class counts run into the tens or hundreds, beyond partial fixes like a residual "background" catch-all category or hierarchical grouping); communicating high-level statistics (annotations, trend lines, and shape-aggregation designs like Lehmann et al.'s pictograms or Yates et al.'s glyph SPLOMs can make implicit distributional statistics explicit, at the cost of object-level fidelity); and too many dimensions (dimensionality itself shouldn't change a scatterplot's appearance, since only two are ever shown, but tasks performed on dimensionally-reduced or projected data differ qualitatively from tasks on raw two-attribute data, motivating direct-manipulation systems like Dis-Function and InterAxis, and multi-axis embeddings like star coordinates and their orthographic variants). The conclusion reiterates the paper's framing contribution rather than claiming new empirical findings: a vocabulary and organizing structure — tasks, data characteristics, and design clusters — that lets designers reason explicitly about scatterplot design trade-offs rather than reinventing ad hoc justifications each time, plus a pointer to the supplemental material's full listing of 62 surveyed design strategies coded by which tasks, data characteristics, and design-decision types they address.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The paper's core organizing move — cross a task taxonomy against a data-characteristics taxonomy against a design-decision taxonomy — is a reusable template for thinking about *any* chart type's design space, not just scatterplots; the "slice of the grid" idea (Table 4) is a concrete way to make that cross-product tractable without pretending to fill in all 4,300+ cells.
- The 12-task list (Table 1) is a useful checklist when critiquing or designing a scatterplot: object-centric tasks (identify/locate/verify/compare a specific mark) are a fundamentally different demand on a design than aggregate-level tasks (characterize distribution, spot anomalies, judge correlation, compare numerosity across regions), and most designs are good at one tier at the expense of the other.
- The central trade-off to internalize: point-grouping/aggregation strategies (binning, KDE, Splatterplots) buy back overdraw-robust aggregate judgments but systematically cost object-level fidelity — an isolated outlier point can be genuinely erased by binning (visible directly in Figure 1's bottom row) even though it would have been an obvious "anomaly" in the raw scatterplot.
- The number-of-points binning scheme (small/medium/large/very large, tied to overdraw thresholds relative to mark size and plot area rather than an absolute count) is a good habit: "how many points" is not a fixed design threshold, it's relative to how much screen space and mark size you have.
- No single scatterplot design dominates across scenarios — the appropriateness of a design is a function of both the analysis task *and* the data's own characteristics (count, class count, dimensionality, whether space is meaningful, distribution shape), which is why the paper insists on all three axes rather than a simple "best practices" list.
- The paper is explicit that several parts of the design space are unsolved open problems, not just under-surveyed: differentiating more than roughly a dozen classes, and using interaction/graph amenities to support aggregate-level tasks like correlation and numerosity, are both flagged as gaps rather than areas with an established best answer.

**Skim/skip guidance:** This is a short, dense paper (about 9 pages of body text plus 2 pages of references) organized very legibly around its four numbered core sections, so it rewards a full read more than selective skimming — but if time is short, Sections 3–6 carry essentially all of the paper's original contribution and Section 2 (background/related work) and Section 8 (conclusion) can be skimmed or skipped, since they mostly restate context and summary rather than new material. Figure 1 (page 1) and its caption are worth studying carefully on their own — they compress the paper's central argument (design appropriateness depends jointly on task and data characteristics) into one comparison a reader can absorb in under a minute. Table 1 (the 12-task list) and Table 3 (the four design-decision clusters) are the two tables worth committing to memory; Table 2 (data attributes) and Table 4 (the illustrative slice) are more useful as reference material to return to than as things to read start-to-finish. Section 6.1's bulleted discussion of specific cells in the slice (aggregate-level tasks vs. point encodings, losing mark fidelity with point grouping, supporting distance judgments, dealing with overdraw, supporting object-centric tasks) is the paper's most concrete, actionable content and shouldn't be skipped even by a reader who skims everything else. The supplemental material (the 62-strategy listing) is referenced but not included in this PDF and would need to be fetched separately if a reader wants the full literature-survey detail behind Table 3.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Figure 1's 3×3 grid: traditional scatterplot vs. hexagonal binning vs. Splatterplot (columns), against a sparse random dataset, a higher-point-count dataset, and an overlapping-distribution dataset (rows) — the paper's single clearest illustration that design appropriateness depends on data characteristics, not just the design itself.
- The 12-task taxonomy (Table 1), in three tiers: object-centric (identify object, locate object, verify object, object comparison), browsing (explore neighborhood, search for known motif, explore data), aggregate-level (characterize distribution, identify anomalies, identify correlation, numerosity comparison, understand distances).
- Task-derivation methodology: 23 model tasks pulled from the literature, refined via an open card sort by four researchers (two faculty, two senior doctoral students, 5–10 years' experience), reconciled against Casner's task-analytic taxonomy.
- The five data-distribution categories used throughout (Figure 2): random, linear correlation, overlap, manifolds, clusters — deliberately narrower than Wilkinson et al.'s nine scagnostics categories.
- Point-count bins tied to overdraw rather than absolute numbers: small (<10), medium (10–100), large (100–1,000), very large (>1,000).
- Literature-survey method for the design catalog: searched InfoVis/EuroVis/PacificVis/VAST 2009–2017 (3,040 papers) for "scatter" in title/abstract, got 117 hits, hand-filtered to 62 relevant papers.
- Table 3's four design-decision clusters: Point Encodings (color, size, symbols, outline, opacity, texture, depth of field, blurriness), Point Grouping (representation type, positional binning, polygon enclosure, shape abstraction), Point Position (subsampling, displacement, animation, projection, zooming), Graph Amenities (grid lines, axis ticks, legend, trend lines, annotations).
- The grid framing in Section 6: 12 tasks × 4 point-count bins × 3 dimensionality options × 2 spatial-nature options × 5 distribution types ≈ over 4,300 discrete scatterplot scenarios; a companion web tool at graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Vis/scattertasks lets a reader browse other slices of it.
- Figure 3's three-way comparison (traditional scatterplot, contour map, Splatterplot) on the same 100-item, four-class dataset, used to argue that contour maps best convey density gradients while Splatterplots better convey discrete "dense region" boundaries — and both cost object-centric task support relative to the plain scatterplot.
- Color-fidelity ceiling cited from Ware: roughly 12 distinguishable hues, cited as a hard constraint on how many classes can be differentiated by color encoding alone.
- The paper's own D3-based tool, d3-twodim, released publicly for experimenting with the surveyed design variables (SVG and WebGL rendering, dropdown/tooltip-driven interaction).
- Named design examples cited throughout: Splatterplots (Mayorga & Gleicher), hexagonal binning, VisIRR's simplified-ellipse enclosures, Keim et al.'s subspace-warped generalized scatterplots, Chen et al.'s "smart" subsampling, Dis-Function and InterAxis (direct-manipulation dimension weighting), Hurter et al.'s MoleView semantic lens.

**Caveats:** This is a framework/taxonomy paper, not an empirical study — the appropriateness judgments in Table 4's illustrative slice are explicitly labeled by the authors as their own subjective assessment, informed by the literature survey and some hands-on experimentation, not the output of a controlled experiment; the paper says as much directly ("many entries would only be our current subjective assessment... to change based on newly discovered designs or empirical evidence"). The scope is deliberately narrow: single two-dimensional scatterplots with one mark per object, explicitly excluding SPLOMs, linked/multi-view scatterplot systems, and multi-attribute glyph encodings, all of which the background section acknowledges as active related areas it is choosing not to cover. The full 4,300+-cell grid the framework implies is never actually populated — only a 60-cell slice is worked through in the paper itself, with the rest deferred to a web tool and to future research; a reader looking for a ready-made lookup table for their own scenario will need to do the same subjective reasoning the authors demonstrate, not find a completed answer. The design-decision catalog (Table 3, 62 surveyed papers) is bounded by keyword search of specific venues (InfoVis, EuroVis, PacificVis, VAST, 2009–2017) and could miss earlier or venue-external work. Finally, this specific file is a preprint ("to appear" in TVCG) rather than the camera-ready published version — page numbers, figure numbers, and any post-review wording changes in the final TVCG vol. 24, no. 1 (2018) publication may differ slightly from what's summarized here, though the framework's substance is unlikely to have changed materially through review.
