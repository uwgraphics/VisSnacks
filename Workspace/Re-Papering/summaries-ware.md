## Ware, "Visual Queries" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 1)

**What it is:** This opening chapter lays out the book's central model of perception — that seeing is not passive registration of a complete visual world but an active, task-driven process built from a rapid sequence of "visual queries," each satisfied by an eye movement and the pattern-finding machinery that follows it. It introduces the bottom-up/top-down architecture of vision (features → patterns → objects, with attention working in reverse), the severe capacity limits of visual working memory, and the design implication that follows directly from all of this: good information design starts from an analysis of the cognitive tasks and visual queries a display must support.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with Simons and Levin's "door study": an actor asking for directions is swapped mid-conversation for a different person (sometimes a different gender) behind a passing door, and more than half of unsuspecting participants fail to notice. He uses this to motivate the chapter's core paradox — we feel like we see everything vividly and completely, yet we retain almost nothing outside of what we're currently attending to. The resolution, via Kevin O'Regan's line "the world is its own memory," is that the brain doesn't need to store the world internally because it can sample any part of it almost instantly with an eye movement; consciousness is better described as "rapid access" to a field of information rather than direct awareness of the world itself.

The chapter then works through the "apparatus of seeing." The eye is compared to a digital camera (upside-down image, three-cone color sampling, ~100 million receptors compressed to ~1 million optic-nerve fibers) but diverges sharply in that "brain pixels" are wildly non-uniform: the fovea resolves roughly 100 points across a pinhead at arm's length, while the periphery can barely resolve something the size of a human head — over half of visual processing power is devoted to the parafovea, under 5% of the visual field. Saccades — ballistic eye movements reaching angular velocities up to 900 degrees/second, each taking about a tenth of a second, with vision suppressed during the jump — are the mechanism that compensates for this non-uniformity, and we are unaware both of the jerkiness and of the blind spot (demonstrated with the classic X/B disappearing-dot exercise).

Ware then builds a three-stage bottom-up hierarchy — features (parallel, massively distributed processing of orientation, color, motion, depth in V1), patterns (mid-level Gestalt-style grouping into contours and regions), and objects (the tiny number, "about three," of nexus objects held in visual working memory at any moment) — and pairs it with the top-down story: attention biases feature detectors toward what's being sought, and Hayhoe and Ballard's eye-tracking study of people making a peanut butter and jelly sandwich shows this concretely — an initial burst of exploratory fixations to get "the lay of the land," followed by long, economical "just-in-time" fixations tied tightly to the action underway (e.g., looking ahead to a jar lid rather than at the hand). The chicken-and-egg problem of visual search (how do we know where to look if we haven't processed the scene, and why look if we already know what's there?) is resolved by positing a crude, low-level feature map that guides a coarse search before detailed processing occurs.

The design implications section works a sustained London Underground map example: a traveler planning a route poses a series of cognitive tasks (which lines connect, is there a shorter route, where to change trains, trip duration, distance to stations, cost), and Ware shows Harry Beck's iconic schematic map supports route-finding beautifully (clear color-coded contour-tracing) but almost completely fails to support proximity/distance tasks, because it deliberately distorts geography for clarity. He walks through the actual query sequence a rider's brain executes — locate start station, locate destination station, trace the connecting colored line via repeated fixations (~2 seconds), then estimate stop-count by judgment rather than counting — and generalizes this into a "nested loops" model: an outer problem-solving loop (find candidate strategies), a middle visual-search loop (eye movements tracing patterns), and an inner loop (pattern testing at each fixation, evaluating ~1-4 simple patterns per fixation at roughly 20 evaluations/second). He notes Google Maps as an example of "distributed cognition" extending outside the skull, invoking Don Norman's "the power of the unaided mind is highly overrated" quote, and closes the chapter's substance with a note on distributed, modular brain architecture (V1/V2, V4, fusiform gyrus, frontal lobes, lateral interparietal area) — deliberately kept functional/abstract rather than deep neuroscience.

The conclusion crystallizes the book's thesis: perceiving is "a skilled active process" of eye movements executed to satisfy information need; the designer's special skill is not drawing ability but "the talent to analyze a design in terms of its ability to support the visual queries of others." Ware explicitly distances the book's concerns from fine art (whose goals are often the opposite of clarity) and states the book's aim as helping designers move from intuitive to explicit, science-grounded understanding of visual task analysis.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Perception is task-driven and sample-based, not a complete internal picture — a display should be designed around the specific cognitive tasks and "visual queries" it needs to support, not around depicting all available data faithfully.
- Visual working memory holds only about 3-4 objects at a time; this is why external visual aids (diagrams, maps, dashboards) matter — they offload work that the brain's limited capacity cannot hold.
- "Just-in-time" processing (get information right when the task needs it, not before) explains both skilled visual behavior and change blindness — a design that requires holding many things in mind simultaneously will fail even if all the information is technically present on screen.
- A single visualization is rarely good at everything: the Underground map excels at route-tracing but fails at distance/proximity judgments — evaluate any chart or map against the specific list of questions it's meant to answer, not in the abstract.
- Attention operates top-down at every processing stage, from raw feature detection up through object recognition — what a viewer perceives is actively shaped by what they're looking for, which is a design lever (e.g., color-coding to make a sought pattern "shout louder").
- Design skill is fundamentally an analytic skill (task analysis → visual queries → color/form/space choices), separable from drawing/software skill — a claim directly relevant to teaching a systematic design process rather than a purely aesthetic one.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The core material — the door-swap experiment, the fovea/periphery non-uniformity, the bottom-up/top-down three-stage model, and the London Underground worked example with its four-step query breakdown and nested-loops diagram — is essential and highly teachable. The anatomical detail (specific brain regions, receptor counts, saccade velocities in degrees/second) is interesting color but skimmable for a design-focused course; it's there to ground the functional claims in real neuroscience, not because students need the numbers. The closing few paragraphs distinguishing this book's design-communication goals from fine art's expressive goals are a useful framing note but can be read quickly.

**Memorable specifics:**

- In the Simons/Levin door study, "more than 50 percent of the time" people failed to notice their conversation partner had been swapped for a different person — "Incredibly, people even failed to notice a change in gender!"
- Kevin O'Regan's line: "the world is its own memory" — the resolution to why we feel we see everything but retain almost nothing.
- The eye/brain has ~100 million retinal receptors feeding into only ~1 million optic-nerve fibers, comparable to a digital camera's data compression.
- The fovea resolves about 100 points across the head of a pin at arm's length; at the edge of the visual field we can barely resolve something the size of a human head — "over half of our visual processing power is concentrated in" the fovea/parafovea, covering less than 5% of the visual field.
- Saccades reach angular velocities of up to 900 degrees per second and take about one-tenth of a second; vision is suppressed during the movement itself.
- Visual working memory holds "about three objects" at a time — the "temporary nexus of meaning and action" — and this is why the door-swap experiment fooled people: the interlocutor's face wasn't task-relevant and got displaced.
- Don Norman (quoted): "The power of the unaided mind is highly overrated... How have we increased memory, thought and reasoning? By the invention of external aids: it is things that make us smart."

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The three-panel door-swap cartoon (p.2) — a clean, classroom-ready illustration of change blindness that pairs perfectly with the >50% failure-rate statistic.
- The brain-pixel "distorted map" diagram (p.5) showing wildly non-uniform circle sizes across the visual field, plus the companion blurred-spreadsheet/blurred-person photos (p.6) — a strong, immediately intuitive demonstration of foveal vs. peripheral acuity for a lecture on visual attention.
- The bar-chart-vs-map "visual queries" example (p.4) — a compact, reusable illustration of how the same underlying cognitive act (search-then-read) applies across totally different chart types.
- The feature → pattern → object pipeline diagram with the dog (p.9-11) — a good schematic for teaching the bottom-up/top-down processing hierarchy in one image.
- The Hayhoe/Ballard peanut-butter-sandwich eye-tracking photo (p.13), with its yellow fixation-circle overlay — a vivid, real-data illustration of "just-in-time" eye movement strategy that instructors can point to when discussing task-driven attention.
- The London Underground map itself (p.15) plus the nested-loops diagram (p.18) — together they make an excellent worked example for a lecture segment on task analysis: a famous, familiar visualization critiqued rigorously against a concrete list of cognitive tasks it does and doesn't support.

**Caveats:** This is an introductory, scene-setting chapter, so its neuroscience is deliberately simplified ("we will simplify to a three-stage model") and several claims are stated with more confidence than hedging (e.g., "about three objects" in visual working memory, "about 20 per second" pattern evaluation) — these are useful rules of thumb for teaching but shouldn't be quoted as precise, uncontested figures without checking Ware's citations in later chapters. The chapter is explicit that it is not a neuroscience text and that exact brain locations "ultimately does not matter" for design purposes, which is a fair caveat to pass on to students who might otherwise over-index on the anatomical labels. The Underground map critique is a designed illustration of a general principle rather than a comprehensive design case study — it's worth noting Ware himself says "this is not to say that it is a bad design" to avoid students walking away thinking Beck's map is being criticized rather than used as a teaching example of trade-offs.

## Ware, "What We Can Easily See" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 2)

**What it is:** This chapter lays out the theory of low-level, pre-attentive visual processing that determines what a viewer can find at a glance versus what requires slow, effortful search — and translates that theory directly into design rules for making graphic symbols "easy to find." It builds on Chapter 1's account of eye movements and executable memories, and sets up Chapter 4's later treatment of color.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the thief-with-a-flashlight metaphor: visual attention moves a narrow spotlight point to point, and most information about the world is only picked up when directly fixated — except for a few things that "shout" loudly enough to be sensed in the periphery. The chapter's driving question is what makes something easy to find, and the answer starts in the neurophysiology of the primary visual cortex (V1) and V2, mapped via single-neuron recording pioneered by Hubel and Wiesel (Nobel Prize, 1981). V1 and V2 are described as tapestries of interlocking regions that process color, form/orientation, motion, and stereoscopic depth in parallel, "far more complex and powerful than anything humans have built to date" — more powerful, Ware says, than weather-forecasting supercomputers. V1/V2 output feeds two downstream systems: the *what* pathway (object identity — "a chicken, a Volkswagen, or Aunt Mabel") and the *where* pathway (spatial location, used to plan eye movements and reaching).

The mechanism that connects low-level features to eye-movement planning is *biased competition*: searching for tomatoes issues an instruction like "all you red-sensitive cells in V1, shout louder," and the loudest-shouting red patch gets the next fixation. This is illustrated with the classic p/q search task — the two highlighted p's pop out instantly while the q's take roughly ten times longer to find, even though readers have thousands of hours of experience with both letterforms, because pop-out depends on low-level feature geometry (the p's mirror-image match), not familiarity.

Anne Triesman's visual search experiments are covered in detail: when a target differs from all distracters by a single low-level feature (color, orientation, size, motion, and to a lesser degree convexity/concavity), response time is flat regardless of distracter count — evidence of parallel, automatic processing. Triesman called this "pre-attentive," a term Ware argues is a misnomer since her subjects were paid to concentrate hard on a known target; he proposes *tunable* instead — properties the eye-movement system can be biased toward, not properties seen without any attention. Inattentional blindness (Mack and Rock) is cited as the flip side: without knowing the target in advance, even fairly blatant things are missed. Pop-out requires sufficient feature contrast (a rule-of-thumb 30-degree orientation difference is given) and depends on background homogeneity — the more the background varies within a channel, the bigger the difference needed to stand out. Crucially, *conjunctions* of two features (e.g., "green AND square") do not pop out — the three green squares example demonstrates that the visual cortex can tune for shape or for color, but not both simultaneously; conjunctive targets require slow, serial, attentive search up the *what* pathway.

Ware extends this into *feature channels* — separate, near-independent processing streams (color, orientation/size, motion) that can each be tuned independently. This lets designers support multiple simultaneous visual queries by mapping different variables to different channels, illustrated with a GDP/population-growth scatterplot that also encodes literacy via shape and urbanization via color, and a town/megastore map example using color, 3D shading/cast shadows, and bold outline shapes as independently tunable channels. He notes hard limits: roughly 8-10 independently searchable symbols is a practical ceiling since each channel offers only about three usable steps (three sizes, three orientations, etc.), and enhancements are asymmetric — increasing size/contrast or adding a part is more effective than the corresponding decrease. Learning does not help complex conjunctive search (the "hidden 6" among digits, ~2 seconds, versus a hardwired-feature dot found ~10x faster; individual faces in a crowd don't pop out even for someone searching for a relative), because expertise operates after fixation, not in directing where the eye goes next.

Motion gets its own section as a uniquely powerful channel, tied to evolutionary predator-detection sensitivity in the visual periphery; Hillstrom and Yantis's finding that *emergence* (not just movement) most powerfully triggers the orienting response is highlighted, along with Ware's own prior work using motion as a "human interrupt" signal, with the caveat that rapid/blinking motion is hard to suppress and is "one of the worst forms of visual pollution" when used gratuitously (Times Square is offered as the extreme case).

The chapter closes on visual search strategy: the *move-and-scan loop*, *eye-movement control loop*, and *pattern-testing loop* (each fixation runs 1-4 tests at ~1/20 second each), a *detection field* that grows with distance from fixation (illustrated by a five-fixation search sequence), a *lateral interparietal*-area memory that inhibits revisiting 4-6 recently fixated locations, and the value of multiscale visual structure (clusters of clusters) for efficient hierarchical search, closing with the idea that designs conforming to learned scan patterns (like reading order) are searched efficiently while those that violate stereotype cause frustration.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Pop-out is real and neurally grounded, but only for single-feature differences on one of a handful of channels (color/hue, lightness, size, elongation, orientation, motion, spatial grouping) — conjunctions of two features do not pop out and force slow serial search.
- To make several kinds of information each independently searchable in one display, map each to a *different* channel (color for one variable, shape/orientation for another, size for a third) rather than stacking distinctions onto a single channel.
- There's a practical ceiling of roughly 8-10 independently searchable symbol types in a static display, because each channel only supports about three discriminable steps.
- Feature differences must be large enough to matter (rule of thumb: ~30 degrees of orientation difference) and "enough" depends on how much the background already varies in that channel — a busy, heterogeneous background raises the bar for standing out.
- Learning/expertise does not rescue conjunctive or complex search — pattern recognition expertise only helps after the eye lands on something, not in guiding the eye there in the first place, so designers can't assume "practiced users" will find things faster in a badly-coded display.
- Motion/blinking is the strongest attention-grabber available and should be used sparingly and deliberately (e.g., a rare, self-terminating signal for a genuinely urgent alert), not as decoration — gratuitous motion is "visual pollution."

**Skim/skip guidance:** The core material — the p/q demo, biased competition, Triesman's pop-out findings and the pre-attentive/tunable distinction, the conjunctive-search examples (three green squares, inverted-T, feature-space diagrams), and the "Lessons for Design" / channels section — is essential and directly usable for lecture. The neuroanatomy detail (V1 layer structure, cortical layer numbers, the "billions of neurons" and parallel-computer analogy, What/Where pathway brain diagrams) is good color but skimmable if time is short; the closing visual-search-strategy section (move-and-scan/eye-movement/pattern-testing loops, detection field, lateral interparietal area) is interesting but more about search behavior generally than about design guidance, and could be trimmed for a design-focused course.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The p/q search demo: finding two highlighted p's among random letters is easy; finding two q's in the same field takes roughly ten times longer and disrupts one's train of thought.
- Hubel and Wiesel's single-neuron probing of V1 won a Nobel Prize in 1981.
- A "hidden 6" among a block of digits takes about 2 seconds to find; by contrast a distinct dot pops out roughly ten times faster, in a single glance — "The features that pop out are hardwired in the brain, not learned."
- Rule of thumb: pop-out from orientation contrast typically needs about a 30-degree difference.
- Practical limit: only about 8-10 symbols can be made independently pop-out searchable in a static display, since each channel offers roughly three usable difference-steps (three sizes, three orientations, three motion frequencies, etc.).
- A pop-out object can be distinguished from its surroundings in less than a tenth of a second, versus eye movements occurring at roughly three per second (and up to several seconds) for non-pop-out search.
- Individual faces in a crowd do not pop out — even a sibling's face requires serial search — but a uniquely colored item (the one yellow jacket) is found in a single fixation.
- Between four and six recently-visited fixation locations are retained (thought to involve the lateral interparietal area) to prevent the eye from re-visiting the same spot.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The "ehklhfdiyaiory..." p/q letter-search demo (p. 24) — a simple, self-running in-class demonstration of pop-out vs. serial search that needs no explanation.
- The four-panel pop-out grid (green dot / oblique lines / large circle / oscillating dots, p. 28) and the fuller "Gray value / Elongation / Curvature / Added surround box / Shape / ... / Blinking / Direction of motion / Phase of motion" 16-panel taxonomy (p. 30) — an excellent single-slide catalog of every low-level pop-out channel, ideal for a "what can you bias for" lecture slide.
- The "three green squares" conjunctive-search figure (p. 31) with its caption explaining the cortex can tune for shape or color but not both — the clearest possible illustration of why conjunctions fail to pop out.
- The feature-space diagrams (color x size scatter panels, p. 32) showing why a target surrounded on all sides in feature space is least distinct — a nice bridge between the biology and an actual scatterplot/encoding argument.
- The GDP/population-growth/literacy/urbanization scatterplot (p. 33) and the town/megastore map (p. 34) — both directly show the "different channels for different queries" design lesson applied to genuine data-viz-style figures, very reusable for a course.
- The "Basic Pop-Out Channels" summary figure (p. 41, hue/lightness/size/elongation/orientation/motion/spatial grouping) — a clean one-page reference chart, good as a takeaway slide or handout.

**Caveats:** Ware is explicit that "pre-attentive" is a misleading term for Triesman's findings — a point worth preserving in any summary rather than repeating the more common textbook shorthand that these effects require "no attention at all." The 8-10-symbol ceiling and the 30-degree rule of thumb are heuristics, not precise thresholds, and shouldn't be over-quoted as hard numbers. The chapter's real-world photographic examples (the crowd/yellow-jacket photo, Times Square) are illustrative rather than data from controlled studies, and the "megastore map" and GDP scatterplot are constructed teaching examples, not published empirical results — treat them as design illustrations rather than evidence. Some cited findings (e.g., convexity/concavity pop-out) are flagged by Ware himself as not yet tied to a known V1 mechanism, so shouldn't be presented as fully settled neuroscience.

## Ware, "Structuring Two-Dimensional Space" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 3)

**What it is:** This chapter explains how the brain constructs two-dimensional patterns — contours, textures, groupings, and their higher-level semantic uses in design — out of the fragmented low-level features described in Chapter 2. It runs from the psychophysics of depth perception through the neuroscience of contour binding and object recognition (V1→V2→V4→IT) to a practical taxonomy of spatial metaphors and a set of worked examples (maps, line graphs, node-link diagrams, a news web page) analyzed as sets of "visual pattern queries."

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the idea of "2.5D space": the eye samples the up-down and sideways (image-plane) dimensions richly — about a million retinal locations refreshed in under a tenth of a second via eye movements — but gets only one depth (away) value per image-plane point, and getting a new depth sample requires walking to a new vantage point, which can take seconds to minutes. This asymmetry (he suggests "2.05 dimensions" is more accurate than "2.5") is why so much perceptual and design machinery is organized around the image plane rather than depth, and why pattern-finding is "the very essence of visual thinking."

The chapter then works through the neuroscience of pattern formation. Contour binding — combining fragmented V1 edge responses into continuous boundaries — is explained anthropomorphically: an edge-detecting neuron enters a "mutual admiration society" with neighboring neurons tuned to the same orientation and in-line position, exciting them while inhibiting differently-oriented neighbors, so that neurons along a real contour fire in unison while fragments are suppressed. A footnote warns against the "homunculus fallacy" — never say a higher mechanism simply "looks at" lower information; explanation requires an actual mechanism. The lateral occipital cortex (LOC) is identified as the site of a "generalized contour extraction mechanism" that binds silhouettes, color edges, texture boundaries, and motion boundaries into a common contour representation (citing Kourtzi & Kanwisher 2001) — which Ware uses to explain why simple line drawings are so effective: a pencil line looks nothing like a real edge but strongly activates this same generalized mechanism.

Texture regions are covered next: grain size, orientation, contrast, and curve-vs-straight are the primary dimensions that make textures pop apart, mirroring the V1 feature properties from Chapter 2; more complex textures (like T's vs. L's) do not segregate as easily. This leads to a discussion of visual interference ("like interferes with like") — text overlaid on a background sharing its feature granularity is hard to read, while low-contrast or dissimilar-granularity backgrounds interfere less — and of channel-based design for overlapping regions (e.g., a map showing both temperature zones and vegetation zones), where heterogeneous channels (color + texture + outline) let a viewer isolate any one region despite overlap.

The "what pathway" (V1→V2→V4→IT) is presented as a hierarchy of increasingly complex, increasingly experience-dependent, and increasingly spatially-invariant pattern detectors, illustrated by "The What Hierarchy" diagram. V4 encodes mid-complexity patterns (spikes, convexities/concavities, T- and X-junctions, per Pasupathy & Connor 2002) and tolerates moderate stretching/rotation of a pattern. IT neurons respond to highly specific learned categories (faces, letters, cars). Ware draws a universal/idiosyncratic distinction: V1 features are essentially the same across people (with rare exceptions like uncorrected childhood vision problems during the critical period), while V4/IT pattern vocabulary is built through individual experience — the basis of expert perceptual skill (an expert reader chunking whole words, a geologist or lighting designer seeing task-specific patterns). He connects this to the deep-learning breakthrough (LeCun, Bengio, Hinton 2015), noting artificial multilayer networks now show face/pattern-selective layers analogous to V1–V4–IT and had to learn low-level features first before higher levels could build on them.

A key design-relevant construct is the "apprehendable chunk": the amount of pattern that can be read into the brain in a single fixation (~1/10 second) without a sequence of eye movements. For unlearned patterns, this is roughly three feature components; for learned/expert patterns (like whole words), a single chunk can hold far more. Complex patterns require serial processing — "multi-chunk queries" broken into subqueries, each resolved by a separate fixation, with visual working memory (Chapter 6) tracking progress. Ware then covers spatial layout/grouping by proximity (points or shapes clustering at multiple simultaneous scales — a group of small dots is itself perceived as a large shape) and the special status of horizontal/vertical orientation (tied to gravity/posture and to the abundance of rectilinear structure in the built environment; humans detect tiny departures from exact vertical/horizontal far more sensitively than deviations from other angles).

The chapter's design section catalogs the basic pattern-forming devices — connecting contour, enclosing contour, proximity grouping, alignment, common color/texture region, common movement — and maps them to "natural semantics": e.g., a big shape means large quantity, physical attachment means "part of," enclosure means containment, connecting lines mean a relationship (with line thickness/color/texture encoding relationship strength or type). Ware invokes Lakoff's *Metaphors We Live By* to argue these spatial mappings aren't arbitrary design convention but tap into spatial metaphor structures fundamental to human reasoning generally. The chapter closes with a worked set of examples — a road map, an ocean current/temperature map, line graphs (including a dual-axis and an uncertainty-band version), a blurred news website, and two node-link diagrams — each analyzed as a specific set of "visual pattern queries" (e.g., "find an efficient route," "find where the strongest currents are located," "can the network function if node X fails?"), explicitly modeling the critical-analysis exercise: naming the query, describing how the design's patterns support or fail to support it, and diagnosing where the design could be improved.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The core method of the chapter — analyzing a graphic as a specific set of "visual pattern queries" and asking how well the design's patterns support each one — is a directly reusable critique technique for any visualization design course.
- Pattern-forming devices (connecting contour, enclosure, proximity, alignment, common color/texture region, common motion) map onto specific "natural semantics" (relationship, containment, similarity, part-of, magnitude) — a compact vocabulary for teaching how graphic encoding choices carry implicit meaning, independent of any legend.
- The distinction between universal, unlearned pattern perception (V1-level; roughly a 3-feature "apprehendable chunk" per fixation) and expertise-dependent, learned pattern perception (V4/IT) explains why novices and experts read the same chart differently, and why chunking/training changes what counts as "simple" in a display.
- Line drawings work because of a specific mechanism (the generalized contour/LOC binding of luminance, color, texture, and motion boundaries into one contour representation) — a concrete, teachable reason sketches and outline diagrams are so effective, not just a stylistic preference.
- "Like interferes with like": overlaying information using features similar to a background layer (in granularity, color, or feature type) actively degrades legibility; maximizing feature-level distinctiveness between layers is the design fix, directly relevant to annotation, labeling, and layered map/chart design.
- Multi-chunk visual queries force a sequence of fixations tracked in visual working memory — a reminder that complex diagrams impose real cognitive cost per additional chunk, motivating design choices that reduce the number of fixations needed for a task.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The 2.5D-space psychophysics opening (pp. 44–45) and the neuron-level "mutual admiration society" contour-binding mechanics (pp. 47–49) are good-to-read background but can be skimmed for the conclusions (2.5D asymmetry drives image-plane-centric design; contours form via mutual excitation/inhibition among aligned edge detectors) without dwelling on mechanism detail. The essential core for a design course is: the generalized contour concept and why line drawings work (p. 49–50), texture region discrimination and interference (pp. 50–51), the apprehendable chunk and serial/multi-chunk processing (pp. 55–56), the pattern-for-design vocabulary and semantic pattern mappings (pp. 58–60, with the two graphical-code/semantics tables), and the worked pattern-query examples (pp. 61–64), which model the analytical exercise directly. The deep-learning aside (p. 54) and the V4-hierarchy neuroscience citations are supporting color, not load-bearing for design practice.

**Memorable specifics:**

- "It would be more accurate to say that visual space has 2.05 dimensions" — Ware's sharpened version of the standard "2.5D" claim, with the reasoning: ~1 million points sampled richly in up-down/sideways per fixation vs. essentially one depth value per point, refreshed on a ten-to-hundred-times-slower timescale (eye movements vs. walking).
- The homunculus fallacy footnote: "It is never acceptable to simply say that a higher-level mechanism *looks at* the low-level information" — a crisp epistemic rule for evaluating any proposed perceptual/design mechanism.
- For unlearned patterns, the apprehendable chunk size is about three feature components per single eye fixation (~1/10 second read-in time).
- People can detect a line rotated only 3 degrees from vertical, and are "extremely sensitive" to small end-to-end misalignments of straight lines — the empirical basis for the special status of horizontal/vertical alignment in layout.
- The Kourtzi & Kanwisher (2001) finding that LOC neurons respond to object shape "irrespective of how the bounding contours are defined" (luminance, color, texture, or motion boundaries) is the empirical anchor for the "generalized contour" concept.
- Deep-learning aside: Hinton's insight that neural nets must learn low-level patterns before higher levels can be learned is called "one of the major breakthroughs that enabled computer AI to analyze patterns as well or better than humans," directly paralleled to the V1→V4→IT hierarchy.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The edge-detector "mutual admiration society" figures (p. 48, noisy-edge-vs-suppressed-fragments pair) are excellent for teaching contour binding via mutual excitation/inhibition — visually clear even divorced from the neuroscience narrative.
- The generalized-contour figure (p. 49, four different dog renderings — photo, silhouette, textured, line drawing — all converging on the same "generalized contour" node) is a strong, reusable illustration for explaining why line drawings and photos can convey "the same object" to the visual system.
- The texture-discrimination pairs (p. 50–51: orientation, grain size, curve-vs-straight, blur; and the hard-to-discriminate T/L, red-green-circle/rectangle, and oriented-spike examples) are ready-made teaching pairs for a lecture on preattentive texture segregation and its limits.
- The "Pattern for Design" and "Semantic Pattern Mappings" figures/tables (pp. 58–60) are the most directly reusable for a design course: they compactly tabulate graphical code → semantic meaning (small shapes = objects/nodes, connecting contour = relationship, enclosure = containment, size = magnitude, etc.) and could anchor a lecture segment or handout on encoding-to-meaning mappings.
- The worked pattern-query examples (pp. 61–64: road map, ocean current map, line-graph variants, news web page, node-link diagrams) are built explicitly as critique exercises with stated queries and design comments — several are usable near-verbatim as in-class critique prompts.

**Caveats:** This is a proof/draft PDF (uncorrected Elsevier proof, not final pagination or typesetting), so page numbers or minor wording could shift in the published version. The neuroscience — particularly the specific claims about LOC as *the* generalized contour mechanism, V4's mid-level pattern vocabulary, and the "critical period" claim about early pattern learning — is presented with appropriate hedging ("emerging evidence," "presumably") but a reader could easily over-read it as more settled than current vision science actually supports; treat the neural mechanism sections as a useful explanatory model rather than a definitive account. The "3-feature apprehendable chunk" figure is stated as a rough research-based estimate ("research suggest[s]"), not a precise universal constant, and shouldn't be quoted as an exact number in design guidance. The chapter's worked examples explicitly include some deliberately mediocre designs for critique purposes — students should not mistake the road map, temperature-graph, or news-page figures for exemplars of good design; several are flagged in the text itself as improvable.

## Ware, "Color" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 4)

**What it is:** This chapter builds a theory-based framework for using color in design, grounded in the physiology of color vision (cone receptors, opponent-process channels) and the perceptual phenomena that follow from it (contrast, assimilation, saturation limits, spatial-resolution differences between channels). It deliberately skips colorimetry and color reproduction as separate technical disciplines, focusing instead on deriving a compact set of design principles — for showing detail, coding categories, emphasizing/highlighting, and building color sequences for maps — from a small amount of underlying theory.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with an evolutionary framing: color vision is nearly worthless to grass-eating animals like zebras and cows (motion matters more than color for a predator's approach) but essential to fruit eaters, who need color to spot ripe fruit against foliage — the chapter's opening photo pair contrasts a color and grayscale view of berries on a bush to make this point vivid. Humans and other great apes have three-dimensional color vision (three cone types); birds have four or more dimensions; most mammals have two.

The retina has rods (numerous, tuned for low light, largely wasted on modern humans who rarely need night vision) and three cone subtypes — short- (S), medium- (M), and long- (L) wavelength sensitive — which is why color vision is three-dimensional and why displays use three color channels (RGB pixels or phosphors). Using David Williams' retinal imaging, Ware shows that S-cones (blue-sensitive) are far fewer and less sensitive than M- or L-cones, and that L- and M-cone sensitivity functions overlap heavily (400–700nm range), with the brain computing their difference to extract hue information. A practical consequence: blue text on black (or small detailed blue elements generally) reads poorly because of insufficient luminance contrast from the sparse S-cones — likewise pure yellow text is nearly as light as white, making small yellow-on-white text hard to read even though yellow-on-black is highly distinct.

The core theoretical machinery is **opponent-process theory** (traced to Ewald Hering, 1878): in V1, cone signals are transformed into three color-opponent channels — **red-green** (L minus M difference), **yellow-blue** (combined L+M cone signal minus S), and **black-white/luminance** (L+M combined, also computing spatial differences across the retina). From this Ware derives a long list of "channel properties," each with a worked example: (1) **Simultaneous contrast** — a gray or hued patch looks different depending on its surround (lightness contrast in the black-white channel, chromatic contrast in red-green/yellow-blue); this isn't mere illusion but supports real-world surface-color constancy, letting us interpret a beach scene reproduced at 1/100th the light of the original. (2) **Unique hues** — black, white, red, green, yellow, and blue are special because each is a strong signal on one channel with neutral signals on the others; Berlin and Kay's 1960s cross-linguistic study of 100+ languages found these are the most universally-named basic color terms. (3) **Spatial detail** sensitivity is much higher in the luminance channel than the chromatic channels — fine wavy-line patterns are legible in black-white but nearly illegible when rendered as pure chromatic (red-green) differences. (4) **Motion** and (5) **stereoscopic depth** are likewise processed mainly via luminance, not chromatic, channels — motion of red-green-only stimuli appears to slow down. (6) **Shape-from-shading** depends on luminance; Ware demonstrates this by remapping a grayscale shaded-surface photo into red-green values, destroying the sense of 3D topography while sharp boundaries remain visible. (7) **Saturation** reflects chromatic-channel signal strength, and (8) maximum saturation is constrained at both light and dark ends (partly perceptual, partly a limitation of display/print gamuts — white is necessarily desaturated on a monitor since it requires all channels maxed). (9) **Color assimilation** is the opposite of contrast: patches take on the hue of adjacent patterns rather than contrasting with them (demonstrated with identical-color arcs that look different against different striped backgrounds); the conditions triggering assimilation vs. contrast are "poorly understood." (10) The luminance channel is **nonlinear** — sensitive to dark differences more than light ones against a dark background, and vice versa against a light background (an effect called *sharpening*). (11) **Color segmentation**: smoothly varying color is perceptually carved into blocks of the unique hues even when the actual gradient is continuous. (12) **Color blindness** (~8% of males) is loss of the red-green channel, not the yellow-blue channel, leaving 2D rather than 3D color space with grayscale perception intact. (13) Color is a **surface property** perceived relative to context, not absolute light level — demonstrated via R. Beau Lotto's checkerboard/cube illusion where two patches that are pixel-identical read as brown vs. yellow depending on apparent lighting.

The design-principles section translates all this into rules. **Showing detail** requires luminance contrast — black/white is most extreme, but yellow-on-black or dark-blue-on-white also work; the ISO recommends a minimum 3:1 luminance ratio between text and background for legibility, which sharply restricts usable text/background color combinations for small text (though for large elements like poster titles, contrast requirements relax). **Color-coding** (e.g., land-use maps) should rely on the unique hues (red, green, yellow, blue) first for maximum learnability, since these have the most consistent cross-linguistic names, supplemented by pink, brown, orange, gray, purple; no more than about a dozen codes can be used reliably in dense, small-symbol designs (studies converge on a range of six to twelve). Visual search for a colored target is easiest when the chromatic difference from other symbols is large, hardest when many other distinct symbol colors compete, and easiest again when non-target symbols are similar to the background (so they can be tuned out) or when both a luminance and chromatic difference exist together. **Large vs. small areas** need opposite treatment: small color-coded symbols should be strongly saturated with luminance contrast against their background, while large background regions should use low-saturation, subdued colors — because area size interacts with perceived saturation and small elements are more vulnerable to contrast-induced color distortion. **Emphasis/highlighting** requires a genuine luminance-contrast difference, not just a saturated hue swap — Ware calls out the common PowerPoint mistake of "highlighting" text with a saturated color that actually reduces luminance contrast, making it less visible; a spinal-fusion medical illustration by Christy Crames is used as an example of good highlighting via desaturating/neutralizing the non-critical background.

**Color sequences** for maps (colormaps) serve two tasks: revealing spatial patterns (best served by luminance-varying sequences) and reading quantitative values via a key (where pure grayscale sequences can produce errors up to 20% of the data range, per Ware's own 1988 study, due to contrast effects). Ware recommends colormaps that "spiral up through color space," increasing luminance monotonically while also varying hue — illustrated with an ocean-height dataset comparing grayscale, a poor green-red map (low red-green channel resolution), a rainbow map (which "can be confusing and emphasizes certain features while failing to show others"), and a double-ended dark-blue-to-white-to-dark-red map by Francesca Samsel that "effectively doubl[es] the luminance range" and reveals the most detail. The rainbow/spectrum sequence is specifically critiqued: because it's ordered by wavelength it's often wrongly presumed to be a perceptually ordered sequence, but an experiment sorting red/green/yellow/blue chips shows no consistent ordering preference for the spectrum sequence versus others — parts of the spectrum are locally ordered, but the whole is not, whereas gray chips are reliably ordered black-to-white or the reverse. Zero-centered data is best shown with a neutral color at zero and increasing saturation for values above/below. **Color on shaded surfaces**: any colored pattern painted on a surface must not interfere with the luminance channel or it destroys shape-from-shading perception — light-colored, low-saturation overlays preserve shape perception while dark, highly saturated overlays "almost completely destroy" it. Finally, **semantics of color** are culturally determined (red = danger/stop in the West but good fortune in China; white = purity in the West but mourning in most of Asia) except perhaps for quantity mappings, where more-saturated or higher-contrast colors more universally signal "more."

The **conclusion** restates the throughline: opponent-channel theory (red-green, yellow-blue, black-white) explains simultaneous contrast, why only a handful of maximally distinct hues can be reliably used as labels, why large areas should be subdued while small areas need strong saturated contrast to compensate for the chromatic channels' poor spatial resolution, and why the six "special" colors (red, green, yellow, blue, black, white) make the best labels. Ware closes candidly: "Color design is subtle and can be a source of beauty and pleasure or disgust and irritation. Mostly this is a matter of socially constructed taste—there are no absolute standards. Our goal in this book is to find the bedrock of functional aesthetics in the wiring of the brain, and sadly, we have little to say about beauty."

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Luminance contrast — not hue difference — is what carries detail, fine text, motion, stereo depth, and shape-from-shading; a saturated-hue "highlight" that lowers luminance contrast (the classic PowerPoint mistake) actually makes text harder to see, not easier.
- There is a real ceiling on how many colors can be reliably used as categorical codes (roughly six to twelve for small, dense symbols); build codes from the "unique hues" (red, green, yellow, blue, plus black/white) first, since these are the most consistently named across languages and the most visually distinct.
- Small color-coded symbols/marks and large background regions need opposite treatment: strongly saturated symbols against low-saturation, subdued large areas — because the chromatic channels have poor spatial resolution and large areas distort the appearance of small ones far more than the reverse.
- Rainbow colormaps are not a perceptually ordered sequence just because they follow wavelength order — they can obscure or exaggerate features; a monotonically increasing luminance ramp (possibly "spiraling" through hue as well) supports both pattern-finding and accurate value-reading better, and simple grayscale ramps, while good for pattern-finding, can produce large (~20%) reading errors due to contrast effects.
- Simultaneous contrast and color assimilation are not just illusions/errors — contrast supports real-world surface-color constancy (why a beach scene looks right on a screen at a fraction of real-world brightness), while assimilation shows perception can also decrease apparent color difference under conditions that are still poorly understood.
- Color meaning is culturally constructed and reverses across cultures (e.g., white = purity in the West, mourning across Asia), so symbolic color choices need audience-specific checking, whereas quantity-to-saturation/contrast mappings may generalize more safely.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The physiological setup (cone types, Williams' retinal images, the S/M/L sensitivity graph) is worth a quick read for vocabulary (opponent channels, unique hues) but doesn't need deep study. The heart of the chapter — and what's most reusable for teaching — is the "Channel Properties" section (contrast, unique hues, spatial detail, saturation, assimilation, luminance nonlinearity, segmentation, color blindness) and the "Principles for Design" section (showing detail, color-coding, large/small areas, emphasis/highlighting, color sequences, color on shaded surfaces). The semantics-of-color section at the end is short and mostly common-sense/cultural-caveat material that can be skimmed. The conclusion is a tight, quotable restatement worth reading even if skimming the body.

**Memorable specifics:**

- ISO recommends a minimum 3:1 luminance ratio between text and background — a concrete, citable number for the "always ensure luminance contrast" rule.
- Color-blind individuals (~8% of males) lose the red-green channel specifically, retaining yellow-blue discrimination and full grayscale perception — yellow-blue colorblindness is "much less common."
- Ware's own 1988 study found gray-scale colormap reading errors as large as 20% of the data range due to contrast effects (C. Ware, "Color sequences for univariate maps," IEEE CG&A, Sept. 1988, pp. 41–49).
- Berlin and Kay's 1960s survey of 100+ languages found black, white, red, green, yellow, and blue are the most universally named basic color terms — direct empirical support for "unique hues" as a design vocabulary.
- The chapter's closing line: "Mostly this is a matter of socially constructed taste—there are no absolute standards. Our goal in this book is to find the bedrock of functional aesthetics in the wiring of the brain, and sadly, we have little to say about beauty."
- R. Beau Lotto's checkerboard-cube demonstration: two patches that are the literal same color read as brown (top center) vs. yellow (front face) purely from apparent lighting context — a striking, reusable illusion for teaching context-dependence of color perception.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The Berlin/Kay-inspired "unique hues" row (black, white, red, green, yellow, blue swatches, p. 70) — a clean visual anchor for teaching why these six colors anchor color-coding schemes.
- The ocean-height colormap comparison (p. 82: grayscale vs. green-red vs. rainbow vs. Francesca Samsel's dark-blue-white-dark-red double-ended map) — an excellent, ready-made teaching example of colormap tradeoffs between pattern-revealing and value-reading, with a clear "winner" and clear reasons why.
- The shape-from-shading destruction demo (p. 71: grayscale surface remapped into red-green) — a vivid, simple demonstration that luminance carries shape information and chromatic channels do not; easy to explain in one sentence.
- The GOOD/BAD symbol-and-area color-coding comparison (p. 79) — directly actionable for teaching the "saturate small areas, desaturate large areas" principle with a clear right/wrong pairing.
- R. Beau Lotto's brown/yellow checkerboard-cube illusion (p. 75) — a strong, well-known illusion for demonstrating that color is a surface-property judgment relative to context, not an absolute measurement.

**Caveats:** This is a physiology-first, "functional aesthetics" account — deliberately narrow in scope (Ware explicitly excludes colorimetry and color reproduction/gamut mapping) and explicitly disclaims any theory of color beauty or taste, which he calls "socially constructed." Some claims are stated with more confidence than the evidence fully supports for a general audience — e.g., color-assimilation conditions are candidly described as "poorly understood," and the recommended six-to-twelve color-code limit is presented as a range from multiple studies rather than a single hard number. Readers should be careful not to over-generalize the opponent-channel model as literally three independent neural populations; Ware's own sidebar concedes "the reality is actually more complex, but opponent channel theory is an extremely useful simplification." The chapter also draws several examples from Ware's own prior publications (e.g., the 1988 colormap study), so some of the "principles" reflect one lab's body of work rather than a broader consensus survey.

## Ware, "Getting the Information: Visual Space and Time" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 5)

**What it is:** This chapter examines the perceptual and cognitive structure of three-dimensional space — how depth is perceived, and how the costs of navigating through space (physical or virtual) shape information access — building toward a set of practical design principles ("2.5-dimensional design") for when and how to use depth, occlusion, and spatial layout in visual designs and interfaces.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the idea that "moving our eyes is the lowest-cost method we have for getting additional information about our environment," and generalizes this into a cost hierarchy: eye movements, rummaging through filing cabinets, and business travel are all methods of "traversing space to get information into our working memory systems," differing enormously in cognitive cost. A map of travel times around Cambridge, England, illustrates that physical distance and cognitive-access cost are not the same thing — some outlying villages are as fast to reach as nearby suburbs. He notes video-conferencing (with a nod to COVID-19) as a technology that changes these costs by preserving social cues cheaply while cutting travel cost.

The chapter's core theoretical section covers depth perception and cue theory. Perceptual egocentric space has *up*, *sideways*, and *towards-away* dimensions; the *towards-away* dimension carries far less information than the other two (because we can't see through most objects), which is why vision is sometimes called "2.5-dimensional" — though Ware cautions the "0.5" shouldn't be taken literally, since depth information is much less than half of what the other two dimensions provide. Depth cues split into *pictorial* (reproducible in a static image: occlusion, size gradients, texture gradients, linear perspective, cast shadows, height on the picture plane, shading, depth of focus, reference to known objects, atmospheric contrast reduction) and *nonpictorial* (stereopsis, structure from motion, lens focus, eye convergence). Each cue is presented in a compact reference-card format with an example image; occlusion is singled out as "the strongest depth cue" — when it competes with another cue like size constancy, occlusion wins — and as both a method and metaphor for rank-ordering information importance.

Stereoscopic depth gets its own section: it's optimal for guiding hand movements toward nearby objects (citing Arsenault & Ware 2004) and is most precise for objects at nearly the same depth, but the brain is poor at using stereo for large relative distances — which is why the 20% of people with little/no stereo vision still drive and walk fine, though they're "clumsy... threading needles." Ware attributes the repeated commercial failure of stereo technology (Victorian stereo photos, 1930s-40s stereo cameras, 2010s stereo movies/TV) to the fact that none of these let viewers manually interact with the represented 3D objects — stereo's real value is hand-guidance, which passive viewing doesn't exploit.

The chapter then introduces *2.5D design* (a term borrowed loosely from David Marr's "2.5D sketchpad," though Ware's principles aren't based on Marr's theory), with a bulleted list of tenets: use depth cues selectively and inconsistently with realism if it serves design goals; minimize overall occlusion; when occlusion is necessary, don't obscure critical information (use transparency); make joints between compound parts clearly visible; make spatial relationships (on top of, attached to, inside of) explicit; use shadows/depth-of-focus/transparency to clarify relative distance when depth is used; keep text in the image plane (slanted text is harder to read); and give common/canonical views of objects for rapid identification. A worked example is an XPLANE business-process diagram using occlusion, height-on-picture-plane, and layering without obscuring any important node.

A section on "how much of the third dimension?" argues that most information (business statistics, social networks, abstract concepts) isn't inherently spatial, and that 3D representations of such data usually fail because navigation in 3D is expensive and hard to build mental maps of — "three-dimensional information mazes are especially to be avoided." Even true 3D virtual spaces should still apply 2.5D principles because viewpoint always creates a 2D image-plane projection; practical interfaces should pair a 2D overview map (in the same orientation as the detail view) with a 3D/perspective detail view.

The chapter pivots to action with Gibson's concept of *affordances* — perceived possibilities for action (a ground surface affords walking, a waist-height surface affords support) — including negative affordances (a brick wall rules out passage) — then extends this to *cognitive affordances* (on-screen buttons as "possibilities for action" in interfaces). This connects to brain anatomy: the *where* pathway runs from V1/V2 to the parietal lobe, containing distinct coarse "action maps" (LIP for eye-movement planning in retinal coordinates, MIP for arm movements, plus head-centered and body-centered maps), each feeding the motor cortex; these maps are temporary and sparse, not detailed or permanent, and much of their operation is unconscious.

A section on "artificial interactive spaces" discusses how video games only need to get physics "roughly" right (the "naïve physics of perception") — gravity is often halved with no player complaint — because our real-world mental models are metaphors guiding interpretation, not exact physics engines. This leads into a discussion of how skill transfers: mouse-cursor correspondence, pull-down menus, and game controllers are becoming as "real" to motor-skill learning as physical objects, raising the possibility that virtual-world physics could eventually ground an alternative naïve physics for younger generations.

The chapter closes with "space traversal and cognitive costs," presenting a rough table of costs for information-access actions (internal pattern comparison ~0.04s, eye movements ~0.1s, mouse hover ~1.0s, mouse select ~1.5s, plus walk/drive/fly/train at their respective speeds, and zooming). Zooming and hyperlinks are highlighted as new "nonmetaphoric" navigation methods with no real-world analog: with exponential zooming at 4x/second, you could go from a view of the whole Earth to a view of a person's hand in about 14 seconds, and to the size of a molecule in 14 more — "the power of exponential change." The Eames-inspired "Powers of Ten"-style zoom sequence illustrates this. The conclusion restates the throughline: "the functional aesthetics of space are related to the cost of accessing information," and depth cues are used by the brain piecemeal, task-dependently — which is precisely what frees designers from having to apply them all consistently.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Depth cues are not a package deal — pictorial cues (occlusion, size gradient, linear perspective, cast shadow, height on picture plane, shading, depth of focus, known-object reference, atmospheric contrast) can be applied independently, each supporting different visual queries; good 2.5D design picks and chooses rather than aiming for photoreal consistency.
- Occlusion is the strongest depth cue and doubles as a rank-ordering device — deliberately layering objects so important ones partially occlude less important ones is a legitimate design technique, provided occluded objects stay identifiable.
- 3D representations of inherently non-spatial data (business stats, social networks, abstract concepts) usually backfire: navigation cost in 3D is high, mental-map formation is slow, and "three-dimensional information mazes" should be avoided — prefer 2D layouts with judicious depth cues for layering.
- Space traversal at every scale (eye movements, mouse actions, walking, driving, flying) has a cognitive cost, and interface design decisions (e.g., surfacing a frequent piece of information via an eye movement instead of a mouse click) can be evaluated and improved by attending to these relative costs.
- Zooming and hyperlinking are historically novel, "nonmetaphoric" navigation techniques with no real-world equivalent — they compress the cost of accessing distant information by orders of magnitude and have measurably changed social behavior (fewer library visits, more remote meetings).
- Affordances — including negative affordances — reframe interface elements (buttons, menus) as perceived possibilities for action, tying visual design directly to the brain's action-oriented "where" pathway rather than to passive image recognition alone.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The reference-card catalog of pictorial depth cues (pp. 91-93: occlusion, size gradient, texture gradient, linear perspective, cast shadows, height on picture plane, shading, depth of focus, known-object reference, contrast reduction) is essential — it's the vocabulary the rest of the book's spatial-design advice depends on — but can be skimmed quickly since each entry is self-contained and formulaic. The 2.5D design tenets list (pp. 95-96) and the "how much of the third dimension?" argument against gratuitous 3D (p. 97) are the chapter's most directly usable design guidance and worth reading closely. The neuroanatomy of the *where* pathway (LIP/MIP, parietal maps, pp. 100-102) is interesting context but not load-bearing for practical design decisions and can be skimmed. The opening travel/videoconferencing framing and the closing cost-table/zooming discussion are good illustrative material but largely restate the same "space traversal = cognitive cost" point in different guises.

**Memorable specifics:**

- Opening epigraph: "It is one thing to say with the psalmist 'I lift mine eyes up to the hills'—it is another thing to drag one's carcass up there" (Chesterton, paraphrased by John Prange).
- Stereo depth perception is precise only for near, similar-depth objects; ~20% of people have little/no stereo vision yet drive and walk without difficulty — they're only clumsy at fine tasks like threading needles.
- Video game designers commonly halve the force of gravity and players never notice — dubbed the "naïve physics of perception."
- Cost table (p. 104): internal pattern comparison ≈ 0.04s, eye movement ≈ 0.1s, mouse hover ≈ 1.0s, mouse select ≈ 1.5s; walking/driving/flying/train rated by speed and by "affordances for contemplative thought" vs. "affordances for interactive cognitive work."
- Zooming at 4x magnification per second gets you from a view of the whole Earth to a human hand in about 14 seconds, and from there to the size of a molecule in another 14 seconds — "the power of exponential change."
- "Live in" is called a "weasel phrase" for describing our relationship to 3D space, since the cognitive organism only ever has ~2.5D worth of usable depth information.

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The Cambridge, England travel-time map (p. 88) — a striking real-world example of cognitive/access cost diverging from physical distance; great opening hook for a lecture on "distance is not cost."
- The pictorial depth cues reference cards (pp. 91-93) — each cue (occlusion, size/texture gradient, linear perspective, cast shadow, height on picture plane, shading, depth of focus, known-object reference, contrast reduction) has a clean, single-concept illustrative image; ideal for a slide-by-slide teaching sequence on depth cues.
- The magenta-vs-cyan balls on a checkerboard (p. 92, "cast shadows") is a crisp, minimal illustration of how shadow position alone anchors depth judgment — very reusable for a short in-class demo.
- The XPLANE business-process diagrams (p. 98) as a real-world, non-academic example of 2.5D design principles (occlusion, height, layering) applied to a practical infographic — useful for showing that these aren't just academic toy cases.
- The Eames-style Earth-to-hand zoom sequence (p. 105) — nine-panel image ladder illustrating exponential zoom scale change; good visual companion to the "cost of zooming" discussion.

**Caveats:** The chapter's cost table (p. 104) is explicitly flagged by Ware himself as "far too rough an approximation" to use for rigorous interface comparisons — it's meant as "food for thought," not a citable benchmark, so don't over-read precision into those numbers. The 2.5D design tenets partly anticipate concepts (joints, object views) that Ware says are "presented later in Chapter 6," so this chapter's list is a preview, not the full treatment. The discussion of stereo technology's commercial failure and of COVID-era videoconferencing is somewhat historically dated/anecdotal (2020-2021 vintage) and reads as topical commentary rather than a durable empirical claim. Finally, the "2.5D" terminology borrowed from Marr is explicitly noted as *not* based on Marr's actual theory — just an appropriated phrase — so it shouldn't be cited as if it carries Marr's specific computational-vision claims.

## Ware, "Visual Objects, Words, and Meaning" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 6)

**What it is:** This chapter traces how the visual system turns retinal patterns into recognized objects and scenes, then meaning, and finally into action — moving from the inferotemporal (IT) cortex through visual and verbal working memory to attention and cognitive control. It sits in the book's arc as the bridge between the low-level pattern-processing machinery covered earlier (V1, V2, V4, lateral occipital cortex) and the higher-level cognitive/design implications the book builds toward, closing with a worked example (receiving a cup of coffee) and a set of design-relevant elaborations: making objects easy to identify, novelty and attention-capture, images as symbols/brands, meaning-emotion links, and imagery/desire.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the 1972 Nick Ut photograph of Phan Thi Kim Phuc to establish that images activate meaning in a fraction of a second — far faster than reading a sentence — which is why images are efficient carriers of certain kinds of information. The chapter then works through the "what" channel: retina→V1→V2→V4→IT, ending at the inferotemporal cortex, an area on each side of the brain (behind the temples) containing subregions specialized for faces (the fusiform gyrus), cars, houses, and other object classes, plus multimodal links to other sensory/language areas. Object and scene recognition is viewpoint-dependent: people are much better at identifying objects and rooms from familiar viewpoints, and as "a rule of thumb, we can rotate an object by about 20 degrees and scale it by a factor of two or three and still identify it rapidly." This works via "generalized views" — a handful of canonical views (full-face, three-quarters, profile) built from V4 patterns tolerant of some distortion, so objects are understood as "patterns of patterns," not matches to an idealized Platonic form.

Ware then presents Irving Biederman's geon theory as a competing/complementary account: the brain may also store 3D structural primitives ("geons" — cones, cylinders, etc.) plus a "structural skeleton" describing how they connect. Evidence for connection-based structure: objects are identified faster when views clearly reveal how parts attach (the teapot/hand silhouette example), and altering an attachment can make an object read as different or "bizarre" (the geon-problem figure with an arm from the stomach). Ware treats geon theory as "probably the most controversial" theory discussed, concluding the brain likely uses both 2D generalized views and higher-level structural analysis depending on object type.

A parallel and faster process is scene "gist": people categorize a scene ("a busy road," "a fast-food restaurant") in under a tenth of a second, as fast as identifying a single object — which is a problem for the traditional view that scenes are identified via their constituent objects. Ware cites Oliva and Torralba's work showing gist comes from global, low-spatial-frequency statistical image properties (e.g., beach scenes have a blue band on top, striated sea in the middle, beige sand with vertical shapes) — recognizable even from heavily blurred/averaged composites with no individual objects visible. Gist also biases object recognition: a blurred automobile on a street and a blurred bottle on a table can be perceptually identical blobs, disambiguated only by scene context (Torralba's priming work).

The chapter then turns to working memory. Visual working memory holds a "nexus" — a temporary binding of active visual patterns to nonvisual stored meaning — for a fraction of a second up to a few seconds, with capacity of roughly one to three objects/chunks at a time. Verbal working memory (Wernicke's and Broca's areas) holds about two seconds of speech, roughly three chunks, in an "echoic loop," and much of what we call "thinking" is internalized speech running in this store — which is why we can't think and listen well simultaneously. The prefrontal cortex governs attentional control and temporary bindings (quoting Miller and Cohen on cognitive control as "active maintenance of patterns of activity in the prefrontal cortex that represent goals and the means to achieve them"). Object selection is described as a "biased competition model": a bottom-up wave activates many candidate patterns in IT cortex in proportion to fit, then a top-down wave — biased by priming and task relevance — determines which one to three win and enter working memory, suppressing the rest. Potter and Levy's 1969 MIT RSVP (Rapid Serial Visual Presentation) experiment is cited: viewers could detect a target object (e.g., a dog) in images flashed at up to ten per second, though they retained almost nothing else from the sequence — establishing an upper bound on object-identification rate, with normal fixations lasting about one-fifth of a second and typically extracting one to three objects.

The "receiving a cup of coffee" example walks through a social scenario — reaching for a coffee mug from an acquaintance — showing simultaneous visual working memory objects (hand, handle, face), links to verbal working memory (a semantic schema of "being offered coffee," a gesture of hospitality) and motor working memory (the reach-and-grasp plan), describing the whole as "a loosely coupled network of temporarily active processes, a kind of dance of meaning."

Design-relevant elaborations follow: (1) objects as "patterns of patterns" means typical/canonical exemplars (a Labrador vs. a Daschund) are recognized faster, and canonical/joint-revealing poses (illustrated with Egyptian low-relief carvings) aid identification; (2) novelty-seeking is an innate drive (even babies show it), and designers/advertisers exploit "gist-object conflict" — mismatched scene and object content — to capture extra cognitive cycles, illustrated by Earthrise (Anders, 1968), Magritte and Dalí surrealism, and a Tim Flach puzzle image; (3) images function as symbols with automatically triggered associations (stop signs, brand logos), and brand value can hinge on this — Coca-Cola's brand is estimated at over ten billion dollars of the company's roughly thirty-three-billion valuation — with the Johnny Cash "Walk the Line" poster analyzed as a layered symbol system (guitar, finger-plate shape, flames, vertical bar); (4) meaning carries emotional valence, and without it "information will not be attended and therefore not processed" — a whale-ship-strike photo is offered as an example of emotive imagery driving action; (5) mental imagery is tied to desire via the "elaborated intrusion theory" (Kavanagh, Andrade, and May, 2005), where imagery gives short-term relief from a psychological deficit but heightens craving afterward, like "scratching an itch," and unrelated cognitive tasks using alternative imagery can break the cycle. The conclusion frames a visual object as "a momentary nexus of meaning" binding external visual features to prior knowledge, estimating "perhaps 95 percent of what we 'see' from the outside world is already in our heads," and closes by debunking the "left-brain/right-brain" creativity myth — creativity emerges from interaction across subsystems, with the frontal lobes as the likely coordinating locus.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Images activate meaning in a fraction of a second — much faster than reading text — which is the core justification for using imagery/icons for time-critical or glanceable information in a display.
- Visual working memory holds only about one to three "chunks" (objects) at a time; this hard limit on simultaneous visual comparison is a real bottleneck that should shape how much a design asks viewers to hold in mind at once.
- Side-by-side placement beats page/screen switching for comparison tasks — Ware states eye movements between co-located items are "at least ten times faster" than navigating between pages, a direct, quotable design argument for layout decisions (small multiples, dashboards).
- Scene "gist" is recognized via global statistical image properties (color/texture layout) in under a tenth of a second, independent of recognizing individual objects — a useful model for why coarse visual structure (color fields, overall layout) registers before any labels or details do.
- Making structural connections between an object's parts visually clear (revealed in silhouette/pose) speeds recognition — directly generalizes to diagrams and icons: keep the "joints"/connections between components visible.
- Novelty-seeking is automatic and involuntary; "gist-object conflict" (surprising elements against a familiar scene) is a legitimate, well-documented technique for capturing attention — useful for understanding both effective explanatory graphics and manipulative advertising, and where the line between them sits.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The neuroanatomy sections (IT cortex, geon theory, gist/statistical scene features, verbal working memory circuitry) are worth reading once for the vocabulary (nexus, biased competition, gist, generalized views, geons) since these terms recur through the book, but the anatomical detail itself is skimmable for a design-focused reader. The most load-bearing material for a vis course is the working-memory capacity discussion and the "receiving a cup of coffee" worked example (pp. 117–118), plus the final "Elaborations and Implications for Design" section (pp. 119 onward: make objects easy to identify, novelty, images as symbols, meaning and emotion, imagery and desire) — that's where the chapter cashes out into actionable design guidance. ## Ware, "Visual and Verbal Narrative" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 7)

**What it is:** This chapter examines how visual and verbal (spoken/signed/written) modes of communication differ in their underlying "logic" and how they combine to convey narrative — the sequence of ideas an audience is meant to follow over time. It builds on the perceptual and pattern-finding material of the book's preceding chapters (3, 5, and 6) and sets up the next chapter's discussion of predictive mental models, using examples ranging from sign language and PowerPoint slides to cinematography, cartoons, and infographic posters.

**AI summary:** Ware opens by dismantling the "picture is worth a thousand words" cliché with a deliberately awkward test case — try drawing "If halibut is more than ten euros a kilo at Good Food, go to the fish market on 5th Street" — to argue the real design question isn't images-versus-words but *when each is most effective and how to combine them*. He uses sign language as a test case for what's "fundamentally visual": sign languages (ASL, BSL, the recently emerged Nicaraguan sign language) are full natural languages invented by deaf people themselves, not translations of spoken language, with their own grammars, a critical period for acquisition (as with spoken language and the *enfants sauvage* cases), and processing in the same Broca's/Wernicke's brain areas used for speech — even though signs must first pass through visual pattern-finding machinery. This shows the visual/verbal divide is not about auditory-vs-visual channels but about **symbolization and logic**.

On symbolization: natural language (spoken or signed) rests on the *principle of arbitrariness* — words and most signs bear no resemblance to their referents (nothing "dog"-like about "dog"). Visual thinking, by contrast, is grounded in *pattern perception*, which is not arbitrary or socially agreed but partly innate, partly learned through interacting with the world. Visual designs are almost always hybrids along this spectrum: emoji-like symbols (♥ ♪ €) are essentially words; realist paintings are almost purely pattern-based; diagrams sit in the middle, mixing arbitrary conventions with meaningful spatial patterns.

On logic: natural language carries "if," "and," "but," "otherwise," "nevertheless" — qualifiers enabling abstract, conditional reasoning (also the basis of programming language syntax). Visual/diagrammatic logic is instead the logic of pattern, object, and space — relations like "inside," "on top of," "connected to," "passes through." Ware illustrates this with the 1980s flowchart-versus-pseudocode history: flowcharts, despite being called a "visual language," were largely words in diamond boxes and turned out to be an expensive, failed detour — "hundreds of thousands of dollars were spent documenting computer programs in this way, only for the results to languish on shelves never to be consulted." Pseudocode, closer to natural-language conditional structure, won out. He reinforces the point with the Edgy Media company-structure example: a diagram instantly reveals hierarchy and answers structural queries ("who does Fan report to?") that the equivalent verbal paragraph obscures.

The chapter then turns to how words and images link moment-to-moment via **deixis** — pointing gestures ("put that there," from Richard Bolt's 1980 MIT Media Lab work) that let ambiguous words like "that" and "there" acquire meaning only in combination with a gesture. This grounds Ware's critique of PowerPoint practice: slides should carry images/diagrams with minimal essential text, narration should come from the speaker, and a visible deictic pointer (laser or simulated) should link speech to image — a discipline he says is "often poorly designed," citing bullet-listing and word-for-word slide/speech overlap as the "worst" failure mode because it causes audiences to read ahead and mentally check out before the speaker arrives. He also notes rooms are typically dimmed for slide visibility at the cost of leaving the speaker's own gestures invisible (contrasted with Al Gore's well-lit delivery in *An Inconvenient Truth*).

A discussion of **mirror neurons** (Rizzolatti's 1990s discovery, triggered accidentally when a monkey's neuron fired both for its own reaching and for watching a grad student lick an ice cream cone) frames empathic, pre-linguistic communication as the substrate beneath narrative — mirror neurons fire in the same pathways used to execute an action, potentially explaining how facial expressions (Ekman's seven basic emotions) transmit felt emotion.

The heart of the chapter is the **cognitive thread**: the sequence of concepts held in visual/verbal working memory plus the links between them, which an effective presentation must capture and roughly align with its audience's cognitive threads. Narrative structure is framed as establishing–elaborating–resolving a problem, at every scale from a single exchange to a whole film. Ware discusses several concrete narrative mechanisms: Q&A patterns (Steven Katz's three-shot cinematographic sequence — two seemingly unrelated shots followed by a resolving third, illustrated with the woman-carrying-parcels/man-collide cliché); visual framing (the camera frame as an attention-control device, illustrated with *The Graduate* storyboards showing how shot sequencing forces the viewer's attention from Benjamin's father to Benjamin to Mrs. Robinson); depth-of-focus as a way to suppress distracting background; FINSTs (Zenon Pylyshyn's term, "fingers of instantiation" — up to four objects the brain can track peripherally while fixating one, illustrated with the playground/crowded-scene example, relevant to cinematographers and game designers managing chaotic scenes); and shot transitions, including the establishing-shot-then-focus-shot convention, which work because perception itself is already discrete/saccadic (frame-by-frame), so the brain is well-suited to jump cuts if they're designed to avoid disorientation.

He extends this to cartoons (action lines as a hybrid arbitrary/motivated symbol showing an object's recent path and implied future position, tied to the brain's constant short-term prediction and "exception" signals when expected motion is violated — citing Jeff Hawkins's *On Intelligence*), mixed-mode web/blog narratives with drill-down links (citing Segel & Heer 2010 on narrative visualization categories), and single-frame narrative diagrams (the Gulf Islands Waterscapes water-conservation poster, where arrow sequences impose a reading order on an otherwise static infographic).

The conclusion ranks narrative "strength" by degree of control over attention: movies and animated cartoons exert the most control (an "inescapable narrative train"); graphic/strip cartoons exert intermediate control (ordered frames but allowing random access); static diagrams, pictures, and photographs convey the weakest, most optional sense of narrative, requiring the viewer to actively discover it.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The visual/verbal distinction isn't about eyes-versus-ears (sign language proves that) — it's about arbitrary, socially-agreed symbols with conditional grammar (words) versus non-arbitrary spatial/relational pattern logic (diagrams: inside, connected-to, on-top-of). Most real designs are hybrids, and the design question is which relationships to express which way.
- The flowchart-versus-pseudocode history is a cautionary tale directly relevant to diagram design: forcing logic that's naturally verbal/conditional ("if," "while") into a visual notation can be strictly worse than just writing it in words, even when the result is technically a "diagram."
- Deixis (linking speech to image via pointing/gaze) is the connective tissue between verbal narration and visual content — this is the theoretical basis for concrete presentation advice: minimal-text slides, narration carried by the speaker, and a visible pointer/highlighter tying words to the exact part of the image being discussed.
- The "cognitive thread" concept gives a vocabulary for narrative design: designing a presentation or visualization means engineering what a viewer attends to, in what order, so their momentary sequence of concepts approximates the one the author intends — versus information-seeking/browsing tasks, where the viewer's thread is self-directed and highly individual.
- FINSTs (max ~4 tracked objects) and depth-of-focus give concrete, numeric constraints for how many simultaneous moving/highlighted elements a visualization or animation can expect a viewer to track, and how to suppress unwanted attention-grabbing in a busy scene.
- Narrative strength is a spectrum designers can choose deliberately: movies/animation (strongest control) > cartoon strips (ordered but random-access) > static diagrams/photos (weakest, viewer-discovered) — useful for calibrating how much "narrative" a given static chart or dashboard can realistically deliver versus needing animation or sequence.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The opening sign-language material (pages 125–127) is more linguistics background than design-actionable and can be skimmed once the core point lands (visual ≠ auditory; the real split is arbitrary-symbol-plus-grammar vs. pattern-logic). The flowchart/pseudocode section and the Edgy Media diagram example are worth reading closely — they're the clearest, most reusable illustrations of visual vs. verbal logic. The PowerPoint/deixis section and mirror-neuron section are short and worth reading in full for direct presentation-design advice. The core of the chapter — cognitive thread, Q&A patterns, visual framing, FINSTs, shot transitions, cartoons, and the narrative-strength conclusion (pages 134–141) — is the essential material for a data-vis course and should be read carefully.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The opening test sentence: "If halibut is more than ten euros a kilo at Good Food, go to the fish market on 5th Street" — used to show conditional logic resists visual expression.
- Pylyshyn's FINST research: people can track a maximum of four moving objects at once ("fingers of instantiation"); under normal (non-lab) conditions, we likely track only one or two objects beyond the one we're focally attending.
- Richard Bolt's 1980 "put that there" — a phrase whose two pointing gestures are required for "that" and "there" to have referents at all; foundational example of deixis.
- Rizzolatti's mirror-neuron discovery: a monkey's motor-cortex neuron, wired to fire when the monkey reached for an object, fired identically when a grad student merely raised an ice cream cone to his own lips — an accidental discovery in the early 1990s.
- Flowcharts as "a very expensive mistake": mandated for large commercial software projects in the 1980s, they cost "hundreds of thousands of dollars" in documentation effort that then went unused, because pseudocode was simply easier to read.
- Katz's three-shot Q&A cinematographic pattern (two ambiguous shots + one resolving shot) and the concrete cliché example of a woman and man walking toward each other who collide, spilling parcels.
- The chapter's closing hierarchy of narrative strength: movies/cartoons (strongest, "inescapable narrative train") > graphic cartoon strips (intermediate, ordered-but-random-access) > diagrams/photos (weakest, viewer must "actively discover" the narrative).

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The four-panel "A is inside B / A passes through B / A is on top of B / A is connected to B" figure (p. 130) is an excellent minimal teaching image for the idea that diagrammatic relations are a distinct visual-logic vocabulary, directly reusable in a lecture on diagram grammar.
- The Edgy Media org-chart-versus-paragraph comparison (p. 130) is a clean, self-contained before/after pair demonstrating diagram value for structural reasoning — very reusable as a classroom example or exercise ("answer these three questions from the text, then from the diagram").
- The flowchart-vs-pseudocode side-by-side (p. 128, egg-counting example) is a strong, concrete artifact for a lecture segment on when NOT to visualize logic — pairs well with the "expensive mistake" anecdote.
- *The Graduate* storyboard sequence (p. 136) is a nice illustration of shot framing controlling attention, though it's copyrighted film material and may need replacement or fair-use framing for reuse.
- The FINST scatter/tracking diagram (p. 137, dots with dashed trajectory paths) is a simple, reusable schematic for explaining divided-attention limits in dashboards or multi-series animated charts.

**Caveats:** The chapter draws heavily on cinematography craft manuals (Katz) and older neuroscience (Rizzolatti-era mirror neuron claims, which have since attracted significant skepticism and are not as settled a finding as this text presents them), and much of its causal narrative about mirror neurons and empathy is speculative — this is presented with the era's confidence but should be flagged as one interpretation, not consensus. The Segel & Heer 2010 typology and the PowerPoint critique are already somewhat dated relative to current narrative-visualization scholarship and presentation tooling, though the underlying design principles (minimal on-slide text, deixis, avoiding word-for-word slide/speech overlap) remain sound. The chapter's central dichotomy (arbitrary/grammatical language-logic vs. non-arbitrary pattern-logic) is a clarifying simplification for teaching purposes but, as Ware himself notes, most real artifacts are hybrids — students shouldn't over-read it as a strict binary when classifying real-world diagrams.

## Ware, "Building Mental Models: Why We Present with Visualizations" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 8)

**What it is:** This chapter builds a theory of *why* presentations (talks, slide decks, reports with charts and images) work at all, grounding it in "predictive cognition" — the idea that the brain's fundamental job is prediction, and that memory, planning, and communication are all downstream of that. It then works through a series of extended, mostly first-person examples (a seaweed-ecology research program, assembly diagrams, UV-radiation charts, advertising imagery, maps) to show how different visualization types — photographs, cartoons, animations, charts, maps, narrative diagrams — each contribute something distinct to building an audience's "executable mental model." It functions as the book's theoretical capstone for presentation design, following earlier chapters on gist, memory, and narrative.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the question of why anyone gives a presentation at all: not merely to inform, but ultimately to change behavior, "and it is action that counts." He introduces *predictive cognition* as an "all-encompassing theory about what cognition is for, what memory is for, and what communication is about," citing Andy Clark's 2013 survey as background. The core claim is that brains are fundamentally future-predicting machines — from millisecond-scale motor predictions (a hand movement's simulated consequence, checked against touch-sensor feedback) to day-to-day driving, where "an exception is triggered" only when something violates the predictive model (a car veering out of its lane), pulling the situation into conscious attention.

From this, Ware builds outward to memory. *Episodic memory* is reframed as a constructive process — "scenario construction" — rather than a replay, and predictive cognition holds that predicting the future and remembering the past are "essentially the same process," differing only in starting point. He cites Mahr and Csibra's (2015) argument that remembering the past is a secondary function whose primary evolutionary purpose is to let us *communicate* executable mental models to others via language — a claim Ware extends to human evolution generally (cognitive functions developing over the 50,000–150,000-year hunter-gatherer period to support transmitting survival knowledge) and, in a striking aside, to AI/robotics research, where "predictive planning is not enough" — robots also need to explain their reasoning to handlers.

The *long-term memory* section makes the point that roughly 95% of what we consciously perceive is "not what is 'out there' but what is already in our heads" — illustrated by the classic observation that spouses often fail to notice each other's new hairstyles or shaved beards. Long-term memory isn't a separate storage bin; the same neurons (e.g., in V4, inferotemporal cortex) both process and store. The hippocampus is critical to consolidating new executable memories (illustrated via amnesia patients who cannot recognize that a relative has only briefly left the room), and memory formation requires either an internal cognitive reward (successfully interpreting a bar chart) or an external one (merging smoothly onto a freeway, getting a smile from a colleague). A short section on *priming* (long-term/implicit memory, "perceptual facilitation") explains why designers and artists often "prime" themselves by reviewing relevant images for a day or two before a creative push.

The chapter's practical core is the translation of "know your audience" into: "Know and understand your audience's mental models and how they may be adapted and extended." Concretely this means (1) explaining unfamiliar chart types explicitly, since "the inventors of creative and innovative visualizations frequently think their designs are as transparent and understandable to the audience as they are to themselves" — usually false; (2) preferring conventional forms (maps, bar charts, node-link diagrams) so "the medium should not dominate the message"; (3) pitching content "somewhere below the median level of understanding" while linking to broader issues of interest; and (4) leveraging *animate agents* — citing Goldin-Meadow et al.'s (2008) finding that people naturally comprehend events via an agent-patient-act order, so visualizations that cast data in terms of active agents (predators, invaders, causal actors) are more readily assimilated, since human cognition is built on agent-based, spatially metaphorical foundations (Pinker's *The Stuff of Thought* is footnoted).

The bulk of the chapter is Ware's extended seaweed case study (joint work with ecologist Jenn Dijkstra on invasive-seaweed habitat structure), used to walk through visualization types one by one: photographs (a diver sampling seaweed, plus close-up and microscope images) that "have an incomparable ability to enrich mental models" in ways bare declarative sentences cannot; a cartoon of a hungry fish and a mesh "sieve" to explain the *spatial sieve* concept (how seaweed fronds let small prey through but block larger predators); an animated PowerPoint "spherical space analysis" showing inaccessible refuge volume; and a complex multi-panel scatterplot from a research paper, shown in full but with only two data points highlighted by arrows (~30,000 animals/m² under invasive *Dasysiphonia* vs. ~100/m² under kelp) — deliberately including the "unexplained" surrounding complexity so the audience's mental model includes "that there is much more information and depth of understanding to be gained by reading the research paper."

Other sections: *narrative assembly diagrams*, drawing on Heiser, Phan, Agrawala, Tversky, and Hanrahan's (2004) Stanford study of furniture-assembly instructions, which identified four cognitive design principles — clear sequence of operations, clearly identifiable components (consistent viewpoint, transparency where needed), consistent spatial layout across frames, and illustrated actions/connections (arrows for placement, dotted lines for fasteners) — and found diagrams built on these principles outperformed manufacturer instructions and even outperformed video, because diagrams support both narrative-order reading and free out-of-order lookup, while "navigating through time in a video sequence is much more difficult." A section on combining figurative and non-figurative elements uses Byrne, Angus, and Wiles' (2019) three-panel UV-radiation bar charts (plain bars → bars with weather icons → a ray-traced figure showing a human absorbing direct and cloud-scattered UV), which counterintuitively shows broken cloud producing more UV exposure than clear sky, with lines that "have a figurative interpretation as rays from the sun which interact with clouds, but also have an abstract meaning as one-tenth of a unit of UV-B radiation received by the human recipient."

Ware also treats visualizations as *advertising* — using a "heavily doctored" Hubble Pillars/Jets image to argue that NASA's goal there is to promote a mental model of "important science" to funders and the public, not to convey results to other scientists — "it is often the case that visualization designers think they are being asked to help with the science of a project, whereas, in fact, they are being asked to simply show it off." A Seabed 2030 ocean-mapping globe (showing <20% of oceans mapped as of 2020) exemplifies maps that simultaneously advertise a mission and function as an actionable planning tool ("any dark gray area is in need of mapping"). Finally, *narrative tension and plot development* discusses how nonfiction presentations borrow fiction's plot structure — introduction (partial activation of mental models, hints rather than reveals), middle (elaboration, possibly multiple competing hypotheses), and resolution — illustrated by a TV documentary about the 2004 Sumatra tsunami that built tension around two competing causal theories (fault uplift vs. submarine landslide) before resolving in favor of uplift. Ware notes written papers front-load their conclusions (in the abstract) so there's little room for tension, but slide presentations benefit from delayed resolution, and multi-threaded narratives carry real costs — "switching context can have a substantial cost" — so thread switches must be clearly signaled.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- A presentation's job is to change the audience's *executable mental model*, not just transmit facts — so before choosing a chart type, ask what mental model you want the audience to walk away simulating, not just what data you want to show them.
- Never assume your audience understands a novel visualization the way you do — "inventors of creative and innovative visualizations frequently think their designs are as transparent and understandable to the audience as they are to themselves." When you go beyond conventional maps/bar charts/node-link diagrams, budget time to explain the chart's mechanics, or the medium will dominate the message.
- Photographs, cartoons, and diagrams aren't just illustration — they do different cognitive work than prose or numbers alone (compare the seaweed photo, the predator-sieve cartoon, and the spherical-space-analysis animation, each targeting a different piece of the mental model).
- It's fine — even useful — to show a complex, only-partially-explained chart (like the full multi-panel seaweed scatterplot) if the goal includes signaling that there's more rigor and depth behind the two points you actually discuss.
- Multiframe diagrams beat video for teaching a procedure (assembly instructions) because readers can jump around and re-check any step; video forces a fixed timeline that gets the viewer's mental model "out of sync." This is a concrete, testable design principle (Heiser/Tversky et al.) for any tutorial content.
- Distinguish visualization-for-understanding from visualization-for-advertising (the Hubble image, the Seabed 2030 globe) — both are legitimate design goals but call for different choices, and conflating them is a common failure mode when a designer thinks they've been asked for "the science" but have actually been asked to make something look impressive.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The theoretical spine — predictive cognition, episodic memory as scenario construction, long-term memory, and priming (pp. 143–148) — is worth reading once for the framing but can be skimmed on a re-read; the payoff is the single boxed guideline ("Know and understand your audience's mental models...") and its four implications. The real teaching content is the extended examples from "Analyzing Seaweed" through "Narrative Tension" (pp. 149–161): these are concrete, example-driven, and easy to lift into a course session on presentation/report design. The assembly-diagram principles and the UV figurative/non-figurative panel are the most directly reusable, self-contained sub-sections if time is short.

- "It is often the case that visualization designers think they are being asked to help with the science of a project, whereas, in fact, they are being asked to simply show it off" (advertising vs. understanding, re: the doctored Hubble Pillars and Jets image).

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The three-panel UV-radiation figure (plain bar chart → bar chart with weather icons → ray-traced figurative/abstract hybrid diagram; p. 156–157, from Byrne, Angus & Wiles 2019) is an excellent, self-contained teaching example of combining figurative and abstract encoding in one graphic, and of a counterintuitive data finding (broken cloud > clear sky for UV exposure) made intuitive by the visualization.
- The furniture assembly-diagram sequence (p. 155, six numbered frames) is a clean, classroom-ready example of narrative diagram principles — sequence, component visibility, consistent layout, illustrated actions/fasteners — that generalizes well beyond furniture to any step-by-step instructional visualization.
- The predator-fish/spatial-sieve cartoon (p. 152) is a good minimal example of how a simple cartoon can stand in for and explain an abstract structural concept ("spatial sieve") before the technical/quantitative visualization is introduced.
- The four-panel scatterplot with only two arrow-highlighted points (p. 154) is a reusable teaching case for "it's OK to show complexity you don't fully explain" — useful for discussing how much detail to include in a presentation chart versus a paper figure.

**Caveats:** The predictive-cognition framework is presented as settled theory ("a radical new theory... upends many traditional views") but it's one contested position within cognitive science, built heavily on a single citation (Clark 2013) and a couple of supporting papers (Mahr & Csibra 2015; Goldin-Meadow et al. 2008) — a course should flag it as Ware's preferred lens rather than consensus. The evolutionary hunter-gatherer dating (50,000–150,000 years) is speculative and stated without much evidentiary hedging. Much of the middle of the chapter is a first-person case study from Ware's own seaweed-ecology collaboration, which is vivid and pedagogically useful but idiosyncratic — readers shouldn't over-generalize the specific "goals for our presentations" list as a universal checklist. Finally, the chapter's examples are almost all about scientific/technical presentation contexts (academic talks, TV science documentaries, NASA funding); it says relatively little about interactive or exploratory visualization, dashboards, or non-presentation contexts, so it pairs best with other chapters (gist, narrative) rather than standing alone as general vis-design guidance.

## Ware, "Creative Meta-seeing" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 9)

**What it is:** This chapter examines the psychology of constructive visual thinking in design — how designers move between mental imagery, sketching, and critique to develop ideas. It closes the book's cognitive-science arc by applying the perceptual and mental-model machinery from earlier chapters (contour extraction, working memory, predictive models) to the specific, effortful, acquired skill of design thinking through drawing.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel sketches to make the point that creative design work is rarely done purely in the head — it happens through a "dialogue with some rapid production medium," whether sketches for artists, clay maquettes for sculptors, or rough diagrams for engineers and programmers. He generalizes this into a four-step cycle: (1) a visual concept forms (constrained or free depending on the task), (2) externalization — a loose scribble puts the concept on paper, (3) constructive critique — visually querying whether the design meets requirements, and (4) consolidation and extension, where lines are strengthened, erased, or added. Steps 2–4 repeat many times.

The chapter then tackles mental imagery directly, contrasting three historical theories: Zenon Pylyshyn's view that imagery is purely symbolic/propositional with no spatial brain representation; Stephen Kosslyn's view that imagery reuses the same neural spatial maps as seeing, creating a "phantom image" in low-level visual cortex; and N.J.T. Thomas's "activity theory," which Ware endorses, holding that imagery consists of the same *active, constructive cognitive processes* as normal seeing rather than a static picture-in-the-head. Evidence marshaled includes eye-movement studies (people's eyes move when imagining a scene, matching fixation locations — though not sequence — from when they actually viewed it) and fMRI work (Ganis, Thompson & Kosslyn) showing higher visual areas activate similarly during seeing and imagining, but primary visual cortex does not activate during imagery — evidence against Kosslyn's low-level "phantom image" and for imagery being a high-level, active process using the same machinery that plans and executes eye movements. Specialized areas (fusiform gyrus for faces, parahippocampal area for spatial locations) activate for both perception and imagery of their respective content.

Ware then turns to the "magic of the scribble": because contour extraction is a generalized, meaning-agnostic perceptual mechanism, a single line or circle can support wildly different interpretations (a circle can read as coin, ball, ring, hole, or plugged hole; Saul Steinberg's 1959 New Yorker cartoon exploits this). He walks through Massironi's bird-drawing exercise — make a meaningless irregular loop scribble, then add a small circle (eye) and a small angle (beak), and "almost any scribble can become a bird" — to demonstrate that meaning is readily projected onto ambiguous marks, and that this projective act is itself a creative tool designers exploit to escape stereotyped thinking.

The chapter distinguishes sketches (visual prototypes resembling the finished thing) from diagrams (abstractions expressing concept structures, often mixing words and graphical conventions — transistors, doors and windows, etc.). Darwin's 1837 notebook tree-of-life sketch is presented as the canonical example of a concept diagram. Arrows get singled out as a uniquely important diagrammatic symbol, able to express motion paths, sequences, causal relationships, and taxonomic "is-a" hierarchies, combining the visual system's pattern-finding power with abstract symbolic meaning.

On process: requirements-gathering and design often proceed together in a "spiral design" methodology, since clients frequently can't articulate what they want until shown a prototype. Visual task analysis breaks design goals into cognitive tasks and then into visual queries the design must support. Citing Suwa and Tversky's study of architecture students designing a museum (and Bryan Lawson's interviews with architects like Santiago Calatrava, whose sketches serve "not... a wish to produce a drawing but rather to understand a problem"), Ware describes "constructive perception" — designers don't just externalize pre-formed ideas; they reinterpret their own marks, discovering new meaning in lines they've already drawn. Cognitive economics of sketching is framed as a tradeoff: visual working memory is fast and flexible but has almost no capacity and is impermanent; paper sketches are slower to modify (roughly a second or two per mark) but hold much more and persist; finished drawings cost the most but are most elaborate and stable.

The chapter closes on the perceptual critique — the meta-seeing of the title — distinguishing concept diagrams from prototype designs and describing self-critique as a simulated "cognitive walkthrough" of how another viewer would query the design. Self-critiquing one's own work is harder than critiquing others' because the designer already knows where everything is and what it means; letting 2–3 days pass lets neural priming from the original design session fade so fresh alternatives can be imagined. Formal cognitive walkthroughs with actual non-designer users are expensive and reserved for more finished designs. Visual skill develops from effortful to automatic (the mirror-reading fMRI study by Poldrack et al. is used as the model case), and — notably — sleep between practice sessions, not raw practice time, is what converts effortful visual skills into automatic ones.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- Sketching is not decoration on top of thinking — it's the medium in which design thinking happens (the "dialogue with paper"). This reframes "just sketch it out" as a cognitively necessary step, not a nice-to-have, for anyone teaching iterative design or prototyping.
- The four-step design cycle — form concept, externalize, critique, consolidate/extend — is a reusable teaching frame for any studio or critique exercise, including visualization design reviews.
- Lines are radically ambiguous (the coin/ball/ring/hole/plugged-hole example, the bird-scribble exercise) because contour extraction is a generic perceptual mechanism — a useful concrete illustration of "the eye/brain finds structure eagerly, even from minimal evidence," relevant to warning students how easily viewers over-read sparse marks in a chart or diagram.
- Concept diagrams (abstract idea structures, e.g., Darwin's tree) versus prototype designs (rough previews of the finished artifact, e.g., a mockup) is a clean vocabulary distinction for talking about early-stage visualization/design artifacts.
- Arrows are a genuinely special diagrammatic primitive — they can encode motion, sequence, causality, and taxonomic hierarchy simultaneously, worth flagging explicitly when teaching diagram/network encoding choices.
- Critiquing your own design is a harder, distinct skill from critiquing others' work, and self-critique improves just by letting time pass (2-3 days) for priming to fade — directly actionable advice for students revising their own visualization drafts.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The mental-imagery theory section (Pylyshyn vs. Kosslyn vs. Thomas, the fMRI evidence) is the most skimmable part for a practical design course — it's neuroscience background supporting Ware's preferred "activity theory," interesting but not load-bearing for design practice. The core material worth close reading is: the scribble/bird exercise and its lesson about projected meaning, the sketch-vs-diagram distinction, the arrow discussion, the Suwa/Tversky "constructive perception" finding, and the closing sections on visual task analysis, the perceptual critique, and visual skill development (especially the sleep-and-automaticity point). The conclusion is a tight, quotable synthesis and worth reading even if skimming the body.

**Memorable specifics:**

- The four-step creative design process: concept formed → externalization (scribble) → constructive critique → consolidation and extension, with steps 2-4 repeating many times.
- Massironi's bird exercise: draw a meaningless irregular loop scribble, add a small circle and a "<" shape, and "almost any scribble can become a bird" — head, wings, tail, and body suddenly appear.
- "A circle on paper can have five different meanings": coin, ball, ring, hole, plugged hole — all from the identical mark.
- Darwin's 1837 notebook page with the first known sketch of the evolutionary tree concept (held at Cambridge University Library) is Ware's canonical example of a concept diagram.
- Adding a mark to a sketch takes "a second or two," while visual working memory can shuffle concepts in and out "several times a second" — the cognitive-economics contrast between mental imagery and externalized marks.
- Quote on Calatrava (from Bryan Lawson's interviews): his sketches are never "the result of a wish to produce a drawing but rather to understand a problem."
- Skill automaticity requires sleep between practice sessions, not just practice hours: "we may practice a task for hours and gain little benefit; but if we practice for only half an hour, then wait a day, some measure of automaticity will be gained" (citing Poldrack et al.'s mirror-reading fMRI study).

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The five-circles-five-meanings figure (coin/ball/ring/hole/plugged hole) — a compact, instantly graspable demonstration of contour ambiguity, ideal for a slide on how little visual information is needed to trigger an interpretation.
- The Massironi scribble-to-bird sequence (random loops → same loops with a dot-and-beak added → recognizable birds) — a strong in-class exercise or figure for teaching how minimal marks carry meaning and how designers exploit ambiguity generatively.
- The Saul Steinberg 1959 New Yorker cartoon (a single continuous line reading simultaneously as a child's whimsical drawing and an adult's harsh scribble) — a vivid, licensable-attribution example (credited to Steinberg Foundation/ARS) of line quality carrying both content and tone; useful for a lecture on expressive line use, though rights would need checking for reuse beyond fair-use teaching contexts.
- Darwin's evolutionary-tree notebook sketch — a strong real-world, high-status example of a "concept diagram" distinct from a visual prototype, good for illustrating diagrams-as-idea-structures versus sketches-as-appearance.
- The spiral-design diagram ("vague concept" → alternating "design improvements" and "better understanding of requirements" loops → "completed design") — a clean, reusable schematic for teaching iterative/spiral design methodology in any studio context.

**Caveats:** The chapter leans on a contested area of cognitive science (imagery theory) and takes a clear side — Thomas's activity theory, which "inspired the creation of this book" — so the fMRI evidence presented (Ganis/Thompson/Kosslyn; Laeng/Teodorescu) should be read as supporting Ware's chosen framework rather than as a neutral survey; readers shouldn't treat the Pylyshyn/Kosslyn debate as fully resolved by these two studies. The architecture-heavy examples (Calatrava, Jiricna, Suwa & Tversky's museum-design study) mean most illustrative cases come from architectural sketching rather than data visualization directly, so instructors will need to bridge the analogy to charts/diagrams explicitly. This is also proof/pre-publication text (marked "uncorrected proof... not allowed to publish this PDF online" on every page), so page numbers or minor wording could differ from the final published book.

## Ware, "The Dance of Meaning" (*Visual Thinking for Information Design*, ch. 10)

**What it is:** This is the closing chapter of the book: a twelve-point recap of the neural/cognitive machinery of visual thinking covered in earlier chapters, followed by a discussion of the design implications that follow from it, organized around pattern-finding, cognitive optimization, learning/cognitive economics, and mental models, and closing with a forward-looking "What's Next?" section on computer-based cognitive tools.

**AI summary:** Ware opens with the chapter's governing metaphor: "Meaning is what the brain performs in a dance with the external environment." Tokens of meaning are spun off into media and picked up again as new meaning is constructed by combining internally stored patterns with externally derived ones — increasingly, computers are also partners in this dance, doing partial analysis and synthesis before presenting results visually.

The "Review" section walks through twelve points. (1) The fovea provides high-resolution vision only in a small area; eye movements sample the world by directing this fovea point to point. (2) Visual queries driven by cognitive goals trigger these eye movements — illustrated with Yarbus's classic eye-tracking study of Repin's painting "They did not expect him," where fixations landed on clothes/furnishings when subjects were asked about the family's wealth, and almost exclusively on faces when asked about ages. (3) Early cortical processing does simultaneous local feature analysis (orientation, size, color, motion) across the whole visual field, which is why the green bars pop out in the example figure — feature-level properties like color and orientation drive what's easy to find with eye movements. (4) There are separate *where* (parietal, action-guided) and *what* (ventral, object-identification) pathways, with an intermediate stage in V4 doing compound-shape/region segmentation before inferotemporal neurons respond to specific meaningful patterns like faces and letters. (5) Various information types — visual, verbal, action-related — combine into short-lived "nexuses of meaning" that constitute working memory contents. (6) One to three such nexuses form per fixation, a "three-object visual working memory limit" that is "a basic bottleneck in visual thinking." (7) Displays support cognition via memory *proxies* — small images/symbols/patterns that, once learned, activate concepts faster (~0.2s) than internal recall, and a screen can hold "upwards of thirty" such proxies versus only ~3 in working memory, though this only works for symbols with pre-learned associations. (8) Visual pattern-finding (e.g., spotting a connection between two nodes) can itself solve cognitive problems, illustrated with a "who talked to whom" node-link diagram. (9) Language processing runs through specialized left-temporal centers using an "informal logic" of ifs/ands/buts, distinct from and complementary to visual/spatial logic. Attention is then discussed at length as the focus of brain activity, tuned top-down (an art critic attending to brushstrokes vs. composition vs. color), with looking as a prerequisite for attending; there's a basic 1–3 Hz cycle of query→eye movement→pickup→process that structures the "cognitive thread," which can be externally driven (a film director controlling where audiences look) or occupied by mundane tasks (walking, reading faces) that consume attentional capacity without awareness. The thread shifts between visual and verbal modalities — we can't do two visual or two verbal tasks at once, but can combine a verbal task with a well-learned visual-motor one (phone talking + folding laundry), and deixis (pointing) is described as "the natural way of linking spoken words and images." (10) Designers exercise "constructive seeing" — mentally testing sketch changes before making them. (11) Long-term episodic memories are "executable predictive models, rather than fixed repositories like books or CD ROMs," strengthened by successful task completion and organized hierarchically (chess piece recognition before strategic pattern recognition; onion-chopping becoming automatic to free capacity for managing an extra dinner guest). (12) Effective visualization-based presentations transmit predictive mental models from presenter to audience, building on audiences' existing models.

The chapter then states four "Implications" for design: support pattern-finding, optimize cognition as nested activities, account for the economics of learning new tools, and design for consumers' mental models — each elaborated in its own section. "Design to Support Pattern Finding" uses Ware's own humpback whale tagging research as an extended case study: a first tool that replayed a 3D animated whale model let ethologists spot behaviors but was extremely slow (identifying a pattern could take 3–4x as long as gathering the data, since people "are not nearly as good at identifying and remembering motion patterns as we are at remembering spatial patterns"); a second tool that transformed the whale's track into a static 3D ribbon with a sawtooth pattern (derived from acceleration data) marking fluke strokes let analysts visually scan for repeated patterns "hundreds of times faster," because static spatial patterns can be scanned at several per second versus real-time replay. "Optimizing the Cognitive Process" frames the human-computer pairing as a *cognitive co-processor* (a term borrowed from Stuart Card's PARC lab), with low-bandwidth mouse/keyboard input from human to computer and high-bandwidth visual output back, each part doing what it does best. It covers Pirolli and Card's information foraging theory (people minimize the "cost" of finding information online much as animals forage for food, following "information scent"), the problems with eye-tracker-based drilling down (calibration difficulty; a ~1cm foveal pickup radius that can contain multiple objects), and Tamara Munzner's Constellation system as a successful example of clickless mouse-over hover queries for exploring hundreds of nodes in a network diagram, versus typical tooltips whose 1-2 second delay would be too slow for tightly coupled interaction. "Learning and the Economics of Cognition" discusses how repeated practice automates cognitive pathways (typing, drawing) freeing attention for higher-level tasks, frames tool adoption as a cost-benefit ("cognitive economics") decision, notes that redesigning entrenched conventions (international road signs) rarely pays off given the distributed relearning cost, and cites Sophie Deneve's view of individual neurons as Bayesian operators accumulating evidence. "Designing for Mental Models" discusses computer interfaces that fail to convey accurate operational models (e.g., ambiguous "hung" states, unhelpful ever-spinning progress indicators) and a BAMS study of Hurricane Sandy showing people badly miscalibrated risk perception (overestimating wind danger, underestimating flood risk — "something like 250,000 cars were damaged from flooding," and only "about 22% of people had an evacuation plan"). The closing "What's Next?" section predicts the book's specifics will age but its broad account of visual thinking will hold, and argues that as more of the world becomes explicitly designed (screens, interfaces, game controllers), design must account for both the brain's fixed capacities and users' evolving skill sets, closing on: "The visual system will always be the highest bandwidth sense by far, and making full use of its flexible pattern-finding capabilities can provide great benefits."

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**

- The Yarbus/Repin eye-tracking demonstration is a canonical, teachable illustration that eye movements are goal-driven visual queries, not passive scanning — what you look at depends on what question you're asking.
- Working memory holds only ~3 "nexuses of meaning" at a time; well-designed displays work around this bottleneck by offering many (up to ~30) fast-access visual "proxies" for concepts instead of forcing viewers to hold everything in their heads.
- Static spatial patterns can be scanned/compared far faster than temporal/motion patterns can be remembered — a design implication with a concrete before/after case (whale replay vs. ribbon visualization) worth using as a teaching example for "turn time into space."
- The "cognitive co-processor" framing (low-bandwidth human input, high-bandwidth visual output, each side doing what it's best at) is a useful mental model for justifying why visualization interfaces exist at all, distinct from pure automation.
- Hover/mouseover queries (Constellation) are a concretely better interaction pattern than tooltip delays for tightly coupled visual analysis — a practical, citable interaction-design guideline.
- "Cognitive economics" gives students a vocabulary for why radical redesigns of entrenched interfaces/conventions usually aren't worth it, and for evaluating whether a new tool's learning cost is justified by its long-term payoff.

**Skim/skip guidance:** The twelve-point "Review" (pp. 182–189) is largely a recap of material from earlier chapters (feature processing, where/what pathways, attention, long-term memory as executable models) and can be skimmed by anyone who has already read the earlier chapters it summarizes — it's most useful as a refresher or as a stand-alone crib sheet if assigning only this chapter. The essential, non-redundant content is in the four "Implications" sections (pp. 190–197): the whale-tracking case study, the cognitive co-processor/foraging-theory/hover-query discussion, cognitive economics, and the mental-models/Hurricane Sandy example — these contain the chapter's actual new arguments and best examples. The final "What's Next?" (p. 198) is a short reflective coda and optional.

**Memorable specifics:**

- "Meaning is what the brain performs in a dance with the external environment" — the chapter's opening line and organizing metaphor.
- Visual working memory holds "from one to three meaningful nexuses" per fixation — "a basic bottleneck in visual thinking."
- Screen-based concept proxies: access via eye movement takes ~0.1s and activates the associated concept in <0.2s; "upwards of thirty" proxies can be usable on screen at once versus ~3 items in working memory.
- The whale-ribbon redesign made pattern identification "hundreds of times faster" than the original 3D replay tool, which itself could take "three or four times as long" to analyze as it took to collect the data.
- Hurricane Sandy mental-model failure: roughly 250,000 cars were damaged by flooding (many written off despite higher ground parking often being available), and only about 22% of people had an evacuation plan.
- The "cognitive co-processor" term, borrowed from Stuart Card's PARC lab, and Sophie Deneve's characterization of neurons as "Bayesian operators, accumulating evidence about the external world or the body, and communicating to other neurons their certainties about these events."

**Figures worth borrowing:**

- The Yarbus/Repin eye-movement figure (p. 182–183, "Unexpected Returns"/"They did not expect him," with task-dependent scanpaths for "How affluent?" vs. "What are their ages?") — a classic, highly teachable demonstration of task-driven visual attention; likely already familiar from other vis courses but ideal for a lecture slide.
- The feature-pop-out figure with green bars among other shapes (p. 183) — a simple, reusable illustration of early feature-based visual search (color/orientation/curvature driving what's "easy to find").
- The whale-track-to-ribbon figures (pp. 191–192) — a strong before/after case study pair (raw 3D replay vs. ribbon-with-sawtooth-fluke-marks) for teaching the principle of transforming temporal data into scannable spatial patterns.
- The cognitive co-processor diagram (p. 193, human/computer bandwidth-asymmetry arrows) — a clean, simple schematic for explaining human-computer division of labor in visual analytics.

**Caveats:** This chapter is explicitly a summary/synthesis chapter rather than a source of new primary findings, so it should be read as a recap plus argument, not as introducing fresh empirical evidence beyond the whale study and the cited hurricane-perception paper (which is itself a secondary citation, not something Ware ran himself). Some claims are the author's own confident theoretical synthesis (e.g., the "three-object" working-memory bottleneck, presented with a caveat that video game players can push it to four, and the strong claim that the book's "broad picture... is essentially correct and will stand the test of time") rather than settled consensus, and should be presented to students as Ware's interpretive framework rather than unimpeachable fact. The chapter is also somewhat book-specific in structure (a numbered recap of Ware's own prior chapters), so points 1–11 will feel redundant to readers who have already covered feature integration, the two visual pathways, and attention elsewhere in the course.
