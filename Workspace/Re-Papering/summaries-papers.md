## Cleveland & McGill, "Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and Application to the Development of Graphical Methods" (JASA, 1984)

**What it is:** The founding paper of the scientific study of visual encodings: two Bell Labs statisticians propose that reading a graph decomposes into "elementary perceptual tasks," rank those tasks by accuracy, test the ranking with two human-subjects experiments, and then use it to argue for redesigning popular chart types. It's the long, complete version — theory, full experimental detail, and the redesign proposals.

**AI summary:** The paper opens by claiming graph design "is largely unscientific" and sets out to give it a scientific foundation via *graphical perception* — the visual decoding of information encoded on graphs. The theory has two parts. First, Figure 1 identifies 10 elementary perceptual tasks people perform when extracting quantitative information: position along a common scale, position along nonaligned scales, length, direction, angle, area, volume, curvature, shading, and color saturation (color hue and texture are deliberately excluded because they lack an unambiguous small-to-large ordering). Second, the tasks are hypothesized to be ordered by accuracy, drawing on the authors' own reasoning, prior psychophysics (Stevens's power law — length judgments are roughly unbiased, area and volume increasingly distorted), and Weber's Law (used to explain why the unfilled portion of a "framed rectangle" makes small differences detectable). The ordering, stated on p. 536: 1. Position along a common scale; 2. Positions along nonaligned scales; 3. Length, direction, angle; 4. Area; 5. Volume, curvature; 6. Shading, color saturation — with the paper explicitly noting that ranks 3, 5, and 6 contain ties "there is not enough information to separate."

Two experiments test slices of this ordering. In the **position–length experiment**, 55 subjects (51 usable) judged, on grouped and divided bar charts (five stimulus types, Figure 4), "what percent the smaller is of the larger" for marked pairs; Types 1–3 required position-along-common-scale judgments and Types 4–5 required length judgments. In the **position–angle experiment**, 51 usable subjects made the same percentage judgments on bar charts versus pie charts. Accuracy was measured as log₂(|judged % − true %| + 1/8), analyzed with midmeans and bootstrap confidence intervals. Results (Figure 16): position judgments were more accurate than length judgments by factors of 1.4 to 2.5 (errors 40%–250% larger for length), and position was 1.96 times as accurate as angle. The pie chart beat the bar chart in only 3 of 40 cases. "Large errors" occurred 5.3 times as often for length and 7.3 times as often for angle as for position, and length and angle judgments showed a consistent bias: underestimation when true percentages were in the 25–50 range.

Section 5 is what made the paper famous beyond the results: applying the principle "construct graphs that use elementary tasks as high in the hierarchy as possible," the authors call for "radical surgery" on popular chart forms. Pie charts and divided bar charts should be replaced by **dot charts** and **grouped dot charts** (Figures 22–25); curve-difference charts like Playfair's East Indies trade chart should be replaced by directly plotting the differences (Figures 6, 26–28 — the brain judges minimum distance between curves, not vertical distance); and shaded statistical maps ("patch maps") should be replaced by **framed-rectangle charts** (Figures 5, 29–31). Section 6 is unusually candid self-criticism: the theory is "a tentative first step," the tasks overlap and might better have been called "elementary graphical encodings," and the experiments already suggest a revision — errors grew from Type 1 to Type 3 as horizontal distance between compared bars grew, so position judgment should really be a continuum degrading with distance.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- This is where "Cleveland and McGill" comes from: the first serious attempt to put chart design on an experimental, scientific footing rather than taste and convention.
- The core move: decompose graph reading into elementary perceptual tasks, then rank encodings by how accurately people decode them. That framing — encodings ranked by accuracy — is the ancestor of every "channel effectiveness" ranking in modern vis textbooks.
- The headline finding: position beats length beats angle/area — established with real measured error factors (position 1.4–2.5× better than length, ~2× better than angle), not just intuition.
- The prescription: pick encodings as high in the ranking as the data allows — which is why they push dot charts over pies and divided bars.
- The ranking was offered as a *tentative, revisable hypothesis* with explicit ties and admitted conjecture — the authors themselves say accuracy isn't the only thing a graph is for.
- The course returns to experiments later; note here how a perception experiment is even set up (stimuli, judgment task, error measure), not the statistical details.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Figure 1 (the ten tasks), the numbered ranking on p. 536, Figure 4 (what subjects actually judged), Figure 16 (the results in one dot plot), and Figures 22–25 plus 29 (pie→dot chart, framed-rectangle map) give ~90% of the value. Read the abstract and Section 6's self-assessment. Skip Sections 4.3–4.4 (midmeans, bootstrap, bias analysis — fine statistics, not needed on a skim) and the extended Section 5.4 material on lowess-era data-analysis graphics.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The precise ranking (p. 536): 1. Position along a common scale; 2. Positions along nonaligned scales; 3. Length, direction, angle; 4. Area; 5. Volume, curvature; 6. Shading, color saturation.
- From the abstract: "The conclusion is that radical surgery on these popular graphs is needed, and as replacements we offer alternative graphical forms — dot charts, dot charts with grouping, and framed-rectangle charts."
- Closing line: "If progress is to be made in graphics, we must be prepared to set aside old procedures when better ones are developed, just as is done in other areas of science."
- Tukey, quoted on shaded maps: maps "that some dignify by the name *statistical map* and that I would gladly revile with the name *patch map*."
- On the pie-vs-bar wars of the 1920s JASA (Eells 1926, Croxton 1927): "The contest appears to have ended in a draw. We conclude that neither graphical form should be used because other methods are demonstrably better."

**Caveats:** The paper itself flags most of what's worth flagging: the ordering is a "tentative working hypothesis," parts are conjectural with no experiment behind them (e.g., the position-common vs. position-nonaligned distinction), only position/length/angle were actually tested, and accuracy of value extraction is only one criterion for a good graph (they concede that if conveying exact numbers were the goal, "tables would be better"). Commonly over-claimed from this paper: that the full ten-task ranking is experimentally established (only slices are); that it applies to all uses of a chart (it's about quantitative ratio-estimation, not detection, gist, or categorical tasks — the authors say detection "must also be taken into account"); and that pies are proven useless (the finding is that angle judgments are ~2× less accurate than position for percentage estimation). Framed-rectangle maps, their own proposal, never caught on. Later work the paper obviously can't mention — notably Heer & Bostock's 2010 crowdsourced replication — broadly confirmed the position > length > angle > area ordering, which is worth knowing since this paper's specific numbers are otherwise dated (paper stimuli, 51 subjects, mostly Bell Labs-adjacent).


## Cleveland & McGill, "Graphical Perception and Graphical Methods for Analyzing Scientific Data" (Science, 1985)

**What it is:** The short, high-visibility version written for Science's general scientific audience a year later — same theory and ranking (lightly revised), a compressed account of now-three experiments, plus a second half that is really a tour of then-new statistical graphics (box plots, lowess, dot charts) for working scientists.

**AI summary:** Rather than rehashing the 1984 paper, note what's different. The list of elementary tasks changes: here the ten are angle, area, color hue, color saturation, density (amount of black), length (distance), position along a common scale, positions on identical but nonaligned scales, slope, and volume (Fig. 1). Color hue — excluded in 1984 — is now *in* and ranked; direction, curvature, and shading are gone (slope and density roughly take their place). The theory section is also new in emphasis: it leads with a worked example of *angle contamination of slope judgments* (Figs. 2–3), borrowing from Marr's and Stevens's computational-vision work on slant and tilt, showing why the same slope ratio looks different in differently shaped panels and why slope perception fails as line segments approach vertical or horizontal. The paper explicitly connects elementary graphical perception to Julesz's *preattentive vision* — "the instantaneous perception of the visual field that comes without apparent mental effort."

The ranking appears as Table 1, "Ordering elementary tasks by accuracy," now with seven ranks: 1. Position along a common scale; 2. Position on identical but nonaligned scales; 3. Length; 4. Angle / Slope (with θ not too close to 0, π/2, or π radians); 5. Area; 6. Volume, Density, Color saturation; 7. Color hue. Note the changes from 1984: length is untied and promoted above angle, slope gets its parenthetical warning, and hue lands at the bottom. Experimentally, the paper summarizes *three* experiments in one figure (Fig. 5): the two from 1984 plus a new Experiment 3 in which 127 subjects (24 high-school students, 60 college students, 43 technically trained professionals) judged seven task types — position (common), position (nonaligned), length, angle, slope, circle area, and "blob" area. Position and length were most accurate, angle and slope roughly tied, and area judgments were the least accurate. Two honest anomalies are reported: position-common and position-nonaligned came out nearly identical in Experiment 3, and position-common errors were much lower in Experiments 1–2 than in 3 — so parts of the ordering remain "partly conjectural." Fig. 9's box plots show accuracy did *not* depend on technical training, supporting the claim that these are basic visual judgments everyone performs daily.

The application section is more data-analysis flavored than 1984's chart-surgery campaign: the Mauna Loa CO₂ example (Fig. 6) shows that judging rate of change from slopes is misleading — the visual impression is two straight lines with a break around 1966, when the yearly changes actually double gradually — so "if it is important for viewers of a graph to appreciate the rate of change of graphed values, then rate of change itself should be graphed." Fig. 7 replaces a divided bar chart with a dot chart; Fig. 8 shows why superposed curves hide their vertical differences (the visual system extracts *minimum* distance, along perpendiculars). The final third — largely absent from the 1984 paper — is a primer on modern graphical methods: dot charts, Tukey box plots (with a full how-to-read explanation), graphing on a log base 2 scale, two-tiered error bars, and lowess scatterplot smoothing.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- This is the version most people actually cite the ranking from — Table 1, with seven ranks and hue at the bottom.
- The two papers' rankings and task lists differ (length untied and above angle; hue and density added; direction/curvature/shading dropped) — evidence the ranking was a living hypothesis, revised within a year, not a stone tablet.
- The stated design principle in one sentence: "options should be selected that result in perceptual tasks as high in the ordering as possible" — and the explicit caveat that "the ordering does not result in a precise prescription… but rather is a framework within which to work."
- The CO₂ example is the most teachable takeaway: don't make readers decode a quantity (rate of change) through a weak channel (slope); compute it and plot it with position.
- Elementary graphical decoding is tied to preattentive processing — the same concept the Wexler primer builds on.
- Accuracy didn't vary with subjects' technical training — the ranking is about human vision, not expertise.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Table 1 plus Figures 1, 5, and 6 give 90% of the value; Fig. 7 (divided bar → dot chart) and Fig. 8 (curve differences) round it out. The boxed Summary and the section "Ordering the Elementary Graphical-Perception Tasks" are the fastest honest read. Skip the trigonometric derivation of slope resolution and the whole final "Graphical Methods" section (box plots, log₂ scales, two-tiered error bars, lowess) unless you're curious what 1985 statistical graphics evangelism looked like.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Table 1's ranking, precisely: 1. Position along a common scale; 2. Position on identical but nonaligned scales; 3. Length; 4. Angle / Slope (with θ not too close to 0, π/2, or π radians); 5. Area; 6. Volume / Density / Color saturation; 7. Color hue.
- "The ordering should be thought of as a tentative working hypothesis, based on current information, that can be expected to evolve."
- The CO₂ punchline: the local rate of change from 1967 to 1980 "gradually increases by a factor of 2" — invisible when encoded as slope, obvious when plotted as position (Fig. 6, bottom).
- Experiment 3's subjects: 127 people across high school, college, and professional groups, with no accuracy difference among them (Fig. 9).
- Every recorded judgment was "what percentage the smaller magnitude was of the larger."

**Caveats:** Same core caveats as 1984, and the paper is again upfront: some ranks rest on no controlled experiment ("Aspects of the ordering are partly conjectural"), and the paper's own data failed to separate common-scale from nonaligned position judgments. Because it's the short version, people quote its Table 1 without the hedges that surround it. Over-claiming to watch for: treating the seven ranks as fine-grained gospel (rank 4's slope entry carries an explicit validity condition; ranks 4 and 6 are unbroken ties); reading it as "never use color for data" (the ranking concerns *quantitative* decoding — the 1984 version excluded hue precisely because it's better "for encoding categories rather than real variables"); and forgetting that both papers say accuracy of extraction isn't the whole story (detection and pattern organization matter too). The chart forms it evangelizes have mixed legacies — box plots and lowess won; framed rectangles and log₂ scales didn't.


## Shneiderman, "The Eyes Have It: A Task by Data Type Taxonomy for Information Visualizations" (IEEE Symposium on Visual Languages, 1996)

**What it is:** A short conference paper that states the **Visual Information Seeking Mantra** and organizes the (then-exploding) zoo of infovis prototypes into a **task by data type taxonomy (TTT)**: seven data types crossed with seven tasks.

**AI summary:** Shneiderman opens by arguing that information exploration "should be a joyous experience," and that visual displays exploit under-used human perceptual abilities — scanning, recognizing, and detecting changes in size, color, shape, movement, or texture. His organizing principle is the mantra: **"Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand"** — printed ten times in a row in the paper, with the explanation that each line represents a project in which he found himself rediscovering the principle. He is explicit that the mantra is "only a starting point."

The core contribution is the taxonomy. He assumes users view **collections of items with multiple attributes**, and proposes seven **data types** — **1-dimensional** (linear: text, source code, lists), **2-dimensional** (maps, floorplans), **3-dimensional** (real-world objects), **temporal** (items with start and finish times that may overlap — deliberately separated from 1-D), **multi-dimensional** (relational/statistical data as points in n-space), **tree** (hierarchies), and **network** (arbitrary links). Crossed with these are seven **tasks**: **overview, zoom, filter, details-on-demand, relate, history, extract**. Each data type gets a paragraph on characteristic user problems (e.g., adjacency and paths for 2-D; parent/child/sibling relationships for trees) plus a tour of period systems (treemaps, cone trees, hyperbolic trees, starfield displays/FilmFinder, LifeLines, parallel coordinates).

The task discussion doubles as a critique of the field circa 1996: overviews should support zoom factors of 3–30; filtering should update displays in under 100 ms (dynamic queries); details-on-demand is usually a pop-up; and *history* and *extract* are called out as tasks that "most prototypes fail to deal with." A final section on advanced filtering describes the filter-flow metaphor for graphical Boolean queries, with usability-study evidence. The summary predicts that successful commercial products will need to handle several data types and the full task list — a prediction that reads well in hindsight (Spotfire, descended from FilmFinder, is cited in the paper itself).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The mantra, verbatim, and — just as important — Shneiderman's own framing of it as a *starting point*, not a complete design theory.
- The idea of abstracting tasks and data types separately, so a technique built for one dataset can be recognized as applicable to another. This is the seed of "abstraction as a building block."
- The seven tasks as a vocabulary for *interaction-level* activity — note these are things a user does with an interface (zoom, filter, extract), not analytic questions. Contrast with Amar et al.
- The observation that a taxonomy "is useful only if it facilitates discussion and leads to useful discoveries" — a good standard for judging any scheme in this module.
- A sense of the 1996 landscape: treemaps, dynamic queries, fisheye views, starfields — many of which survived.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the abstract, Section 2 (the mantra), the framing paragraphs of Section 3, and the per-task discussion (especially the neglected *history* and *extract*). Skim the per-data-type example inventories (a tour of 1990s systems, useful mostly as historical color). Section 4 (advanced filtering / filter-flow Boolean queries) can be skipped for this module's purposes — it's a separate research thread.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The mantra: **"Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand"** — repeated ten times, each line "one project in which I found myself rediscovering this principle."
- Seven data types: **1-D, 2-D, 3-D, temporal, multi-dimensional, tree, network**.
- Seven tasks: **Overview, Zoom, Filter, Details-on-demand, Relate, History, Extract**.
- "Information exploration should be a joyous experience."
- Concrete engineering numbers: <100 ms filter feedback; overview zoom factors of 3–30; users take "10–20 minutes to accommodate to complex treemaps."
- Opens with a Gombrich (*Art and Illusion*) epigraph about needing "a developed system of schemata" to describe the visible world.

**Caveats:** The paper is often reduced to the mantra alone, and the mantra is often over-claimed as a universal design law — Shneiderman himself flags it as a starting point, and later literature has qualified it ("overview first" doesn't fit every scenario, monolithic large data, or search-first workflows). The "tasks" are interface actions, not analytic goals, so it doesn't tell you *what the user is trying to learn* — exactly the gap Amar et al. address. The system survey is thoroughly dated. Also, despite "task by data type taxonomy" in the title, the paper never actually presents the crossed matrix as a filled-in table — it's two lists plus discussion.


## Amar, Eagan & Stasko, "Low-Level Components of Analytic Activity in Information Visualization" (IEEE InfoVis, 2005)

**What it is:** An empirically grounded taxonomy of **ten low-level analytic tasks** — the specific questions people actually ask of data — derived by affinity-diagramming ~200 analysis questions that students posed about five datasets.

**AI summary:** The authors argue that existing task taxonomies (Wehrend & Lewis, Roth & Mattis, Zhou & Feiner, Shneiderman, Card, Chi) were built to serve *system design and presentation generation* rather than to describe user analysis. They frame this as **"representational primacy"** — a data-centric view that relies on user skill to generate insight — versus the **"analytic primacy"** they advocate: mapping visualization systems more closely to users' analytic goals. Motivating evidence includes studies where visualization tools produced insights domain experts didn't value, and commercial tools scoring only 68–75% accuracy on simple tasks.

Their method is the memorable part: students in a Spring 2004 infovis course generated **196 valid analysis questions** about five datasets (cereals, mutual funds, cars, films, grocery surveys) that they then had to answer with commercial tools (Spotfire, Table Lens, InfoZoom, SeeIT). Affinity diagramming of the questions yielded **ten task primitives**, each given a **pro forma abstract** — a fill-in-the-blank template like "What are the values of attributes {X, Y, Z} in the data cases {A, B, C}?" — plus concrete example questions ("What is the mileage per gallon of the Audi TT?").

The discussion section is where the thinking deepens. Real questions are often **compound tasks** — compositions of primitives (e.g., "Who starred in the most films in 1978?" = Retrieve Value + Compute Derived Value + Find Extremum). Some things are deliberately *excluded*: basic mathematical **comparison** is treated as a meta-operation below the taxonomy's level; **higher-level questions** ("How do mutual funds get rated?") sit above it; and questions with **uncertain criteria** ("Do cereals X, Y, Z sound tasty?") involve value judgments beyond the primitives. The authors explicitly position the set not as a "normative picture" but as **"a vocabulary for discussion"** and an informal checklist for evaluating what a system's representations and interactions actually support. They also candidly note methodological limits: students saw the tools before writing questions, and the glut of correlation questions may reflect Spotfire's scatterplots.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The ten tasks by name — this is the vocabulary that stuck, still used to organize perception studies and system evaluations today.
- The level distinction: these are *low-level analytic questions*, sitting between high-level knowledge goals ("understand trends in movie popularity") above and perceptual/mathematical operations (comparison) below. Knowing which level you're talking about is most of the skill of task abstraction.
- The idea of **composing primitives**: real tasks are compounds, and decomposing a fuzzy user question into primitives is exactly the abstraction exercise the course wants you to practice.
- "Representational primacy" vs. "analytic primacy" — a compact way to say: start from what the user needs to find out, not from what the picture can show.
- The taxonomy as *checklist and vocabulary*, not a completeness proof — the authors say so themselves.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: Sections 1, 3, 4 (the ten tasks with their pro forma abstracts — read all ten, they're short), and Section 5 (compound tasks and omissions — this is where the intellectual content is). Skim Section 2 (related-work tour of older taxonomies) and Section 6.1 (detailed comparison with Wehrend & Lewis). The methodological-concerns subsection (6.3) is worth a quick read as a model of honest self-critique.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The ten tasks: **Retrieve Value, Filter, Compute Derived Value, Find Extremum, Sort, Determine Range, Characterize Distribution, Find Anomalies, Cluster, Correlate**.
- The corpus: 196 questions, five datasets — **cereals, mutual funds, cars, films, grocery surveys**.
- Pro forma abstract format, e.g. Correlate: "What is the correlation between attributes X and Y over a given set S of data cases?"
- The Filter subtlety: "Which cereals have *high* fiber?" is Filter (needs an operating definition, independent of other cases); "Which cereal has the *highest* fiber?" is Find Extremum (relative to the whole set).
- Terms of art: **data case**, **attribute**, **aggregation function**; the framing pair **representational primacy / analytic primacy**.

**Caveats:** The corpus is students-in-a-course, not professional analysts — the authors themselves speculate that experts doing exploratory hypothesis formation might yield different tasks, and that the tools shown in class biased the questions (lots of correlation because Spotfire has scatterplots). The set is explicitly not complete: comparison, higher-level exploration, and value-laden judgments are outside it, which is commonly forgotten when the ten tasks are treated as *the* task taxonomy. It also says nothing about interaction (no "zoom") by design — pairing it with Shneiderman is what gives coverage. And it's a vocabulary, not a design procedure: naming the task doesn't yet tell you the encoding.


## Gleicher, Riveiro, von Landesberger, Deussen, Chang & Gillmann, "A Problem Space for Designing Visualizations" (IEEE CG&A, 2023)

*(Note: this is Mike's own paper — summarized straight; his personal take goes on the re-papering page separately. A papers/problem-space draft page already exists on the site.)*

**What it is:** A viewpoint article proposing a **problem space** for visualization design: six axes — the journalistic **5 Ws and How** — for describing the *problem* a visualization must solve, deliberately kept independent of any solution.

**AI summary:** The core tenet, borrowed from design and product-development literature, is the **separation of problem from solution**: articulating needs independently of how they'll be met focuses work on the right problems, frees the search for solutions from preconceptions, and enables communication and checking. The authors even note that presuming the answer will be "a visualization" already conflates problem and solution — "it might be better to call this a problem space of data analysis and communication problems." The framework adapts Schulz et al.'s "Design Space of Visualization Tasks" (which also used the 5Ws+H) but shifts the focus from formalizing *task* to broadly characterizing *the problem*.

The six axes: **Who** has the problem (audience characteristics — expertise, motivation, spatial ability, accessibility — plus other stakeholders like developers and maintainers); **Why** do they need it (the goal — the axis that "most closely aligns with the common notion of task"); **What** is the data (form, but also semantics, scale, time-dependence); **When** in the analysis process (exploration vs. communication vs. justification suggest different designs); **Where** is the context (physical place, display, social setting, time pressure, stakes); and **How** do they expect to be helped — carefully defined as the users' *pre-conceived expectations* of a solution (design constraints or biases to acknowledge), not the solution itself. A useful practical trick appears under Why: asking "Why?" of an over-specific task description moves you up the abstraction ladder; asking "How?" moves you down — but risks smuggling the solution back in.

The framework is grounded in three worked use cases — **keyhole surgery planning** (the running example), **hospital infection-pathway tracing**, and **investigative-journalism document exploration** — each walked through axis by axis. A self-assessment section evaluates the space against Kerracher and Kennedy's criteria (relevant, independent, thorough, complete, orthogonal, precise), with honest concessions: the axes are "ultimately inter-twined or at least correlated," and precision was not a goal. A sidebar surveys the task-abstraction literature (Wehrend & Lewis through Brehmer & Munzner and Schulz et al.), positioning task as "one of many aspects that must be considered." The intended uses: a **checklist** for design, evaluation, and documentation, and a **vocabulary** for articulating problems consistently.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The six questions — Who, Why, What, When, Where, How — as a checklist you can actually run before designing anything.
- The module's headline point: **task (the Why) is just one axis of six**. Task abstraction, the subject of the other readings, characterizes the goal but not the audience, context, process phase, data realities, or user expectations.
- The discipline of separating problem from solution — and the specific trap that "How" names: users and designers arrive with preconceptions that should be surfaced as constraints or biases, not silently baked in.
- The "Why?/How?" laddering trick for finding the right level of task abstraction.
- Uncertainty *about* the problem specification (unknown users, unknown display) is itself design-relevant information, distinct from uncertainty *in* the data.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the introduction, Table 1, and the six axis sections (short, with the surgery example threaded through). The sidebar on task abstractions is an efficient map of this whole module's literature — worth careful reading as orientation. Skim the two additional use cases (they repeat the pattern) and the "Is the Problem Space Useful or Good?" self-evaluation, though note its candor about non-orthogonality.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Table 1, the six axes: **WHO** (who has the problem / will use it), **WHY** (objective), **WHAT** (data), **WHEN** (phase of analysis), **WHERE** (context), **HOW** (how they *expect* to be helped).
- The one-sentence version: "a visualization is intended to help someone (the who) do something (the why) with the data (the what) in some phase of the analysis process (the when) and in some context (the where) using some method (the how)."
- The Aristotle hook: the 5Ws trace back at least to Aristotle's rhetoric, who "argued that ignorance of any of these elements can lead to faulty reasoning."
- The three use-case figures: surgery-planning multi-view tool (Fig. 1), infection-tracing dashboard (Fig. 2), journalism document-exploration dashboard (Fig. 3).

**Caveats:** It's a viewpoint/position piece, not an empirical result — the framework is justified by the authors' design experience and post-hoc application to already-completed projects, which they acknowledge. The mapping onto the 5Ws is admitted to be "a little contrived," and the axes aren't truly orthogonal or precise (also admitted). It offers a checklist, not a method: it tells you what to consider, not how to get answers or convert them into a design. Readers sometimes expect a taxonomy with categories to fill in; deliberately, there isn't one.


## Mackinlay, "Automating the Design of Graphical Presentations of Relational Information" (ACM TOG, 1986)

**What it is:** The paper that formalized visualization as **encoding**: graphical presentations are treated as *sentences of graphical languages*, judged by **expressiveness** and **effectiveness** criteria, and generated automatically by a prototype system, **APT (A Presentation Tool)**.

**AI summary:** Mackinlay's goal is an application-independent **presentation tool** that automatically designs effective 2-D static presentations (bar charts, scatter plots, connected graphs) of relational data, so application builders needn't be graphic-design experts. Two problems follow: codifying graphic design knowledge, and generating enough design variety to cover arbitrary inputs (he estimates over **17,000** possible design problems for just two binary relations, over 21 billion for four). His fundamental move is linguistic: a chart is a sentence with precise syntax (arrangements of graphical objects) and semantics (an *Encodes* relation between graphical properties and facts).

**Expressiveness** is a two-part criterion: a set of facts is expressible in a graphical language if some sentence "(1) encodes **all the facts** in the set, (2) encodes **only the facts** in the set." Both halves get worked examples: a single position axis provably *cannot* express a one-to-many relation (a mark can't be in two places), and a bar chart of cars' *nation* — a nominal attribute — fails the "only" condition because bar lengths inevitably encode an ordering that isn't in the data ("as if the USA cars were longer or better"). **Effectiveness** he treats as explicitly conjectural, since "there does not yet exist an empirically verified theory of human perceptual capabilities." Focusing on accuracy of interpretation, he builds on **Cleveland and McGill's** ranking of perceptual tasks for quantitative data (position most accurate, down through length, angle, slope, area, volume, to color/density) and — the paper's most-reused contribution — **extends it to ordinal and nominal data** with separate rankings per data type: position tops all three columns, but color, near the bottom for quantitative data, "is a very effective way of encoding nominal sets." He flags plainly that this extension "has not been empirically verified."

The generative machinery is a **composition algebra**: a basis set of primitive graphical languages built from **Bertin's** vocabulary (marks — points, lines, areas — with positional and **retinal** properties: color, shape, size, saturation, texture, orientation), combined by three operators — **double-axes**, **single-axis**, and **mark composition** — under the **Principle of Composition** ("Compose two designs by merging parts that encode the same information"). Ties among expressive designs are broken by the **Principle of Importance Ordering**: "Encode more important information more effectively." APT implements all this as generate-and-test search in a logic program (~200 rules, 1–2 minutes per design on a Symbolics LISP machine), with backtracking, and is sensitive to the output medium — given color, it produces a scatterplot with color-coded repair records; restricted to monochrome, it reasons its way to an aligned bar chart instead.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The two criteria, as a pair of design questions you can ask of any chart: does it show *all and only* the data (**expressiveness**)? and does it use the most accurately-read channels for the most important data (**effectiveness**)?
- Expressiveness comes *first* — effectiveness only ranks designs that already express the data correctly. The nominal-bar-chart example is the canonical illustration of a chart that lies by construction.
- The effectiveness ranking *depends on data type* — one ranking per quantitative/ordinal/nominal, not one universal list. This is the table that echoes through Munzner's textbook and beyond.
- Why this paper is in Tableau's ancestry: encode-by-rules plus rankings is exactly what automatic chart pickers (Show Me, and every "recommended chart" feature since) operationalize.
- Charts aren't primitives; they're *compositions* of encodings — a scatterplot is two position encodings; a chart with sized, colored points is a mark composition. This is the "charts as encodings" idea in 1986 form.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the abstract, Section 3 (the price/mileage example — scatterplot vs. labeled scatterplot vs. aligned bar charts sets up all three concerns), Section 4, the *prose and examples* of Sections 5–6 (the two expressiveness conditions, the Nation bar chart, Figures 14–15 and the area analysis in Figure 16), and Section 9 (media sensitivity — a satisfying trace of the whole system reasoning). Skim or skip the formal machinery: the predicate-logic definitions, Theorem 1's proof, the formal composition-operator definitions (7.2), and the implementation details (Section 8). The ideas survive intact without the notation.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Expressiveness, near-verbatim: a set of facts is expressible if there is a sentence that "encodes all the facts in the set" and "encodes only the facts in the set." "Expressing additional information is potentially dangerous because it may not be correct."
- The Cleveland & McGill–based quantitative accuracy ordering (Fig. 14): **position, length, angle, slope, area, volume, color/density** — extended in Fig. 15 to per-type rankings where **position is #1 for all three data types** and color hue jumps from bottom (quantitative) to near-top (nominal). *(Extraction note: the two-column layout of Fig. 15 scrambled in text extraction; the claims here are the ones the surrounding prose states directly.)*
- **Principle of Importance Ordering:** "Encode more important information more effectively."
- **Principle of Composition:** "Compose two designs by merging parts that encode the same information."
- Key figures: Fig. 11 (the misleading nominal bar chart), Figs. 5/6/7 (scatterplot vs. labeled scatterplot vs. aligned bar chart), Fig. 15 (the ranking table), Figs. 30/31 (same data, color vs. monochrome designs).

**Caveats:** Mackinlay himself labels the effectiveness theory "conjectural" and the ordinal/nominal extension unverified — treating Fig. 15 as settled perceptual law is the classic over-claim (and precisely what Bertini pushes back on). The "all and only the facts" standard is stricter than real practice (annotation, redundant encoding, and embellishment all technically violate it). And the AI framing (logic programming, generate-and-test) is period technology — the *criteria* are the durable contribution, not the search engine.


## Bertini, "Beyond Precision: Expressiveness in Visualization" (Fell in Love With Data, Substack, 2022)

**What it is:** A blog essay by a visualization professor arguing that the standard teaching story — rank the channels by precision, assign the most important data to the best channels — is "neither sufficient nor necessary" for effective design, and that **expressiveness** (semantic match between what you want to convey and what the encoding conveys) deserves the central role.

**AI summary:** Bertini states the orthodoxy he's grown uncomfortable teaching in three steps: visualization maps **data values to visual channels**; some channels are **better** than others; therefore **choose the best channels**. This pedagogy, descending from **Bertin** through **Cleveland and McGill** to the channel-effectiveness ranking in **Munzner's textbook** (which he calls his favorite and still teaches from), fails in two ways. First, position-based charts aren't always better: why does anyone use a heat-map when line charts exist? Second, the ranking can't discriminate *among* designs that all use position. His best anecdote: students asked to compare NYC boroughs by crash injuries and deaths produced scatterplots — technically position-optimal but wrong for a ranking task — and when challenged, protested **"But you said position is the best channel!"** — and he "could not point to any element in my course that would justify my recommendation. That is when I realized there was a gap." He adds a Franconeri example (four position-based arrangements of the same data that are far from equivalent, from their joint position paper "Why Shouldn't All Charts Be Scatter Plots?") and Alberto Cairo's re-sort of a Georgia Department of Public Health bar chart, where ordering alone changes the perceived pattern.

His alternative is **expressiveness**, which he credits to "the foundational work of Jock Mackinlay, who I believe introduced the term in the first place": data contains information we want to *express*; visual properties *express* certain kinds of information; good design is a **good match** between the two. Worked examples: a **diverging vs. sequential color scale** — the diverging scale expresses "there is a meaningful zero/threshold and two sides," a matter of semantic matching, "in fact a diverging color scale may even be less precise!"; and the pie chart, which despite orthodoxy expresses a **part-to-whole** relationship that a bar chart of absolute values doesn't (he still prefers a single stacked bar).

Two headings carry the argument forward. **"There is more to express"**: beyond categorical/ordinal/quantitative (he notes Munzner's magnitude vs. identity channels go partway), designers may need to express *directionality, part-to-whole, order, grouping, space/time*. **"More can be expressed"**: the channel list is under-specified — calling position "a" channel is "kind of funny" when position works so differently on one axis, orthogonal axes, polar coordinates, maps, or as grouping/alignment, and differently again for dots vs. bars vs. polygons. He also flags an overlooked flip side: expressiveness is equally about **not accidentally conveying information you didn't intend**. He closes honestly — granularity is a real problem, expressiveness-based guidance needs empirical validation against measurable effectiveness — and with the quotable thesis: "We do not visualize *data*, we visualize *ideas* based on data" (linking Hullman's visualization-rhetoric work, plus pointers to Tversky's congruence principle and Cognitive Fit Theory).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The rankings answer "how precisely will this be read?" — but most design choices (heat-map vs. line chart, sorting bars, diverging vs. sequential color, pie vs. bar) turn on *what the arrangement says*, not how precisely it's read.
- Expressiveness and effectiveness are *both* Mackinlay's criteria — this post is a corrective to teaching that kept the effectiveness ranking and let expressiveness shrink to "don't use hue for quantities."
- The scatterplot anecdote as a self-test: a design can win on channel precision and still be wrong *for the task* — encoding choices must answer to task abstraction, tying this reading back to Shneiderman/Amar.
- The negative-space rule: check what your chart accidentally expresses (spurious order from bar lengths, spurious two-sidedness from diverging colors), not just what it intends.
- "Position" is not one thing — common scale, separate axes, polar, spatial, grouping — so "use position" is under-determined advice.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the opening three-step story and its two problems, the student scatterplot anecdote, the "Enter Expressiveness" section with the color-scale and pie examples, and the paragraph on accidentally conveying unintended information. Skim "What can we do?" and "Why does it matter?" (research agenda for the field, less actionable for a learner) and the P.s. (a pointer list to Tversky and Cognitive Fit Theory he admits he hasn't yet processed). The linked Correll/Bertini/Franconeri arXiv paper is the formal follow-up for readers who want more.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The orthodox syllogism he attacks: (1) map data values to channels, (2) some channels are better, (3) choose the best channels — "Except, it does not work very well."
- Students: **"But you said position is the best channel!"**
- "Deciding whether to use a diverging or non-diverging color scale is not a matter of *precision* or *accuracy* (in fact a diverging color scale may even be less precise!), it's a matter of good semantic matching."
- His off-the-top-of-head list of things to express: **directionality, part-to-whole, order, grouping, space/time**.
- "We do not visualize *data*, we visualize *ideas* based on data."
- Anchors: Munzner's channel-ranking figure; the heat-map vs. line chart pair; the four-position-charts Franconeri figure; Cairo's Georgia DPH re-sort.

**Caveats:** It's an opinion essay, not a study — Bertini says himself that expressiveness-based guidance still needs empirical validation. It's a corrective, not a demolition: he keeps precision as "a useful factor" and keeps Munzner's book; students sometimes over-read it as "the rankings are wrong" when the claim is "the rankings are incomplete." The mild irony worth flagging: he's arguing for a fuller reading of *Mackinlay*, where expressiveness always came first — so this pairs with the Mackinlay summary above, not against it. And his own proposals (richer characterizations of position, of things-to-express) are explicitly unfinished — "who knows" whether they lead anywhere, by his own admission.


## North, "Toward Measuring Visualization Insight" (IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 2006)

**What it is:** A short "Visualization Viewpoints" opinion piece by Chris North arguing that if insight is visualization's purpose, then the field's dominant evaluation method — controlled experiments on benchmark tasks — is poorly suited to measuring it, and sketching two ways to move evaluation closer to insight.

**AI summary:** North opens from a provocation about the 2005 IEEE Visualization conference: alongside genuinely inspiring evaluation work, there were "a few too many Boolean usability studies" that only asked whether users liked a tool or not. This leads him to restate a claim he treats as widely (if informally) held: **the purpose of visualization is insight, and the purpose of visualization evaluation is to determine to what degree visualizations achieve this purpose.** Since insight is rarely defined rigorously, he proposes five **characteristics of insight** instead of a formal definition: **complex** (involves large amounts of data synergistically, not single values), **deep** (builds up over time, generates further questions), **qualitative** (uncertain, subjective, multiple resolutions), **unexpected** (serendipitous, creative), and **relevant** (connects data to domain knowledge and impact). He illustrates complexity with a running example — finding a max/min value is simple insight; recognizing a distribution's shape (his Figure 1 histogram of US county rent data) is far more complex.

North then turns to the standard evaluation method — controlled experiments on **benchmark tasks** — and argues it is structurally opposed to those five characteristics. His **benchmark-task critique** lists four fundamental problems: tasks must be *predefined* by administrators (no room for unexpected insight); they need *definitive completion times*, usually under a minute (no room for deep insight); they need *definitive, Boolean-scorable answers* (no room for qualitative insight); and they require *simple, easily specified answers* like clicking an object or naming an identifier (no room for complex, relevant insight). He notes the field's default benchmark tasks — "find the record meeting attribute criteria," "find the record with the max value of attribute X" — are often solvable faster by a simple query or summary statistic than by any visualization, which undercuts their relevance as a proxy for insight. He also flags the implicit assumption that complex tasks decompose hierarchically into simple ones as unproven, and points out confounds like the performance/accuracy tradeoff (is a "faster" visualization actually better, or are users just guessing and giving up sooner?).

The article then proposes two remedies, in increasing order of radicalism. The first, modest step is to write **more complex benchmark tasks** — e.g., asking users to characterize a distribution's shape from a multiple-choice list ("normal," "uniform," "linearly increasing") or to make estimation judgments (coverage, cluster density) scored as continuous rather than Boolean values. This helps but still constrains users to predefined questions, and costs experimenters longer, noisier tasks and larger sample sizes. The second, more radical proposal is to eliminate benchmark tasks entirely, converting them from an independent variable to a dependent one: instead of assigning tasks, let users explore data freely and observe what insights they generate. This is his **insight-based methodology**, built on three innovations: an **open-ended protocol** (users explore until they feel they've learned all they can, seeded by initial questions but expected to go beyond them), **qualitative insight analysis** (a think-aloud protocol where each reported finding is coded as an "insight occurrence," rated on dimensions like category, complexity/depth on a 1–5 scale, and time-to-generate), and an **emphasis on domain relevance** (using real domain-expert participants and domain-expert coders to judge the value of insights, not just visualization experts). He acknowledges the costs — longer sessions, heavier coding effort, the need for motivated domain experts as both subjects and coders, and greater subjectivity and variance in results — but argues these are resource problems, not fundamental ones. He closes pragmatically: both methods are needed together (benchmark tasks for low-level perceptual effects, open-ended insight studies for the "grand purpose"), and if combined in one session, benchmark tasks should come *after* the open-ended portion so they don't anchor users' thinking.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:** 
- Learn to separate a visualization's *stated purpose* (generating insight) from the *proxy* researchers actually measure (task time and accuracy on artificial benchmark tasks) — and to ask, for any evaluation, whether the metric really represents the goal.
- Take away North's five characteristics of insight (complex, deep, qualitative, unexpected, relevant) as a checklist for critiquing any evaluation design, including your own course projects.
- Understand the specific mechanism of the mismatch: benchmark tasks require predefined, quick, Boolean-scorable, simple answers — each requirement directly cancels one of the five characteristics of insight.
- See a concrete methodological alternative — the open-ended, think-aloud, insight-coding protocol — as a template for "how would I evaluate this differently if I cared about insight rather than speed."
- Notice the performance/accuracy confound argument; it generalizes well beyond this paper to any speed-vs-correctness tradeoff in an evaluation.
- Connect this reading directly to the "Convince [who] that your [what] is [good how]" framing from the evaluation lecture: North is essentially arguing that most benchmark-task studies convince nobody that a visualization is *insightful*, only that it's *fast at a narrow query*.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Careful-read. It's four pages and reads quickly; nothing here should be skipped. The two dense lists (five characteristics of insight; four benchmark-task problems) are the load-bearing content and worth re-reading slowly. The two proposed methods sections can be read a bit faster once the critique lands, since they're more sketch than fully worked-out methodology.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Opening epigraph (Merriam-Webster): "Insight: The capacity to discern the true nature of a situation; The act or outcome of grasping the inward or hidden nature of things or of perceiving in an intuitive manner."
- The claim he's testing: "The purpose of visualization is insight. The purpose of visualization evaluation is to determine to what degree visualizations achieve this purpose."
- His complaint about the 2005 Vis conference: "there were also a few too many Boolean usability studies that offered only two alternatives: either the users liked the visualization tool in question, or they did not."
- The five characteristics, verbatim as headers: *Complex*, *Deep*, *Qualitative*, *Unexpected*, *Relevant.*
- The four benchmark-task problems, each ending in a "leaving little room for..." refrain — e.g., "They must be predefined by test administrators... leaving little room for unexpected insight," down through "...leaving little room for complex and relevant insight."
- Example benchmark tasks he singles out as too simplistic: "find the data record that meets the following attribute criteria" and "find which record has the maximum value of attribute X."
- The treemap example: "treemaps make it easy to find the largest rectangle, but probably distorts the recognition of rectangle size distribution."
- Depth coding scale: "1 represents a simple obvious fact in the data and 5 represents a deep inference that integrates multiple data types."
- Sequencing advice for combined studies: "the benchmark tasks should not precede the open-ended portion. Otherwise, user thinking will become constrained by the benchmark tasks."
- Figure 1: histogram of median monthly US county rent, generated in SAS JMP, with southeastern counties highlighted in dark — used purely to demonstrate that "recognizing this shape" is a more complex insight than finding a single value.

**Figures worth borrowing:** None really earn it. Figure 1 (the rent histogram) is a real dataset visualization used as a complexity example, not a conceptual diagram — it illustrates the "complex" characteristic reasonably well as a "here's what a complex insight looks like" teaching image, but it isn't diagrammatic and isn't essential; the concepts (the five characteristics, the four benchmark-task problems, the open-ended-vs-benchmark distinction) are better rendered as original VisSnacks diagrams than borrowed from the paper, which has no schematic/process figures at all.

**Caveats:** This is an opinion/viewpoints piece, not an empirical study — North is arguing a position and pointing to his own prior work (ref. 5, Saraiya, North & Duca 2005) as the fuller worked example of the insight-based methodology; students should know the "insight-based methodology" is developed in more depth elsewhere and this piece is the manifesto version. The definition of insight remains deliberately informal (a list of characteristics, not operational criteria), which is both the paper's honest strength and its main weakness — it's been criticized in the years since for being hard to operationalize and for the resulting insight-counting methodologies being just as susceptible to coder bias and low reliability as the benchmark methods it critiques. In practice, the field's evaluation practice has only partly followed this program: benchmark-task controlled experiments remain extremely common (arguably still the default in CHI/VIS papers) because they're cheaper, more replicable, and easier to publish, while insight-based/qualitative studies are used more in applied/design-study and long-term case-study contexts (this is very much the tension the course's nested-model discussion revisits — different levels of the nested model call for different evaluation types, and North's binary of "benchmark tasks vs. open-ended insight studies" is a simplification of a richer evaluation-methods landscape that includes think-aloud usability studies, longitudinal deployments, and design studies not framed in insight terms at all). Also dated: written in 2006, before visual analytics evaluation (VAST challenges), crowdsourced/online experiment platforms, and more recent replication-crisis-driven scrutiny of qualitative coding reliability — all of which have reshaped how "insight-based" evaluation is actually practiced.


## Rind, Aigner, Wagner, Miksch & Lammarsch, "Task Cube: A Three-Dimensional Conceptual Space of User Tasks in Visualization Design and Evaluation" (Information Visualization, 2016)

**What it is:** A terminology paper that untangles the overloaded word "task" in visualization by identifying three orthogonal dimensions — **perspective**, **abstraction**, **composition** — that together form the "task cube," and by proposing two precise replacement terms, **objective** (the why) and **action** (the how), for what the field has been calling "task."

**AI summary:** The authors start from a diagnosis: "task" is used so inconsistently in visualization and HCI that it obstructs communication, citing Preece et al.'s 1994 remark that "the notion of 'task' is increasingly difficult to pin down" and Munzner's characterization of it as "deeply overloaded in the visualization literature." Rather than propose yet another taxonomy of individual tasks, they build a **conceptual space** for talking about task *concepts* (Figure 1). Its three dimensions: **abstraction** — concrete tasks ("find the quarter of Google's largest revenue") versus abstract tasks expressed via generic task-framework categories ("indirect lookup"); **composition** — a continuous high-level-to-low-level scale reflecting how tasks decompose into subtasks (long-term challenges down to small steps); and **perspective** — the *why* (ends, intended outcomes) versus *how* (means, executed steps) dichotomy, borrowed from Brehmer and Munzner's "ends–means ambiguity." The paper argues these three are non-redundant: abstraction and composition are both distinguishable because a low-level task is *part of* a high-level task (mereology) while an abstract task is a more generic *category of* a concrete task (subclassing) — the same distinction as part-of versus subclass-of in object-oriented programming.

Perspective gets the sharpest technical treatment. **Objective** is defined, adapting Roth: "an objective is a question on data that the user raises for inquiry, consideration, or solution while aiming to solve a problem or satisfy a curiosity." **Action** is defined, adapting Gotz and Zhou: "an action is a discrete step towards addressing an objective." Crucially, the paper insists there is *no direct decomposition relationship* between objectives and actions — a why-hierarchy of subobjectives and a how-hierarchy of action sequences/action patterns both exist and can each be composed or decomposed at multiple levels, but the mapping between the two hierarchies is many-to-many and tool-dependent (an EHR-specific tool might let one action satisfy an objective that a general-purpose tool needs many low-level actions to reach). This is why perspective can't be collapsed into composition, and — since both objectives and actions can themselves be described at different levels of abstraction (the concrete objective above can be abstracted as "indirect lookup"; a scatterplot axis change can be abstracted as "reconfigure," "arrange," or "visualize") — why perspective can't be collapsed into abstraction either.

An extended worked example (the VAST Challenge 2014 "Kronos Incident") walks the concepts down four levels of composition, from the high-level objective "support law enforcement" through subobjectives ("reconstruct networks of people"), to a lower-level objective ("identify the leaders" of a suspect organization), down to the concrete low-level objectives and actions a real winning team actually performed (search-box term co-occurrence, then a date-range widget) — showing objectives and actions can be abstracted independently using existing frameworks (Brehmer & Munzner's why-typology, Andrienko & Andrienko's comparison-task categories, Yi et al.'s and Gotz & Zhou's action vocabularies).

A "Comparison to literature" section systematically works through how Preece et al., Munzner, Roth, Norman, Gotz and Zhou, Fuchs, and ConcurTaskTrees each carve up the goal→activity→task→action→operation hierarchy (summarized in Figure 3, a table lining up five different authors' composition-level vocabularies side by side) and how each addresses why versus how — showing the same confusion of vocabulary the paper is trying to fix. "Role of tasks in visualization" then proposes a process model (Figure 4, extending Sacha et al.'s knowledge generation model) in which a user's objective and background knowledge drive a plan/hypothesis, which is executed as a sequence of actions, producing findings that accumulate into insights, which can trigger replanning — objectives sit on the human side of a human/computer boundary, actions are what cross it.

The paper's remaining two sections apply the framework practically. "Implications for design and evaluation" walks through six scenarios — domain characterization, designing visualization artifacts, stimuli for controlled experiments, setting context in case studies, studying interactive analysis/reasoning from logs, and integrating objective/action tracking into artifacts at runtime — each with real design-study examples (RelEx, LiveGantt, ChronoLenses, SemanticTimeZoom, Sandbox, Aruvi). "Categorizations of abstract tasks" then surveys **37 existing task-categorization frameworks** (Table 1) and classifies each by why/how, by composition level (high/intermediate/low), and by type of abstraction (generic, data-type-specific, domain-specific, tool-architecture-specific) — finding a majority (24 of 37) are objective-oriented, that high-level objective categorizations are rare (real high-level objectives tend to be too domain-specific to categorize generically), that action categorizations cluster at the low-composition end, and that generic abstractions dominate both columns.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The headline terminological fix: stop saying "task" when you mean **objective** (why, a question raised about data) or **action** (how, a discrete executable step) — and say which you mean.
- The three-dimension diagnosis of *why* "task" is confusing: people conflate abstraction (concrete↔abstract), composition (high-level↔low-level), and perspective (why↔how) when they're actually orthogonal. A given "task" statement can be moved along any of the three independently.
- The key insight for the module: objectives and actions don't decompose into each other in a fixed way — the same objective can require one action in a well-designed tool and ten in a poorly designed one. This is a strong argument for stating design requirements as *objectives*, not as action sequences, since actions are an artifact of the tool you haven't built yet.
- A ready-made worked example (the Kronos Incident decomposition, Figure 2) of walking from a vague high-level goal down to concrete, executed actions — a template for how to do task analysis on your own project.
- The survey (Table 1, 37 frameworks) functions as an annotated map of nearly every task taxonomy the module cites elsewhere (Shneiderman, Amar et al., Brehmer & Munzner, Andrienko & Andrienko, Yi et al.) — useful as a cross-reference once you've read those individually.
- The "why not just define one universal intermediate level" question, and the paper's answer: visualization objectives are too open-ended and situational for one categorization to serve every scenario — hence a *space* to navigate, not a single taxonomy to adopt.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: the abstract, the "Conceptual space" section (Abstraction/Composition/Perspective definitions and Figure 1), the objective/action definitions, and the Kronos Incident "Example" section (it's the fastest way to make the abstract dimensions concrete). Figure 4 (the process model) and the six-scenario "Implications for design and evaluation" section are worth a careful read for anyone doing a design study or planning an evaluation. Skim the long "Comparison to literature" section (Figure 3's table captures its payoff; the surrounding paragraph-by-paragraph author-by-author walk is reference material, not essential reading) and skim Table 1's 37-row survey — scan it for frameworks you already know, rather than reading every row.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The two core definitions, verbatim: "an objective is a question on data that the user raises for inquiry, consideration, or solution while aiming to solve a problem or satisfy a curiosity"; "an action is a discrete step towards addressing an objective."
- The three axes of the cube (Figure 1): **perspective** (why / how), **abstraction** (concrete / abstract), **composition** (low-level / high-level).
- The line distinguishing objectives that don't count: "We would not consider an assignment such as 'pan to the year 1983' or 'click on the blue rectangle' as an objective. Such assignments are purely perceptual and mechanical and do not carry the notion of the user raising an inquiry."
- The Kronos Incident example chain: "support law enforcement" → "reconstruct networks of people" → "identify the leaders" (of the POK) → search co-occurring terms "POK" and "leader," then browse word clouds by time range.

- Figure 3's five-column comparison of composition vocabularies (Cooper et al., Preece et al., Norman, Gotz and Zhou, Fuchs) — goal/activity/task/action/operation, none of the five agreeing on where the tool-specific boundary falls.

**Figures worth borrowing:** Figure 1 (the task-cube diagram itself — perspective/abstraction/composition as three labeled axes) is the paper's single most reusable image and a strong candidate for a VisSnacks page introducing task abstraction generally. **Caveats:** This is a terminology/conceptual-framework paper, not an empirical one — the 37-framework survey (Table 1) is explicitly "non-exhaustive" and the classification of each framework into why/how, composition level, and abstraction type was a subjective consensus call by the five authors, not a validated coding. The proposed "objective"/"action" terminology, while argued carefully, has not achieved anything like universal adoption in the field the way "high-level/low-level" or Brehmer & Munzner's typology has — readers should treat it as one strong terminological proposal among several competing ones, not as settled community consensus. The paper also concedes its own limits candidly: composition has "no general classification rules," the dimensions are "clearly distinct in a theoretical view" but "difficult to separate... in practice," and the ambiguities surfaced in the survey "might also be inherent as a 3D-covariance that deserves deeper investigation" — i.e., the authors don't claim the cube fully resolves the messiness they diagnose, only that it gives better language for discussing it. Common over-read to watch for: treating "objective" and "action" as if they were the paper's main contribution — the authors themselves frame the *three-dimensional space* (which lets you diagnose *which* kind of task-confusion is happening) as the primary contribution, with the terminology as a secondary, more contestable proposal.
