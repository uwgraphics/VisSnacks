## Munzner, "What's Vis, and Why Do It?" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 1)

**What it is:** The opening chapter of Munzner's textbook: a definition of visualization unpacked clause by clause, ending with the what–why–how analysis framework that structures the rest of the book.

**AI summary:** The chapter is built around one carefully worded definition: "Computer-based **visualization** systems provide visual representations of datasets designed to help people carry out tasks more effectively." Rather than surveying chart types, Munzner spends the chapter justifying each part of that sentence through a series of "why" questions (sections 1.2–1.14). Vis earns its keep when you need to *augment* human capabilities rather than replace people with automatic methods — it shines for ill-specified problems where you don't know in advance what questions to ask. She distinguishes transitional uses (a vis tool that "works itself out of a job" on the way to an automated solution) from long-term uses like exploratory scientific analysis and presentation. Computers matter because manual drawing doesn't scale past tiny static datasets; vision matters because it is a high-bandwidth, well-characterized channel with parallel preattentive processing (she explicitly argues sound and other senses are poor substitutes). Visual **external representations** let us offload internal cognition and memory to the perceptual system.

Two arguments in the middle are the philosophical core. First, showing the data in detail matters because summary statistics mislead — Anscombe's Quartet (Figure 1.3) is her canonical demonstration that four datasets with identical mean, variance, correlation, and regression line have wildly different visible structure. Second, any depiction of data is an abstraction involving choices about what to emphasize; "it's not just about making pretty pictures," and vis differs from art, movies, and advertising in that correctness and effectiveness — not beauty or emotional engagement — are the design goals.

The design-oriented sections argue that the space of vis **idioms** (distinct approaches combining **visual encoding** and **interaction**) is enormous, that most points in that design space are ineffective for any specific usage context, and that the sensible goal is to **satisfy** rather than **optimize** — illustrated by a search-space metaphor (Figure 1.5) of nested known/consideration/proposal spaces, with the moral that you should deliberately consider multiple alternatives rather than fixating on your first idea. **Validation** is hard because "better" and "effective" hide many operational questions (previewing chapter 4). Designers face three resource limitations — computational capacity, human perceptual/cognitive capacity (memory, attention, **change blindness**), and display capacity (**information density**, with the tree layouts of Figure 1.6 as the example) — making **scalability** a central concern. The chapter closes with the payoff (1.14): analyzing existing systems is a stepping stone to designing new ones, via the **what–why–how** framework in which each *data–task–idiom* trio is an analysis **instance**, and complex usage is a chained sequence of instances where one instance's **output** becomes the next one's **input** (Figures 1.7, 1.8).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The definition itself, and the discipline of unpacking it: vis is computer-based, visual, dataset-driven, and — crucially — judged by whether it helps people do *tasks* more effectively.
- Vis is for augmenting humans on ill-specified problems; if the question is fully specified, automate instead. Knowing when *not* to use vis is part of the skill.
- Anscombe's Quartet as the standard argument for "show the data": summaries hide structure that pictures reveal.
- Effectiveness, not beauty, is the yardstick — and *any* picture of data is an abstraction that emphasizes some things at the expense of others.
- Design means searching a huge space where most options are bad: generate multiple alternatives, satisfice, don't fixate.
- The what/why/how (data/task/idiom) framing — this is the skeleton the whole book (and the course's building-blocks approach) hangs on.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read 1.1 (the big picture), 1.6 (Anscombe), 1.10–1.11 (effectiveness and why most designs fail), and 1.14 (the what–why–how framework) carefully — 1.14 especially, since chapters 2 and 3 assume it. Sections 1.2–1.5 (human in loop, computer in loop, external representation, vision) can be skimmed once you buy their conclusions; 1.7–1.9 are short and quick; 1.12–1.13 (validation, resource limits) preview later chapters and can be skimmed on first pass. Skip 1.15 (Further Reading) unless chasing sources.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The boxed definition: "Computer-based visualization systems provide visual representations of datasets designed to help people carry out tasks more effectively."
- "Vis is suitable when there is a need to augment human capabilities rather than replace people with computational decision-making methods."
- Figure 1.3: Anscombe's Quartet — identical statistics, four very different scatterplots.
- Figure 1.5: the search-space metaphor (known / consideration / proposal / selected), with "Good!" vs. "Bad!" strategies; **satisfy** vs. **optimize**.
- Figure 1.6: three encodings of the same tree at different information densities.
- Figures 1.7–1.8: the what/why/how stack and chained analysis instances.
- "Why doesn't dictate how" is set up here (means vs. ends, the sorting example in 1.14).

**Caveats:** The framing of machine learning as something vis helps you transition toward or audit (the stock-trading and "is the ML system trustworthy" examples) reads as prescient but dated in its examples — the human-vs-automation boundary has moved a lot since 2014. The dismissal of sonification and other modalities reflects the state of ~2014 research and her deliberate scoping, not a settled verdict. The what–why–how framework is *her* scaffold — she explicitly calls it "a guide to get you started, not a straitjacket" — so don't mistake it for the only decomposition of vis. Students sometimes misread ch. 1 as saying interaction is always required or that presentation vis is second-class; she says neither.

## Munzner, "What: Data Abstraction" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 2)

**What it is:** The "what" of the what–why–how framework: a compact vocabulary for describing data in visualization-relevant, domain-independent terms — data types, dataset types, attribute types, and semantics.

**AI summary:** The chapter's motivating move (2.2) is that raw values are meaningless without two crosscutting pieces of information: **semantics** (real-world meaning) and **type** (structural or mathematical interpretation). Her opening examples — the number sequence "14, 2.6, 30, 30, 15, 100001" and the line "Basil, 7, S, Pear," each given several plausible interpretations — dramatize that you can't design an encoding until you know what the data *is*. Extra interpretive information is **metadata**, though she declines to draw a hard line and calls everything *data*. The vocabulary she then builds is the **data abstraction**: five basic **data types** (2.3) — **items**, **attributes**, **links**, **positions**, and **grids** — which combine into four basic **dataset types** (2.4): **tables** (items × attributes; **flat** or **multidimensional**), **networks** (nodes and links, with **trees** as the acyclic special case), **fields** (attributes sampled from a **continuous** domain on a grid, requiring care about **sampling** and **interpolation**), and **geometry** (shape/position only, often with no attributes at all). Other groupings — **sets**, **lists**, **clusters**, plus **paths** and **compound networks** — round out the taxonomy. A historically important aside: the spatial-field vs. nonspatial-table split is exactly the old **scientific visualization** (space is *given*) vs. **information visualization** (space is *chosen*) divide. Dataset availability (2.4.6) crosscuts everything: **static files** vs. **dynamic streams**.

Attribute types (2.5) get the chapter's most-used distinction: **categorical** (no implicit ordering — fruit, movie genres) vs. **ordered**, with ordered split into **ordinal** (ordered but no arithmetic — shirt sizes, rankings) and **quantitative** (magnitudes supporting arithmetic — height, price). Ordering direction can be **sequential**, **diverging** (two sequences meeting at a zero point — her example: a full elevation dataset with sea level as zero), or **cyclic** (hours, weekdays, months). Attributes may also have hierarchical structure (time aggregating from days to weeks to years).

Section 2.6 argues that type and semantics are crosscutting — one doesn't dictate the other — and focuses on the semantics she needs later: **key** vs. **value** attributes. A key acts as an index for looking up values (statisticians' independent vs. dependent variables). Flat tables have at most one key (and Table 2.1's duplicate "Amy" shows why *Name* fails as a key); **multidimensional tables** need several keys jointly. In fields, spatial position acts as a quantitative key, and fields are classified by multivariate structure into **scalar**, **vector**, and **tensor** fields. Finally, temporal semantics: a dataset is **time-varying** when time is a key attribute rather than a value, **time-series** data is the ordered time–value special case of tables, and she carefully disambiguates two meanings of "dynamic" (time-varying semantics vs. stream availability).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The core reflex: before designing anything, translate your data into abstract terms — what are the items, the attributes, the structure? Domain jargon out, generic vocabulary in.
- The 4 dataset types (tables, networks, fields, geometry) built from 5 data types (items, attributes, links, positions, grids) — Figure 2.1 is the one-page cheat sheet.
- The attribute-type ladder — categorical / ordinal / quantitative — is the single most load-bearing distinction in the course: it drives which visual channels are appropriate (that's chapter 5's payoff).
- Semantics and type are independent: knowing a column holds numbers tells you nothing about whether it's a quantity or a code (postal codes don't add).
- Key vs. value semantics: which attributes index the data and which are measured — the distinction underlying tables, fields, and (as she notes) Tableau's dimensions/measures.
- Sequential vs. diverging vs. cyclic ordering — small vocabulary now, big payoff for colormap and time-encoding choices later.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read 2.1–2.5 carefully; they're short and every term recurs all semester (2.5, attribute types, is the most important handful of pages in the book for this course). Within 2.4, the fields material (2.4.3, especially grid types in 2.4.3.2) can be skimmed unless you work with spatial/scientific data. In 2.6, read 2.6.1 (key vs. value, flat vs. multidimensional tables) carefully; skim 2.6.1.3–2.6.1.7 (scalar/vector/tensor field taxonomy) and read 2.6.2 (temporal semantics) at moderate depth for the time-varying vs. time-series vs. stream distinctions. Skip 2.7.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The "Basil, 7, S, Pear" example — is it a produce shipment, snow removal, or a lab rat in a maze? — as the argument that data is meaningless without semantics and types.
- Figure 2.1: the full "What?" taxonomy diagram (reused throughout the book as a navigation device).
- Table 2.1 (the Amy/Basil/Clara table) and Figures 2.5/2.9: the orders spreadsheet annotated with item/attribute/cell and colored by attribute type — none of its columns is a valid key, even Order ID.
- Definitions worth quoting: an **attribute** is "some specific property that can be measured, observed, or logged"; a **key** "acts as an index that is used to look up value attributes."
- The elevation example for diverging data: mountain height is sequential, bathymetry is sequential, full elevation is diverging around sea level.
- The postal-code example: a number whose type is a *code*, not a quantity — "adding two of these numbers together does not make sense."

**Caveats:** The big one for CS765: Munzner deliberately *skips* the classic levels-of-measurement story. NOIR's nominal appears only as a margin synonym for categorical, and interval vs. ratio is relegated to footnote 3, where she collapses them because the distinction "is typically not useful when designing a visual encoding" (citing Stevens 1946). The course supplements this separately, so don't be confused when other readings use four levels where Munzner uses three types. Her terminology is idiosyncratic in places she herself flags: *dimension* is reserved for spatial position channels (others use it for any attribute), *field* is used loosely relative to its mathematical meaning, and "dynamic" is disambiguated into two meanings (time-varying vs. streaming) that much of the literature conflates. The scivis/infovis split she describes as historical is even more blurred today. Finally, note the taxonomy is a design aid, not an ontology — real datasets are "complex and hybrid combinations," and she says translating into these terms is a goal, not a guarantee.

## Munzner, "Why: Task Abstraction" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 3)

**What it is:** The "why" of the framework: a controlled vocabulary of *actions* (verbs) and *targets* (nouns) for describing what users are trying to do with a vis, abstracted away from domain language — plus worked examples of full what–why–how analyses.

**AI summary:** The central claim (3.2) is that transforming task descriptions from domain-specific language into abstract form is what makes reasoning about vis design possible: otherwise "everything just appears to be different." Her showcase example pairs an epidemiologist ("contrast the prognosis of patients intubated in the ICU...") with a biologist ("see if the tissue samples treated with LL-37 match up with the ones without...") and shows both are instances of the same abstract task: "compare values between two groups." The framework supplies verbs (**actions**) and nouns (**targets**); complex activities become chained sequences of tasks where one task's output is the next's input. She adds a *who* dimension (3.3): the designer vs. the end user, with tools on a specific-to-general continuum. A theme she repeats emphatically: *why doesn't dictate how* — the goal of a vis is separable from the idiom design that achieves it.

Actions (3.4) come at three independent levels. At the high level, **analyze** splits into **consume** — with three cases: **discover** new knowledge (to **generate** or **verify** hypotheses), **present** something already understood to an audience, and **enjoy** (casual encounters, exemplified by Name Voyager, Figure 3.3, whose users weren't its intended parents at all) — and **produce**, with three goals: **annotate**, **record** (e.g., Tableau's graphical history, Figure 3.4), and **derive**. Derive (3.4.2.3) gets the most airtime and is the chapter's most design-relevant idea: "Don't just draw what you're given; decide what the right thing to show is, create it with a series of transformations from the original dataset, and draw that!" Her examples: temperature re-typed as hot/warm/cold or burned/not-burned depending on task; trade balance = exports − imports (Figure 3.5), where encoding the **derived attribute** directly beats making the eye judge differences between two curves. At the mid level, **search** splits four ways by whether target identity and location are known: **lookup** (both known), **locate** (target known, location not), **browse** (location known, target not), **explore** (neither) — illustrated by finding humans vs. rabbits in a tree of mammal species. At the low level, **query** has three scopes: **identify** one target, **compare** some, **summarize** all (synonym: **overview**), with a choropleth election map as the running example.

Targets (3.5) are the nouns: for all data, **trends**, **outliers**, and **features**; for one attribute, individual values, **extremes**, and the **distribution**; for many attributes, **dependency**, **correlation**, and **similarity**; for network data, **topology** and **paths**; for spatial data, **shape**. Section 3.6 is a one-page preview of the *how* half of the book (encode/manipulate/facet/reduce, Figure 3.7). Section 3.7 then makes the framework concrete with three worked analyses: SpaceTree vs. TreeJuxtaposer as two different *hows* for the same *what* and *why* (Figures 3.8–3.9); Strahler numbers as a derived attribute that lets you draw only the top 5000 of half a million nodes and still see a recognizable tree skeleton (Figures 3.10–3.11); and a fluid-dynamics system where features invisible in physical space pop out in linked plots of derived variable spaces (Figures 3.12–3.13).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The abstraction move itself: strip domain vocabulary until tasks from different fields become comparable — "contrast" and "match up" are both *compare*. This is the skill the course wants you to practice, more than memorizing the taxonomy.
- Actions come in three independent levels — analyze (consume/produce), search (lookup/locate/browse/explore), query (identify/compare/summarize) — and the search 2×2 (target known? location known?) is the most reusable piece.
- *Why doesn't dictate how*: presentation doesn't imply static, discovery doesn't imply a particular idiom. Keep goals and design choices in separate mental boxes.
- Derive is a first-class design decision: the data abstraction you visualize is chosen, not given. Transforming data is often the highest-leverage design move (trade balance, Strahler numbers).
- Targets vocabulary (trends, outliers, distribution, correlation, topology, shape...) gives you nouns for stating precisely what a chart should reveal.
- Real analyses are chained sequences of what–why–how instances; Figures 3.11 and 3.13 show what a complete written-out analysis looks like — the template for course assignments.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read 3.1–3.2 carefully (the motivation is the point of the chapter), 3.4.2.3 (derive) carefully, and 3.4.3–3.5 (search, query, targets) carefully but quickly — they're definitional and Figure 3.1 compresses all of them. Sections 3.4.1's subsections (discover/present/enjoy) can be read at moderate depth; 3.3 (who) is short and skimmable. Section 3.6 is a preview of the rest of the book — skim it. In 3.7, read 3.7.1 and 3.7.2 (SpaceTree/TreeJuxtaposer and Strahler) as worked examples; 3.7.3 (fluid dynamics) is skimmable unless the derived-spaces idea intrigues you. Skip 3.8.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The epidemiologist/biologist pair reducing to "compare values between two groups" — the canonical demonstration of why abstraction pays.
- "Don't just draw what you're given; decide what the right thing to show is, create it with a series of transformations from the original dataset, and draw that!" (3.4.2.3)
- The recurring refrain "why doesn't dictate how" (stated in 3.4.1.1 and echoed in 3.4.1.2).
- Figure 3.1: the full "Why?" taxonomy (actions + targets); Figure 3.2: the three action levels; Figure 3.6: targets.
- The search 2×2: lookup / locate / browse / explore — with the mammal-tree example (humans are easy to look up; rabbits you must locate — they're lagomorphs, not rodents).
- Figure 3.5: trade balance as a derived attribute; the shower-temperature example (quantitative → hot/warm/cold → burned/not-burned) for task-dependent re-typing.
- Figure 3.10: Strahler-number filtering — 5000 of 500,000+ nodes still shows the tree's skeleton.
- Name Voyager (Figure 3.3) as the *enjoy* example, and as a warning that designers' intended goals and users' actual goals can diverge.

**Caveats:** The taxonomy's terms are hers, chosen from many competing task classifications (the Further Reading cites 30 prior classifications via Brehmer & Munzner 2013, the paper this chapter is based on); other vis literature says "explain vs. explore" where she says present vs. discover, and "find" where she says search — she flags these herself in margin notes. Students commonly misread the levels as a pipeline; they're *independent* choices you can specify at all three levels, and the search categories in particular get misapplied when people forget both axes (identity *and* location). The distinctions can feel fuzzy at the boundaries (browse vs. explore, feature vs. trend — "features" is explicitly task-dependent); the framework is meant as a lens for making design reasoning explicit, not a decision procedure that outputs the right chart. The tool examples (SpaceTree, TreeJuxtaposer, Name Voyager, Tableau's 2008-era history feature) are dated as software, but they're doing rhetorical work as analyses, and that use holds up.

## Munzner, "Analysis: Four Levels for Validation" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 4)

**What it is:** Introduces the **nested model** of visualization design — four cascading levels (domain situation, task/data abstraction, visual encoding/interaction idiom, algorithm) — and shows how each level has a distinct **threat to validity**, which dictates the appropriate validation method.

**AI summary:** Munzner splits vis design into four nested levels, shown as concentric boxes in **Figure 4.2**: at the top, the **domain situation** (a specific group of target users, their domain, questions, and data); next, the **task and data abstraction** level, which maps domain-specific questions and datasets into domain-independent vocabulary (abstract tasks like browsing, comparing, summarizing; abstract data types like tables, networks, fields); then the **visual encoding and interaction idiom** level, which designs the specific picture (encoding) and the way users manipulate it (interaction); and finally the **algorithm** level, the computational procedure that instantiates the idiom efficiently. The levels are **nested**: output from an **upstream** level is input to the **downstream** level, and a bad choice at an upstream level (e.g., domain situation) cascades and cannot be fixed by even perfect downstream work. This is why she distinguishes situation/task blocks as *identified* (discovered through user research) from data/idiom/algorithm blocks as *designed* (created through creative decisions) — the whole process is iterative, "design as redesign," not strictly linear top-down.

Each level has a specific **threat to validity** (Figure 4.4): **wrong problem** (you misunderstood their needs), **wrong abstraction** (you're showing them the wrong thing), **wrong idiom** (the way you show it doesn't work), and **wrong algorithm** (your code is too slow). Different threats require different validation approaches (Figure 4.5), and she distinguishes **immediate** validation (doesn't require implementation — e.g., interviews, complexity analysis, heuristic justification against known perceptual principles) from **downstream** validation (requires building/implementing the lower levels first — e.g., lab studies, field studies, benchmark timing, adoption rates). Domain validation uses **field study** / **contextual inquiry** (observing users in real settings) plus downstream adoption-rate tracking. Abstraction validation requires target users trying the tool themselves on their own work (not a task specified by the designer) and can escalate to field studies documenting behavior change after deployment. Idiom validation combines immediate justification against perceptual/cognitive principles, heuristic evaluation, and expert review with downstream **lab study** (controlled experiment measuring time/errors on abstracted tasks), qualitative/quantitative result-image analysis (**quality metrics**), and informal usability studies (explicitly *not* counted as validation — they precede it). Algorithm validation uses computational complexity analysis (immediate) and wall-clock time/memory benchmarking (downstream), plus correctness checking.

She flags **mismatches** as a common failure mode in weak vis papers: validating a claim at the wrong level (e.g., defending a new encoding idiom with algorithm timings, or defending an abstraction claim with a controlled lab study whose task was dictated by the experimenters rather than discovered from users). No single paper can rigorously validate all four levels — contributions typically target one or two levels, and good papers make clear which. The chapter closes with five worked **validation examples** (genealogical graphs, MatrixExplorer, flow maps, LiveRAC, LinLog, Sizing the Horizon), each annotated with a small "levels validated" diagram, showing how real published systems distribute their validation effort unevenly across the four levels.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The nested model gives you a vocabulary for locating *where* a design claim lives and *what kind of evidence* would actually support it — this is the single most reusable idea in the book.
- A "great idiom" cannot rescue a "wrong abstraction," and vice versa — validity is not fungible across levels; each level must be checked on its own terms.
- Threats cascade downstream but validation evidence generally must come from downstream (implemented) results — so validation is inherently asymmetric and can't all happen up front.
- Distinguish *identified* blocks (situation, task) from *designed* blocks (data abstraction, idiom, algorithm) — this maps directly to when you're doing user research vs. making creative design decisions.
- Watch for the "mismatch" failure pattern in papers (and in your own project reports): citing the wrong kind of evidence for the claim being made is one of the most common critique points.
- Problem-driven (top-down, domain-first) vs. technique-driven (bottom-up, idiom/algorithm-first) are both legitimate angles of attack, but each has a characteristic failure mode (skipping the abstraction step vs. never articulating assumptions about upstream fit).

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: 4.3 (Four Levels of Design) and 4.5–4.6 (Threats to Validity, Validation Approaches) — these carry the core vocabulary and are Module 4 required content. 4.4 (Angles of Attack) is worth a careful read too since it frames problem- vs. technique-driven work, relevant for project framing. Section 4.7 (Validation Examples) can be skimmed — read the framing paragraph for each example and look at its "levels validated" figure; the paper-by-paper prose is useful mainly as a worked template for how to write validation reasoning about your own project, not for the specific historical systems. 4.8 (Further Reading) is a bibliography — skip unless chasing citations.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The four threats: "Wrong problem: You misunderstood their needs." / "Wrong abstraction: You're showing them the wrong thing." / "Wrong idiom: The way you show it doesn't work." / "Wrong algorithm: Your code is too slow." (Figure 4.4)
- On the danger of implicit abstraction: the "lost in hyperspace" web-navigation example — many early web-vis papers implicitly assumed users needed a visual representation of the full hyperlink topology; in fact users don't need an internal mental model of that structure, so no matter how clever the encoding, the tools added cognitive load rather than reducing it.
- Andrews quoted on why usability studies aren't validation: "Formative methods [including usability studies] lead to better and more usable systems, but neither offer validation of an approach nor provide evidence of the superiority of an approach for a particular context."
- She distinguishes **validation** from **verification**, borrowing "threats to validity" from software engineering/simulation: validation asks whether you built the right product; verification asks whether you built the product right.
- "Design as redesign" [Green 89] — the four levels are iteratively refined, not designed strictly top-down.
- The genealogical-graphs case study's insight that the term "family tree" is misleading because the true data type is a general graph with structural constraints, not a tree — a nice concrete abstraction-level example.

**Figures worth borrowing:**
- **Figure 4.1/4.2** — the canonical nested-boxes diagram of the four levels (domain situation / abstraction / idiom / algorithm). This is *the* diagram for teaching the nested model; ch. 4's opening figure is essentially the icon for the whole course's framing.
- **Figure 4.4** — the four threats mapped onto the nested boxes ("wrong problem" / "wrong abstraction" / "wrong idiom" / "wrong algorithm"). Extremely quotable, good as a standalone teaching slide.
- **Figure 4.5** — threats and validation approaches at each level, with the "downstream dependency" red arrows showing that outer-level validation requires inner levels to be built first. Useful for teaching why validation is hard/asymmetric.
- **Figure 4.14** (LinLog vs. Fructerman-Reingold node-link clustering) — a strong, still-relevant comparative example showing how an encoding/algorithm choice (edge-length energy model) visibly reveals or hides cluster structure; good "before/after" pedagogical pair distinct from screenshots of old GUI chrome.
- The small per-example "levels validated" diagrams (Figures 4.7, 4.9, 4.11, 4.13, 4.15, 4.17) as a set — useful as a template for asking students to produce the same diagram for their own project's validation plan.

**Caveats:** The five validation examples are all older systems (2003–2009) with dated screenshots (MatrixExplorer, LiveRAC, flow maps) — useful as validation-reasoning templates but not as visual/interaction exemplars; don't reproduce the screenshots as "what good vis looks like today." The nested model is idiosyncratic to Munzner's framework (though widely adopted in the vis community) — students should know "domain situation / abstraction / idiom / algorithm" is her terminology, not a universal standard, though it's become close to one in HCI/vis pedagogy. The chapter's claim that "any individual research paper would use only a small subset of these validation methods" is worth flagging explicitly to students who may otherwise expect every project (or paper they read) to validate all four levels. Also note the deliberately narrow use of "user study" (she uses it broadly for both lab and field studies, flagging that the term is used ambiguously in the literature) — worth clarifying since students will encounter both usages when reading papers.

## Munzner, "Marks and Channels" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 5)

**What it is:** The core theory chapter of Munzner's book: it defines the atomic building blocks of visual encoding — marks and channels — and gives principled rules (with perceptual evidence) for choosing which channel should carry which data attribute.

**AI summary:** Munzner's opening claim is that any visual encoding, however complex, can be decomposed into an orthogonal combination of **marks** — basic geometric elements (points/0D, lines/1D, areas/2D) that depict items or links — and **visual channels** that control the marks' appearance: position, color (hue, saturation, luminance), size (length, area, volume), tilt/angle, shape, motion, and more. Marks can represent items, or links between items via **connection** (line) and **containment** (nesting) marks. A key structural distinction: channels split into two families matching two kinds of human perception. **Identity channels** (spatial region, hue, motion, shape) tell us *what* or *where* something is and suit **categorical** data; **magnitude channels** (position, length, angle, area, luminance, saturation, etc.) tell us *how much* and suit **ordered** data.

Two principles govern channel choice. The **expressiveness principle** "dictates that the visual encoding should express all of, and only, the information in the dataset attributes" — most fundamentally, ordered data should look ordered and unordered data should not (violating this is called "a common beginner's mistake"). The **effectiveness principle** dictates that the importance of the attribute should match the **salience** of the channel — encode the most important attributes with the most effective channels. These combine into the chapter's centerpiece, the ranked channel lists of Figure 5.6: for ordered data, position on a common scale beats unaligned position, then length, tilt/angle, area, 3D depth, luminance/saturation, curvature/volume; for categorical data, spatial region beats hue, then motion, then shape. Notably, spatial position tops *both* lists — and Munzner is explicit that the rankings are "my own synthesis" of prior frameworks and empirical studies (Bertin, Cleveland & McGill, Mackinlay, Heer & Bostock).

The second half unpacks what "effectiveness" actually means, via five criteria: **accuracy** (grounded in **psychophysics** — Stevens' power law S = Iⁿ, and Cleveland & McGill's graphical perception experiments, replicated via crowdsourcing by Heer & Bostock); **discriminability** (how many perceptible **bins** a channel offers — line width supports only three or four); **separability vs. integrality** (some channel pairs, like position+hue, can be read independently; others, like width+height or red+green, fuse perceptually); **popout** (preattentive detection of a distinct item in constant time regardless of distractor count — works for single channels, fails for most channel combinations); and **grouping** (containment > connection > proximity > similarity). The chapter closes with **Weber's Law** — perception is fundamentally *relative*, not absolute — which explains why aligned/framed bars are easier to compare, and why luminance and color perception are entirely contextual (**color constancy**).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The decomposition habit: stop thinking in chart types; analyze any visualization as marks + channels. This is the vocabulary the whole course builds on.
- The two-way match: identity channels ↔ categorical data, magnitude channels ↔ ordered data. Getting this backwards is the canonical expressiveness violation.
- The effectiveness rankings (Fig. 5.6) as a design default: put your most important attribute on spatial position, because position dominates the user's **mental model**.
- Rankings aren't magic — they rest on measurable things: accuracy experiments, bin counts, separability, popout. Knowing *why* a channel ranks where it does lets you reason about cases the table doesn't cover.
- Channels interact: you can't just stack encodings freely (integral pairs fuse; popout doesn't survive conjunctions of channels).
- Perception is relative (Weber's Law), which is the underlying reason alignment and common scales matter so much.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: 5.3–5.4 (definitions, expressiveness/effectiveness, the rankings) — this is the exam-grade core — plus 5.5.3 (separability) and 5.6 (relative judgements), which are the ideas students most often haven't seen before. Skim: 5.5.1's psychophysics details (get the gist of Stevens and Cleveland & McGill without memorizing exponents), 5.5.4–5.5.5 (popout and grouping — study the figures more than the prose). Skip or glance: 5.7 Further Reading (though it's a good pointer list — Ware for perception, Healey's popout demo page).

**Memorable specifics:**
- Figure 5.1/5.6 — the ranked channel chart; arguably the single most-reproduced figure in vis education.
- Figure 5.4 — the elegant progression bar chart → scatterplot → +hue → +size, showing one channel added per attribute.
- Figure 5.7 — Stevens' power law curves (length has exponent n = 1.0: perceived length matches true length; area is compressed, saturation magnified).
- Figure 5.8 — Cleveland & McGill's error rankings alongside Heer & Bostock's crowdsourced replication.
- Figure 5.11 — the red-circle-among-blue-circles popout demo (and its failure with mixed shapes and colors, forcing **serial search**).
- Figures 5.13–5.15 — Weber's Law bars, and the Adelson checker-shadow and colored-cube illusions for luminance/color relativity.
- Quotables: the expressiveness principle ("express all of, and only, the information...") and "The identity channels tell us... *what* something is or *where* it is... the magnitude channels tell us *how much*."

**Caveats:** The rankings are Munzner's synthesis, not a single experimental result — she says so herself — and they are per-attribute-type, not universal (hue is second-best for categorical data but dead last as a magnitude channel; students often flatten this into "color is bad"). "Effectiveness" here means accuracy-centric reading of single attributes; it doesn't capture memorability, engagement, or gist tasks. Separability/integrality is a continuum with open questions, not a lookup table. Sidebars note the terminology thicket (channel = retinal variable = visual attribute...; identity/magnitude = metathetic/prothetic in psychophysics) — worth flagging since other readings use other words. Gestalt principles are explicitly *not* covered. The 2014 text predates the last decade of graphical-perception research, but nothing central here has been overturned.

## Munzner, "Rules of Thumb" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 6)

**What it is:** A set of eight slogan-titled design guidelines — synthesized from empirical studies, prior literature, and Munzner's own experience — covering 3D use, dimensionality, memory/animation, immersion, overview/zoom/filter, latency, color, and the form-vs-function tradeoff.

**AI summary:** Munzner opens by stating these rules are "not set in stone" — a current synthesis of an incomplete, still-evolving research frontier (Figure 6.1 lists all eight as slogans). The longest rule, **No Unjustified 3D**, argues that 3D is easy to justify only when the user's task involves understanding inherently 3D shape or spatial structure; in all other contexts it needs careful justification because of several costs. She grounds this in perceptual mechanics: the **power of the plane** (the shared "planar" spatial-position channel for up-down/left-right is highly accurate, but arbitrary 3D depth position is not equivalent perceptually); the **disparity of depth** (depth judgment accuracy has power-law exponent n=0.67, worse than area's 0.7, versus n=1.0 for planar length); **line-of-sight ambiguity** (we get information about only one point per ray along the depth axis, versus millions of rays for the other two axes) and the aphorism that we "see in **2.05D**" (Colin Ware) rather than true 3D; **occlusion** (the most powerful depth cue, but it hides information, requiring costly interactive navigation and **motion parallax** to resolve, especially costly for unfamiliar abstract structures like a node-link graph in 3D); **perspective distortion/foreshortening** (destroys the power of the plane and the size channel — 3D bar charts are harder to read than 2D ones); other depth cues like familiar size, shadows/shading (add clutter, can be mistaken for marks, interfere with color channels), and stereoscopic depth (a comparatively weak cue, most useful for near objects, that doesn't fix perspective distortion); and **tilted text isn't legible** (off-plane text rendering is blocky/jaggy with current display tech). The chapter balances this with **benefits of 3D: shape perception** — when the task requires understanding 3D geometric structure (streamlines through a volume, fluid flow, molecular structure), 3D with interactive navigation lets users build mental models faster than assembling multiple 2D views, and experimental evidence supports 3D outperforming 2D specifically for shape tasks while 2D wins for precise relative-position judgments. Section 6.3.9 (Empirical Evidence) stresses the documented dissociation between stated preference for 3D and actual measured performance, and walks through several studies (Data Mountain, Tory et al.'s information-landscape comparison) where careful controls overturned earlier claims of 3D benefit — points-based displays beat landscape/terrain idioms for search and estimation tasks, and 2D landscapes beat 3D landscapes.

The remaining rules are shorter. **No Unjustified 2D** parallels the 3D argument one dimension down: 1D lists have higher information density and are better for lookup/alphabetical search than 2D node-link layouts unless the task truly requires seeing topological structure. **Eyes Beat Memory** argues that switching between simultaneously visible external views has far lower cognitive load than comparing against internal memory of a prior view; this motivates overview+detail idioms (e.g., a viewport rectangle in an overview pane) over idioms that rely on the viewer remembering earlier state. It's grounded in **working memory** limits and **cognitive load**, and covers **animation vs. side-by-side views** in detail: Munzner distinguishes narrative storytelling, single transitions, and video-style multiframe playback, arguing transitions are effective for helping people track a small number of changes (with strong evidence they beat jump cuts for maintaining context) but multiframe animations with many simultaneous changes overwhelm attention — side-by-side small multiples are typically better for detailed comparison across dozens (not hundreds) of frames. This section also covers **change blindness** — the striking finding that people fail to notice drastic changes (e.g., a person being swapped mid-conversation) when attention isn't directed at the changing region — as a rationale for why multiframe animation tracking is hard. **Resolution over Immersion** argues pixels are a scarcer, more valuable resource than presence/immersion for almost all vis tasks, since immersive tech (HMDs, stereo, 6-DOF tracking) trades away both display resolution and integration with the user's normal desktop workflow; immersion is only worth it when the abstraction is inherently 3D spatial data and presence itself matters. **Overview First, Zoom and Filter, Details on Demand** presents Shneiderman's classic mantra, framed via the what-why-how abstraction as an idiom aimed at the "summarize" task; overviews are created via **reduce** actions (filtering and/or aggregation), and the mantra applies best to moderate-size datasets — for huge datasets the alternative slogan is **Search, Show Context, Expand on Demand**. **Responsiveness Is Required** covers **latency** as categorical rather than continuous, citing Card et al.'s three time constants (Table 6.1: perceptual processing 0.1s, immediate response 1s, brief tasks 10s), then discusses visual feedback design, the interplay of interaction-mechanism/feedback/system-update latencies, and the cost/benefit tradeoff of interactivity itself (risk of degenerating into "human-powered search" if too much reliance is placed on the user checking every possibility). **Get It Right in Black and White** (Maureen Stone) is a color-encoding check: literally convert your image to grayscale to verify the most crucial information survives via the luminance channel alone. **Function First, Form Next** closes with Munzner's rationale for the book's functional focus — effective-but-ugly designs can be refined toward beauty in collaboration with visual designers, while beautiful-but-ineffective designs usually must be scrapped, so function should come first, though she's explicit this is not "form never."

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The 3D rule is the chapter's centerpiece and directly supports the 3D/scientific-vis lecture theme "we sense 2D, we infer 3D": the mechanics (power of the plane, line-of-sight ambiguity, "2.05D," occlusion, perspective distortion) explain *why* 3D for abstract data is usually a bad default, and *when* (shape-understanding tasks on inherently spatial data) it's actually justified.
- Depth cues are not interchangeable or additive — occlusion is powerful but costly, stereo is weak and short-range, shading/shadows add clutter and interfere with color channels; a student should be able to name which depth cue is doing the work in a given 3D visualization and what it costs.
- "Stated preference for 3D" versus "measured task performance" is a real, repeatedly documented dissociation — a caution against trusting user self-report or first impressions (Data Mountain history is the canonical cautionary tale).
- Eyes Beat Memory gives a perceptual justification for the ubiquitous overview+detail / linked-views idiom family, and for skepticism about animation-heavy interfaces with many simultaneous changes.
- Change blindness is a striking, memorable, easily-demoed phenomenon worth using in class to motivate why multiframe comparison tasks need side-by-side small multiples rather than pure animation.
- The latency time-constants (0.1s / 1s / 10s) are a concrete, actionable design target — worth internalizing as numbers, not just "make it fast."
- Function First, Form Next is a useful stance for grading/critiquing student projects: ineffective-but-pretty should lose to effective-but-plain.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Careful read: 6.3 (No Unjustified 3D) in full — including 6.3.1–6.3.9 — since it's the richest, most citation-dense, and most exam/discussion-worthy section, and directly supports the 3D/scientific-data lecture. 6.5 (Eyes Beat Memory, including 6.5.1–6.5.3 on memory/attention, animation, and change blindness) also merits careful reading — foundational for the deeper-perception module. 6.7 (Overview First...) and 6.8 (Responsiveness) are solid but can be skimmed for the slogans and Table 6.1 if time-limited. 6.4, 6.6, 6.9, 6.10 are short and can be skimmed quickly — each is essentially one paragraph-length idea worth remembering as a slogan even without deep study. 6.11 (Further Reading) is a bibliography — skip unless chasing citations.

**Memorable specifics:**
- The eight rule titles as slogans (Figure 6.1): No Unjustified 3D; No Unjustified 2D; Eyes Beat Memory; Resolution over Immersion; Overview First, Zoom and Filter, Detail on Demand; Responsiveness Is Required; Get It Right in Black and White; Function First, Form Next.

- "Given an effective but ugly design, it's possible to refine the form... In contrast, given a beautiful and ineffective design, you will probably need to toss it out and start from scratch" — Munzner's rationale for functional focus, paired explicitly with "I don't advocate 'form never.'"

**Caveats:** These are explicitly Munzner's own synthesis/opinion ("my current attempt... deeply incomplete"), not settled consensus — some rules rest on solid empirical citations (3D vs 2D shape/position tasks, change blindness, Card's latency constants) while others are more heuristic/argument-based (Function First Form Next, Resolution over Immersion) and should be presented to students as reasoned design advice rather than proven law. The 3D section leans on human-factors/VR literature that is now itself somewhat dated (Cockburn and McKenzie studies, Data Mountain, ~2000s); modern high-resolution VR/AR headsets have shifted the resolution-vs-immersion tradeoff somewhat since the book's 2014 writing, so "resolution over immersion" may need updating commentary for current hardware. The animation discussion (small multiples vs. animation) is a common point of contention in the vis research community (the Tversky et al. 2002 meta-review is influential but contested); worth flagging as an area of ongoing debate rather than closed consensus.

## Munzner, "Arrange Tables" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 7)

**What it is:** The chapter that spends the top-ranked channel — spatial position — on table data: a systematic tour of how choices about expressing, separating, ordering, and aligning in space generate the familiar statistical charts (bars, scatterplots, heatmaps, parallel coordinates, pies) as points in one design space.

**AI summary:** The **arrange** design choice "is the most crucial visual encoding choice because the use of space dominates the user's mental model of the dataset" — a direct payoff of chapter 5's channel rankings. Figure 7.1 lays out the design space: **express** values directly with spatial position; **separate, order, and align** regions; choose **axis orientation** (rectilinear, parallel, radial); and choose **layout density** (dense, space-filling). The organizing distinction for tables is **keys versus values**: a key is an independent attribute that indexes items; a value is a dependent cell attribute. Counting keys generates idioms: zero keys → scatterplots (two quantitative values expressed with both position channels); one key → bar/dot/line charts (list alignment); two keys → heatmaps (matrix alignment); many keys → recursive subdivision. Each idiom gets a compact what/why/how/scale analysis table.

The separate/order/align trio deserves attention: separation must be by a *categorical* attribute (expressiveness again — categorical data gets regions, not positions along an ordered axis), while ordering and alignment must use an *ordered* attribute. Small choices carry weight: ordering bars by the data value instead of alphabetically reveals patterns that a name-ordered chart hides (Fig. 7.4); in stacked bars only the bottom segment and total sit on a common scale; line charts must not be used with categorical keys, because connection marks imply trends — Zacks & Tversky's subjects read a gender line chart as "the more male a person is, the taller he/she is" (Fig. 7.9). A line chart's **aspect ratio** matters too: **banking to 45°** picks ratios that put informative slopes near the diagonal, where our angle discrimination is best (the sunspot data of Fig. 7.10 tells two different stories at ratios 4 and 22).

For axis orientation, Munzner contrasts rectilinear layouts (the default, highest precision) with **parallel** layouts — parallel coordinates, which trade the scatterplot's two-attribute limit for dozens of axes, showing correlation as parallel segments (positive) or a single crossing point (negative), at the cost of training time and pairwise-only patterns — and **radial** layouts, where the angle channel is both less accurately perceived and inherently cyclic. This grounds the famous pie-chart analysis: pies encode with angle/area judgements, bars with high-accuracy aligned position (Fig. 7.17); pies' one genuine virtue is showing part–whole relations, but a **normalized stacked bar chart** does that with a more accurate channel. Radial layouts are better only for genuinely periodic data (Fig. 7.19). Finally, **dense** layouts (e.g., the Tarantula software-coverage view, one pixel-line per line of code) and **space-filling** layouts trade legibility of individual items for information density — with the caveat that space-filling designs forfeit **white space**.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- Standard charts aren't a zoo of separate species: bar, scatter, line, heatmap, pie, SPLOM, and parallel coordinates are all systematic combinations of a few arrangement choices applied to keys and values.
- The key/value question ("how many keys does this table have?") is the first thing to ask when choosing an arrangement.
- Ordering regions is a free, high-leverage design choice: order by the data, not the alphabet, unless lookup-by-name is the task.
- Line charts assert trend relationships; use them only when the key is ordered. (The Zacks & Tversky result is the memorable evidence.)
- The pie chart argument in its honest form: it's about angle vs. aligned-position channel accuracy, and there's a legitimate part-to-whole use case with better alternatives.
- Every idiom has scalability limits (bars: dozens–hundreds of levels; scatterplots: hundreds of items; heatmaps: a million items but only 3–11 color bins; parallel coordinates: hundreds of lines) — "how big can this get?" is part of choosing an idiom.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read carefully: 7.1–7.3 (the design-space framing and keys/values), the opening of 7.5 (separate/order/align logic), the bar chart and line chart examples including Fig. 7.9 and aspect-ratio discussion, and 7.6.3's radial/pie analysis. Skim: the individual example blocks (stacked bars, streamgraphs, cluster heatmaps, SPLOMs) — the recurring what/why/how/scale tables are excellent skim targets that carry most of the content; likewise the parallel coordinates example if pressed for time (get the correlation-pattern idea from Fig. 7.13). Skip or glance: 7.5.3 (volumetric grids — one paragraph saying "don't"), 7.7 (dense/space-filling, unless the Tarantula case study appeals), 7.8 Further Reading.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Figure 7.1 — the four-part arrangement design space (express / separate-order-align / orientation / density).
- Figure 7.4 — the cat/wombat/capybara bar chart, alphabetical vs. value-ordered.
- Figure 7.9 — Zacks & Tversky's bar-vs-line charts of height by gender; the "more male, taller" misreading.

**Caveats:** Vocabulary is idiosyncratic in places: "key/value" is Munzner's database-flavored framing (elsewhere: independent/dependent variables), and her "dot chart" deliberately differs from Wilkinson's "dot plot" (she flags this in a sidebar). ## Munzner, "Arrange Spatial Data" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 8)

**What it is:** Covers the design space for visualizing data that already has inherent spatial semantics — geometry (including geographic data) and spatial fields (scalar, vector, tensor) — where the position channel is dictated by the data rather than freely chosen by the designer.

**AI summary:** Munzner frames this chapter as the exception to the general **arrange** framework (express/separate/order/align) developed elsewhere in the book: for spatial datasets, the right move is almost always to **use given** spatial position as the layout substrate rather than to encode other attributes with it, because the expressiveness principle says the most effective channel (spatial position) should carry the data's most important aspect — the spatial relationships themselves. Figure 8.1 lays out the taxonomy: **geometry** (shape conveyed directly, often **geographic** or otherwise derived) versus **spatial fields** (attributes attached to every cell in space), further split into **scalar fields** (one value per cell) and **vector/tensor fields** (many values per cell). For geometry, she discusses **cartographic generalization** — the cartography term she equates directly with her own "abstraction" concept — covering filtering, aggregation, and level-of-detail choices in map-making, and works through the **choropleth map** as a worked idiom example (area marks from given geometry, color from a sequential colormap encoding a quantitative attribute per region).

For scalar fields, three idiom families are introduced: **slicing** (showing a single 2D cross-section of a 3D field), **isocontours** (deriving lower-dimensional surface/line geometry — **isolines**/contour lines/isopleths in 2D, **isosurfaces** in 3D — at a chosen threshold value, then displaying them with standard graphics techniques), and **direct volume rendering** (computing an image directly from the full field without deriving intermediate geometry, using a **transfer function** that maps data value, and often also gradient magnitude, to color and opacity). Worked examples include topographic terrain maps (isolines layered on geographic geometry), the **flexible isosurfaces** idiom (using a **contour tree** — simplified from over a million edges down to under 100 — to help users navigate which isovalue reveals meaningful structure, since raw isosurfaces at many levels would occlude each other in 3D), and **multidimensional transfer functions** (the Simian system, which lets users interactively build 2D transfer functions using a derived space of data-value vs. gradient-magnitude to isolate material boundaries like air-tissue and tissue-bone in medical CT/MRI data).

Vector fields (velocity fields, commonly from computational fluid dynamics) are characterized by **critical points** — locations where velocity vanishes, classified as **sources**, **sinks**, and **saddle points**, each of which may or may not have circulation around it (Figure 8.7's five-type taxonomy: saddle, circulating sinks, circulating sources, noncirculating sinks, noncirculating sources). Four idiom families recur for both vector and tensor fields: **flow glyphs** (local per-cell objects, e.g., arrows encoding magnitude via length and direction via orientation), **geometric flow** (derived trajectories from a sparse set of seed points — **streamline** for steady fields, **pathline** for unsteady/time-varying fields, plus **streakline** and **timeline** variants, and their surface generalizations: stream/path/streak/time surfaces), **texture flow** (dense-seed approaches like **Line Integral Convolution**, or LIC, which smears white noise according to local flow), and **feature flow** (global computation to explicitly detect and encode structures of interest like critical points, vortices, and shock waves — also called **topological flow vis**). Laidlaw et al.'s empirical study comparing six 2D vector-field idioms found no single winner across all three tasks tested (finding/classifying critical points; predicting particle transport, i.e., **advection**), but the two local arrow-glyph idioms fared worst overall — a useful empirical corrective to the intuitive appeal of arrow glyphs. The similarity-clustered streamlines example shows how derived per-line attributes (curvature, torsion, tortuosity, combined into a "signature") can drive a cluster hierarchy used for interactive filtering and coloring of 3D streamline bundles. Tensor fields (e.g., diffusion tensor MRI, measuring properties like stress, conductivity, diffusivity) reuse the same four idiom families but require richer glyphs since a tensor at each cell encodes more information than a vector; **tensor glyphs** are illustrated with **ellipsoid glyphs**, which decompose the tensor into shape (isotropic/sphere, partially anisotropic/planar, or fully anisotropic/linear — demonstrated physically with ink diffusing through Kleenex versus newspaper) and orientation, though ellipsoids have the acknowledged weakness of ambiguity from a single viewpoint, motivating more sophisticated **superquadric tensor glyphs**.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The core principle — when data has inherent spatial semantics, use the given position rather than repurposing the position channel for something else — is the chapter's single load-bearing idea and connects directly to the "we sense 2D, we infer 3D" framing of the 3D/scientific-vis lecture (isosurfaces/volume rendering are literally about inferring 3D structure from a 2D display).
- Cartographic generalization is presented as literally the same concept as "abstraction" in the vis framework, applied to maps — a good bridge for students who already know GIS/cartography vocabulary.
- The scalar/vector/tensor field distinction, and correspondingly slicing/isocontour/volume-rendering (scalar) vs. glyph/geometric/texture/feature (vector & tensor) idiom families, gives a compact taxonomy for scientific visualization that generalizes across CFD, medical imaging, and geographic data.
- Critical-point vocabulary (source, sink, saddle, circulating vs. noncirculating) is the standard language for describing flow-field structure and worth knowing by name.
- The Laidlaw et al. empirical result — that intuitive arrow glyphs performed worst across vector-field tasks — is a good concrete instance of "designer intuition versus measured performance" that pairs well with ch. 6's 3D empirical-evidence discussion.
- Transfer functions (for volume rendering) and contour trees (for flexible isosurfaces) are examples of a recurring pattern: deriving an auxiliary abstract structure specifically to help users navigate a large family of possible views/thresholds — a technique-design idea that generalizes beyond scientific vis.

**Memorable specifics:**
- Choropleth map worked-example table format (What:Data / How:Encode) — a nice reusable template for teaching idiom analysis.

**Caveats:** This chapter is fairly dense with scientific-visualization-specific jargon (transfer functions, contour trees, advection, tortuosity/torsion/curvature signatures) that may be more technical detail than a general vis-literacy audience needs — for VisSnacks framing, the taxonomy (scalar/vector/tensor; geometric/texture/feature/glyph) and the 1-2 headline empirical results (Laidlaw's arrow-glyph finding) are probably the durable takeaways, while the deeper algorithmic material (marching cubes, LIC internals, tensor eigen-decomposition) is more appropriate as an aside/footnote than core content. Several example systems and figures are old (Kniss 02/05, Carr et al. 04, McGuffin-era tools, Laidlaw 05) — good for teaching the underlying idiom concepts but not representative of current rendering fidelity or interface design; avoid presenting the screenshots as "state of the art." The chapter is scoped to *given* spatial data only — it explicitly does not cover the (larger) topic of choosing spatial layouts for data without inherent spatial semantics (that's handled elsewhere in the book, e.g., dimensionality reduction, network layout), so instructors should be clear this chapter is the "space is given" half of the spatial-arrangement story, not the whole of it.

## Munzner, "Arrange Networks and Trees" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 9)

**What it is:** A survey of the design space for laying out network and tree data in space — node-link diagrams (connection marks), matrix views (derived tables), and containment-based layouts (treemaps, icicle plots) — with cost/benefit tradeoffs between the families.

**AI summary:** The chapter opens with the "Big Picture" (9.1) framing three families keyed to the containment/connection/spatial-position channels shown in Figure 9.1: **node–link diagrams** use connection marks (nodes as point marks, links as line marks); **matrix views** use area marks in 2D alignment to directly show adjacency; and **containment** marks (nesting) show hierarchical structure for tree data specifically. Section 9.2 works through node-link idioms in depth. Node-link layouts support tasks involving **topology** — tracing paths, finding neighbors, understanding direct/indirect connections — because the discrete **hops** (number of links traversed) between nodes is a meaningful network-oriented distance measure, unlike the continuous distance of the 2D plane. The extended example is **force-directed placement** (also called spring embedding, energy minimization, or nonlinear optimization): nodes repel each other while links act as springs pulling connected nodes together, with spatial position an indirect side effect of the physics simulation rather than a directly encoded attribute. Its strengths are simplicity of implementation and intuitive appeal; its weaknesses are being **nondeterministic** (unlike a scatterplot or bar chart, reruns look different, undermining spatial memory), prone to getting stuck in a **local minimum**, and — most importantly — poor scalability: layouts degenerate into an unreadable **hairball** once **link density** (links per node) exceeds roughly three or four, or once node count reaches a few hundred. Multilevel approaches like **sfdp** improve this by **coarsening** the network into a cluster hierarchy and laying out progressively more detailed versions, but even these hit hairball limits at tens of thousands of nodes/edges (Figure 9.5 shows this breakdown directly).

Section 9.3 covers the **adjacency matrix** view, where nodes are laid out along both axes of a square region and a link is shown by coloring the cell at the row/column intersection; this converts the network into a derived table. Undirected networks need only half the matrix (above or below the diagonal); directed ones need the full square. Section 9.4 is the payoff comparison: node-link views are intuitive with no training, excel at topology tasks (path tracing, neighborhoods), but collapse into occlusion past a link density of three-to-four. Matrix views eliminate occlusion entirely, scale to edge counts up to the square of the node count (single-level: up to ~1 million edges; multilevel: up to 10 billion), are stable and predictable in required screen space (supporting reordering, geometric/semantic zooming), and support fast node lookup — but they require training to read (most users don't intuitively recognize a **clique** as a block on the diagonal versus a criss-cross of lines) and are fundamentally weak at topological structure tasks like multi-hop path tracing, which an empirical study [Ghoniem et al. 05] confirmed remains hard in matrix views regardless of network size. Section 9.5 covers **containment** marks for trees: **treemaps** nest rectangles recursively, sized by an attribute, trading away topological/path clarity for excellent support of querying leaf-node attribute values (e.g., spotting the largest files by area). The chapter closes by noting hybrids exist (GrouseFlocks combines connection for the base network with containment for its derived cluster hierarchy) and that most real trees mix multiple channels (Figure 9.9 shows seven idioms for one tree dataset, several combining spatial position for depth/sibling-order with connection or containment for parent-child relationships).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:** 
- The three-way idiom split (node-link / matrix / containment) is not arbitrary aesthetics — each is optimized for different tasks, and choosing wrong costs real readability.
- The core "Big Lesson" instance here: node-link diagrams often let spatial position be an emergent side effect of a physics simulation rather than a deliberately chosen encoding — that's a specific, teachable case of "when position is flexible, use it wisely" going wrong (or at least, being used ambiguously).
- Link density (edges per node) is the single number to reason about when predicting whether a node-link layout will be readable; above ~4, expect a hairball regardless of how clever the algorithm is.
- Matrix views are the underused alternative for dense networks — they trade intuitive readability for perceptual scalability, and this tradeoff is worth explicitly teaching since most students only know node-link.
- "What is this network good for showing" maps directly onto task type: topology/path tasks favor node-link; density/clique/lookup tasks favor matrices; leaf-attribute-query tasks favor treemaps/containment.
- Force-directed placement's weaknesses (nondeterminism, local minima, brittleness to parameters) are concrete, named failure modes worth being able to name, not just vague "it doesn't scale."

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read 9.1, 9.2 (through Force-Directed Placement example and the multilevel/sfdp example), 9.3, and 9.4 carefully — these carry the course's core content (node-link vs matrix tradeoffs, force-directed mechanics, link density limit). Section 9.5 (containment/treemaps) can be skimmed for the definition and Figure 9.8/9.9 comparison; the GrouseFlocks compound-network example is a nice-to-have, not essential. 9.6 (Further Reading) is reference only — skip unless chasing a citation.

**Memorable specifics:**
- "The upper limit for node–link diagram effectiveness is a link density of around three or four [Melançon 06]."
- "Trees have a link density of one, with one edge for each node."
- Force-directed placement described as "also known as spring embedding, energy minimization, or nonlinear optimization."
- "Straightforward force-directed placement is unlikely to yield good results when the number of nodes is more than roughly four times the number of links" — i.e., L < 4N is the rule of thumb given in the idiom summary tables.
- Matrix views: "up to a limit of one thousand nodes and one million edges, just like cluster heatmaps and all other matrix views that use small area marks"; multilevel matrix views "might handle up to ten billion edges."
- The empirical finding: matrix views are better for most low-level tasks at large network sizes, but "the task of finding a multiple-link path between two nodes was always more difficult in matrix views, even with large network sizes."
- Idiom summary tables (Idiom / What: Data / How: Encode / Why: Tasks / Scale) recur for Force-Directed Placement, sfdp, Adjacency Matrix, Treemaps, GrouseFlocks — a reusable template for characterizing any visualization idiom.

**Figures worth borrowing:**
- Figure 9.1 (design choices overview: node-link/connection, adjacency matrix/derived table, enclosure/containment, each tagged for networks vs. trees) — a clean one-slide summary of the whole chapter's taxonomy, ideal as a course roadmap graphic.

**Caveats:** The chapter is somewhat dated on specific algorithm citations (sfdp, GEM, FM3, TopoLayout, GrouseFlocks are all mid-2000s/early-2010s systems); newer force-directed and multilevel approaches (and GPU-accelerated layouts) have pushed scale further since 2014. The "link density of 3-4" threshold and the "1000 nodes / 1 million edges" matrix limit are useful rules of thumb but are approximate, drawn from specific studies, and shouldn't be treated as hard physical laws. Munzner's terminology (compound network, multilevel network as near-synonyms) can be confusing on a first read — the chapter itself flags this ambiguity in a margin note. Also worth flagging for students: this chapter is purely about arrangement/layout, not about node/edge attribute encoding (color, size) beyond passing mentions — that's addressed elsewhere in the book (color in ch. 10, discussed for this course as chapter 10).

## Munzner, "Map Color and Other Channels" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 10)

**What it is:** A systematic treatment of color as a visual encoding channel — its perceptual basis, the taxonomy of colormaps (categorical, sequential, diverging, bivariate), why rainbow colormaps mislead — plus a shorter survey of other magnitude/identity channels (size, angle, curvature, shape, motion, texture).

**AI summary:** Munzner opens by decomposing the colloquial notion of "color" into three separable perceptual channels: **luminance** (the black-and-white channel, carrying high-resolution edge information), **hue**, and **saturation** (the amount of white mixed into a pure color). This split traces to the retina's **cones** (versus low-light **rods**) and the visual system's three **opponent color channels** — red-green, blue-yellow, and black-white/luminance — which explains **color deficiency** (informally "color blindness," affecting ~8% of men, most commonly along the red-green axis; blue-yellow **tritanopia** is rare and not sex-linked, unlike **deuteranopia** and **protanopia**). She reviews color spaces: **RGB** is computationally convenient but a poor perceptual match since its axes aren't separable channels; **HSL/HSV** (hue-saturation-lightness) is more intuitive for artists but only **pseudoperceptual** — its computed lightness L does not track true luminance, demonstrated starkly in Figure 10.3 where six hues at identical HSL lightness show very different true luminance. **L\*a\*b\*** is the perceptually uniform space designed so equal steps look equally different, suitable for interpolation and color-difference computation (unlike RGB or HSL). Crucially, luminance and saturation are **magnitude channels** (ordered, but with low discriminability — roughly 2-5 bins for luminance, ~3 for saturation, worse in small non-contiguous regions), while **hue** is an **identity channel** with no implicit perceptual ordering (Figure 10.5) — people reliably order gray between black and white, but not red/blue/green/yellow relative to each other, only via learned conventions like traffic-light order. Hue is "the highest ranked channel for categorical data after spatial position," with roughly 6-12 discriminable bins depending on region size/contiguity. A fourth channel, **transparency**, interacts so strongly with the others that it should essentially only be paired with hue, at very few (two) levels.

Section 10.3 builds the **colormap** taxonomy (Figure 10.6), mirroring the categorical/ordered data-type split: **categorical** (qualitative) colormaps use hue as identity to distinguish groups, needing careful attention to the 6-12 bin discriminability limit and to matching saturation to mark size (large regions need low-saturation "pastel" colors, small regions/lines need high saturation) — Figure 10.7's ColorBrewer comparison and Figure 10.8's mouse/human chromosome example make this concrete (21 categorical colors work fine as large contiguous regions but collapse to ~12 distinguishable bins when the same colors appear as scattered small regions). **Ordered** colormaps split into **sequential** (min-to-max progression, e.g., a hue ramping from pale to full saturation, or pure grayscale) and **diverging** (two hues at the endpoints meeting at a meaningful zero/neutral midpoint — white, gray, black, or yellow). This is where the chapter delivers its best-known argument: rainbow colormaps have three specific, named perceptual problems, all visible in Figure 10.12 — they use hue (an identity channel with no implicit order) to encode ordered data; they are **perceptually nonlinear** (equal data steps look like unequal visual steps at different points in the range); and they can't show fine detail because hue contrast doesn't support edge detection the way luminance does. The fix she recommends is **monotonically increasing luminance** colormaps that combine multiple hues (for nameable semantic categories/mid-level structure) with luminance that rises steadily (for accurate ordering and fine detail) — demonstrated in the geographic-elevation comparison of Figure 10.12. She notes segmented rainbows remain fine for categorical data with few categories, and that a perceptually-linearized rainbow is possible but sacrifices dynamic range/brightness (Figure 10.13). **Bivariate** colormaps encode two attributes at once; safe when one attribute is binary, unreliable when both have multiple levels. Section 10.3.4 covers **colorblind-safe design**: avoid encoding solely through hue on a red-green axis, add luminance/saturation redundancy, and test with a color-blindness simulator.

Section 10.4 surveys remaining channels more briskly. **Size** channels (length/1D, area/2D, volume/3D) show sharply declining perceptual accuracy in that order — length judgment is nearly as accurate as unaligned position, area is "significantly less accurate" (Stevens power-law exponent 0.7), and volume is "quite inaccurate," in an equivalence class with curvature at the bottom of the ranking; larger-dimensional size codings subsume smaller ones (can't use both length and area independently). The **angle** channel (also called **tilt** or **orientation**) has both sequential and cyclic character — a **tiltmap** can show sequential, diverging, or cyclic ordered data (Figure 10.14) — with perceptual accuracy that's very good near canonical horizontal/vertical/diagonal angles but degrades in between (89° vs 90° is easy; 37° vs 38° is not). **Curvature** is a low-accuracy channel usable only on line marks, in the same low tier as volume. **Shape** is treated as an identity channel for point/line marks (not usable on area marks, which are shape-constrained by definition); with enough pixels, dozens or hundreds of shapes are discriminable, but a small 10x10-pixel region limits this to roughly a dozen. **Motion** channels (direction, velocity, flicker frequency) are understudied but known to be extremely salient and strongly separable from static channels — their strength (impossible to ignore) is also their weakness (hard to selectively ignore), making motion best suited to transient highlighting rather than ongoing encoding. **Texture/stippling** decomposes into orientation, scale, and contrast (density, mapping to luminance), useful for either categorical (a dozen or two bins) or ordered (3-4 bins per subchannel) data.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- The three-way decomposition of "color" into luminance/hue/saturation is the single most important takeaway — most confusion about color in vis comes from treating "color" as one channel when it's really three with very different perceptual properties.
- Hue has no built-in perceptual order; luminance and saturation do. This is *the* explanation for why rainbow colormaps mislead, and it is a clean, teachable, falsifiable claim (test: ask someone to order red/green/blue/yellow — they can't agree; ask them to order five grays — they will).
- The rainbow colormap critique has three separable named problems (identity-channel misuse, perceptual nonlinearity, poor fine-detail/edge accuracy) — worth teaching as three distinct arguments, not one vague complaint.
- ColorBrewer operationalizes good colormap practice (categorical vs sequential vs diverging) and is a tool students should actually use, not just know about.
- Discriminability is not a fixed number — it depends on mark size and contiguity (Figure 10.8's mouse/human genome example is the clearest teaching case: the same 21-color categorical map works great as big contiguous blocks and fails as scattered small ones).
- Non-color channels (size, angle, shape, motion) have their own accuracy rankings and idiosyncrasies worth knowing when color isn't available or is already spoken for (e.g., size is good for ordered data but interacts badly with almost everything else; motion is best for highlighting, not steady-state encoding).
- Colorblind-safe design is a concrete, checkable design constraint (redundant luminance/saturation coding, simulator testing), not an afterthought.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read 10.1, 10.2 (all of Color Theory — vision, color spaces, luminance/saturation/hue), and 10.3 (Colormaps, especially 10.3.2 Ordered Colormaps and the rainbow critique, and 10.3.4 Colorblind-Safe Design) carefully — this is the heart of the color crash-course. Section 10.3.1 (Categorical Colormaps) is worth a careful read for the discriminability/region-size interaction but can move faster. Section 10.3.3 (Bivariate) can be skimmed — useful to know it exists and is risky, not essential in depth. Section 10.4 (Other Channels) can be skimmed section-by-section for the accuracy rankings and one key fact per channel (size: length>area>volume; angle: cyclic + accurate near canonical angles; shape: identity channel, point/line marks only; motion: salient but hard to selectively ignore) — students don't need the full texture/stippling subchannel breakdown in depth. 10.5 (Further Reading) is reference only.

**Memorable specifics:**
- "Luminance and saturation are magnitude channels... hue is an identity channel."
- "Unlike luminance and saturation, hue does not have an implicit perceptual ordering" — demonstrated in Figure 10.5.
- Rainbow colormaps: "hue is used to indicate order, despite being an identity channel without an implicit perceptual ordering... the scale is not perceptually linear... fine detail cannot be perceived with the hue channel; the luminance channel would be a much better choice, because luminance contrast is required for edge detection in the human eye."
- "We can tell 89° from 90°, 44° from 45°, and 1° from 0°; however, we cannot tell 37° from 38°" — angle channel accuracy.
- Size channel ranking: length judgments "extremely accurate," area "significantly less accurate" (Stevens exponent 0.7), volume "quite inaccurate," in an equivalence class with curvature at the bottom.
- "For small regions, designers should use bright, highly saturated colors... When colored regions are large... use low-saturation colors; that is, pastels."

**Figures worth borrowing:**
- Figure 10.1 (design-choice roadmap for color encoding/mapping and other channels: hue/saturation/luminance, categorical/ordered-sequential/diverging/bivariate colormaps, plus size/angle/curvature/shape/motion) — a single clean taxonomy diagram for the whole chapter, ideal as a lecture-opening roadmap.

**Caveats:** Munzner explicitly avoids the term "perceptually linear luminance" and "brightness" as technical terms, using luminance/hue/saturation in a hybrid, vis-specific way that doesn't map cleanly onto any single standard color-space vocabulary (she flags this herself in a margin note) — worth telling students this is her own analytical framework, not a universal standard. The chapter's treatment of texture and motion is admittedly thinner/less studied than color ("motion is less studied than the other visual channels"), so those sections read as more provisional. The rainbow-colormap critique, while well-supported and widely echoed since (Rogowitz & Treinish, Borland & Taylor), is itself now a somewhat dated-feeling fight in the sense that awareness has grown significantly across visualization tooling since 2014 (e.g., matplotlib's default long ago moved away from jet); still correct, just less of a live controversy than the text's tone implies. ## Munzner, "Manipulate View" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 11)

**What it is:** A design-space treatment of interaction as one of the core "how" categories in Munzner's framework — the three basic operations of change, select, and navigate — covering what a view can change about itself, how users pick items of interest, and how changing viewpoint (via zoom/pan/rotate, or dimension reduction via slice/cut/project) works.

**AI summary:** Section 11.1-11.2 frames the chapter: interactivity is "the most fundamental breakthrough of vis on a computer display compared with printed paper" because a view can dynamically respond to user input rather than being a fixed static encoding. Munzner identifies **change** as covering the full set of all other idiom design choices — encoding, arrangement, ordering, viewpoint, filtering, aggregation — with the same generality as any static design choice, just applied dynamically. Section 11.3, "Change View over Time," works through categories of change: changing the visual encoding entirely (Tableau's drag-and-drop switching between bar charts, stacked bars, and geographic maps, Figure 11.2), **reordering** (often called **sorting**) which "lies in the privileged status of spatial position as the highest ranked visual channel" — reordering makes sense for categorical attributes but not for already-ordered ones. The LineUp example shows reordering/realigning for comparing multiple weighted rankings, using **slope graphs** (also called **bump charts**) to show rank changes between columns, and small-multiple aligned bar charts. A large sub-theme is **animation**, particularly **animated transitions**: a series of frames smoothly bridging one state to another, contrasted with an abrupt **jump cut**. Munzner is notably measured about animation's appeal: "while animation has intuitive appeal to many beginning designers, it is valuable to think carefully about cognitive load and other trade-offs." The benefit of animated transitions is helping users maintain context by showing how an item moves to its new position, rather than forcing viewers to do item tracking themselves — most useful "when the amount of change is limited, because people cannot track everything that occurs if many items change in different ways all over the frame." An empirical study [Heer and Robertson 07] found carefully designed animated transitions do support better graphical perception of change.

Section 11.4, "Select Elements," covers the design choices around what can be a **selection** target (data items, links, attributes, attribute levels, or entire views), how many independent **selection types** are supported (simplest: binary selected/not; common: click vs. **hover** as two types), and how many elements the selection set can hold (exactly one, many, zero-acceptable, primary-vs-secondary distinctions). Selection is tightly coupled to **highlighting** — visually indicating what's selected — and Munzner stresses these are conceptually separate design choices even though often treated as synonyms. Highlighting approaches include color change (with the caveat that this temporarily hides existing color coding — "a fundamental limitation"), adding an outline (preserves existing color coding), size/linewidth increase, shape change (solid to dashed line), motion coding (oscillating selected points — an empirical study found motion coding "often outperformed more traditional highlighting approaches of coloring, outlining, and size changes" [Ware and Bobrow 04]), and drawing explicit connection marks between selected items across views (the **context-preserving visual links** example, which routes curves between items in multiple linked views balancing link length, occlusion, color contrast, and bundling). Selection outcomes matter too: a selection is often "a first step in a multistage sequence," feeding into a subsequent change (filter, aggregate, re-encode, or navigate to center the selected item).

Section 11.5, "Navigate: Changing Viewpoint," introduces the camera metaphor: navigation changes the point of view from which things are drawn, decomposed into **zooming** (moving the camera closer/farther), **panning** (translating the camera parallel to the image plane — a synonym in 2D is **scrolling**), and **rotating** (rare in 2D, important in 3D). Zooming in acts like filtering (fewer items in more detail); zooming out acts like aggregation (overview). **Geometric zooming** matches real-world intuition (an object simply gets bigger/smaller); **semantic zooming** instead adapts an object's representation to the number of available pixels — Munzner's example is a file icon that shows just a filename when small, a title when medium, and a multiline summary when large; the LiveRAC system (Figure 11.7) demonstrates semantic zooming across a stretchable grid of time-series charts, switching between color-only, sparklines, and full line charts as box size changes. Section 11.5.3 contrasts **unconstrained navigation** (camera can go anywhere; conceptually clean but users struggle with 3D's six degrees of freedom, risk of camera-inside-object collisions, or ending up pointed at nothing) with **constrained navigation** (limits on camera motion, e.g. zoom-range limits, or automatic animated-transition trajectories to frame a selected item) — constrained navigation is "particularly powerful when combined with linked navigation between multiple views."

Section 11.6, "Navigate: Reducing Attributes," extends the camera metaphor to dimensionality reduction, with three idioms: **slice** (choose one value along a dimension, extract only matching items — e.g., picking a height to view a 2D cross-section of a 3D head scan; generalizes to a **hyperplane** in higher dimensions, as in the HyperSlice system's matrix of orthogonal 2D slices for many-variable abstract data), **cut** (position a plane dividing the volume in two, discard everything on the near side — a **clipping plane** in graphics terms, showing 3D context behind the cut unlike a slice), and **project** (show all items but drop specific dimensions — **orthographic projection** discards excluded dimensions entirely, as in architectural floor/side/front plans, a synonym for **dimensional filtering** with abstract data; **perspective projection** retains partial depth information via foreshortening; **map projections** are a cartographic special case transforming curved-to-flat space).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- Interaction decomposes cleanly into three verbs — change, select, navigate — and nearly every interactive vis technique is one of these or a combination; this is a useful checklist for critiquing or designing an interactive system.
- The "why not interaction" cost theme is directly supported here: animation/animated transitions have real costs (cognitive load, limits on how much simultaneous change people can track) that Munzner flags explicitly, not just benefits — a good counterpoint to naive "just animate it" instincts.
- Selection and highlighting are two separable design decisions, not one — worth teaching as a pair of independent questions ("what can be selected, and how many/which kinds" vs. "how is the selection shown").
- Highlighting by color change has a specific, nameable cost: it hides the existing color encoding. Outline, size, shape, and motion are alternatives that preserve it — a concrete design-choice tradeoff, not just a list of options.
- Zooming has two very different flavors — geometric (same representation, just bigger/smaller) vs. semantic (representation itself changes with available pixels) — and semantic zooming is the more powerful, less obvious of the two.
- Constrained vs. unconstrained navigation is a direct, teachable tradeoff between expressive freedom and usability; most real systems blend them (constrained shortcuts, unconstrained fallback).
- Slice/cut/project map an intuitive 3D-camera vocabulary onto general dimensionality reduction, including for abstract (non-spatial) high-dimensional data — a nice bridge from spatial/scientific vis intuitions to abstract data problems.

**Skim/skip guidance:** Read 11.1, 11.2, 11.3 (especially the Animated Transitions example and its cognitive-load caveats), 11.4 (Selection Design Choices and Highlighting), and 11.5 (Navigate: Changing Viewpoint, especially geometric vs. semantic zooming and constrained vs. unconstrained navigation) carefully — these map directly onto the course's interaction lecture themes (change/select/navigate, why-not-interaction costs). Section 11.6 (Slice/Cut/Project) can be skimmed — useful vocabulary and good figures, but more relevant to scientific/spatial visualization than the typical abstract-data course thread; know the three terms and one example each rather than the full HyperSlice mechanics. 11.7 (Further Reading) is reference only.

## Munzner, "Facet into Multiple Views" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 12)

**What it is:** A design-choice taxonomy for splitting information across multiple views or layers — juxtaposing views side by side, superimposing views as layers, and partitioning data into regions — one of Munzner's five approaches to handling visual complexity (the others being derive, change, reduce, embed).

**AI summary:** Munzner opens by naming **facet** (the verb "to split") as covering two structurally different design choices: juxtaposing multiple views side by side, and superimposing views as layers within a single view. Both require partitioning the data into the views or layers. Juxtaposed views trade off against a *changing* single view (covered in ch. 11): simultaneously visible views are easy to compare by eye movement, while a changing view forces the viewer to rely on working memory — a scarce internal resource — invoking the slogan **Eyes Beat Memory**. Juxtaposition costs display area (a scarce external resource); superimposing costs visual clutter instead, since it doesn't need more screen space but has strict limits on distinguishable layers (roughly two is easy, three possible with care, more is very difficult), whereas juxtaposition scales to several dozen views with care.

For juxtaposed and coordinated views (**linked views**, synonymous with multiple views, coordinated views, coupled views), there are three major independent design axes: how much visual encoding is shared (**shared encoding views** vs. **multiform views**, where at least one channel differs across views — most saliently, what's mapped to spatial position); how much data is shared (**shared data** where both views show everything — usually paired with multiform encoding to avoid redundancy; **overview-detail** where one view shows a subset selected from the other, often with a **detail-on-demand** popup; and **small multiples**, where each view shows a different partition of the dataset, aligned in a list or matrix so that shared encoding makes spatial position directly comparable); and whether navigation is synchronized (**linked navigation**). A key interactive idiom cutting across encoding choices is **linked highlighting** (a.k.a. **brushing**, a.k.a. **cross-filtering**), where selecting items in one view highlights them with a shared color in all others — this reveals whether a region contiguous in one view is contiguous or dispersed in another. A 2x3 combinations table (Figure 12.6) shows same/different encoding crossed with all/subset/none data sharing; "same encoding + same data" is redundant and "different encoding + no shared data" has no linkage, so those two of six combinations aren't useful.

Partitioning — dividing a multiattribute dataset into meaningful regions — is covered as its own design problem (**partitioning** is the inverse of **grouping**; synonyms include **hierarchical partitioning** and **dimensional stacking**; the statistics term is **conditioning**). Choices include how many attributes/levels to split on (recursively, forming nested regions), the order of attributes used to split, and how many views to use. She distinguishes **region**, **glyph**, and **view** along a size/complexity continuum with no strict dividing line — a **mark** is a single geometric primitive, a **glyph** is a composite object built of multiple marks with internal structure (further split into **macroglyphs**, large enough to read individually, and **microglyphs**, so small their internal structure forms a visual texture rather than being individually legible), and a **view** is a full encoding with its own frame. The grouped-bar-chart vs. small-multiple-bar-chart comparison (12.4.2) shows the same two-level partition interpreted either as glyph size or region nesting: grouped bars interleave the second-level regions within the first, small multiples keep them contiguous, and this changes which comparisons (across-attribute vs. within-attribute) are easy. **Recursive subdivision** (via systems like Trellis and HiVE) shows that changing the order of partitioning attributes, or the arrangement idiom (matrix, treemap-style variable sizing, choropleth), radically changes what patterns are salient — the same recursive split can be read as matrix alignment, containment hierarchy, or a set of nested small multiples.

The **superimpose** family stacks layers in the same coordinate frame (an image-space compositing operation) rather than side by side. Design choices: how many layers, how they're visually distinguished (ideally via non-overlapping channel ranges, e.g., a foreground/background split, with luminance contrast as a key tool — "Get It Right in Black and White," ch. 6.9), and whether layers are **static layers** (all shown simultaneously, viewer shifts attention, as in cartographic layering of roads over land-use area fills) or **dynamic layers** (constructed on the fly from interactive selection, as in Cerebral's hover-driven one-hop neighborhood highlight, or hierarchical edge bundles' three static layers of tree-hierarchy, red-green edges, and nodes). An empirical study (Javed et al.) found superimposed line charts win for local-span comparison tasks (e.g., finding the max at one instant) while juxtaposed small multiples win for global/dispersed tasks (e.g., comparing slopes across the whole series), especially as the number of series grows.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:** 
- Facet is one of the five big strategies for taming complexity (facet, reduce, derive, change, embed) and it's specifically about splitting data across space (views) or depth (layers) rather than showing less of it or showing it over time.
- Juxtaposition vs. superimposition is a real trade-off: display area (external, scarce) vs. visual clutter/distinguishable-layer limits — know when to reach for each.
- Linked highlighting/brushing is the single most generalizable multi-view interaction idiom — it's worth recognizing by name and understanding why it works (revealing spatial contiguity across views).
- The three independent axes for coordinating views — shared encoding, shared data, shared navigation — give a compact vocabulary for describing almost any multi-view system precisely, rather than describing it anecdotally.
- Partitioning order and attribute choice are not cosmetic: the same recursive split, reordered, reveals completely different patterns (HiVE example) — this is a design decision with real consequences, not an afterthought.
- The region/glyph/view continuum (mark to microglyph to macroglyph to full view) is a useful vocabulary for describing "how much stuff is crammed into one visual unit," relevant well beyond this chapter.

## Munzner, "Reduce Items and Attributes" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 13)

**What it is:** A design-choice taxonomy for reducing (or increasing) how much data is shown at once in a view — via **filtering** (eliminating elements) or **aggregation** (merging elements into a derived stand-in) — applied to either items or attributes.

**AI summary:** Reduction is one of Munzner's five major strategies for managing visual complexity (alongside facet, derive, change, embed), and like the others it's not mutually exclusive with the rest. Reduction idioms are named for their reducing action but many are bidirectional — a dynamic parameter that reduces data at one setting can increase it at another. The chapter's core distinction is **filtering** (simply eliminating elements — items or attributes) versus **aggregation** (creating a new derived element that stands in for multiple original ones). She flags a real cognitive trade-off: filtering is straightforward for users to understand and compute, but people have an "out of sight, out of mind" mentality and tend to forget about filtered-out elements even after recent actions; aggregation is safer cognitively because the stand-in element is designed to convey information about the whole group it replaces, but by definition it cannot convey everything that's been summarized — "the challenge with aggregation is how and what to summarize in a way that matches well with the dataset and task."

**Filter** (13.3) is covered for both items and attributes. **Item filtering** eliminates items by their attribute values without changing which attributes are shown; the FilmFinder example demonstrates **dynamic queries** — a tightly coupled loop where interactive widgets (sliders, dual sliders, alpha sliders) immediately update the visual encoding, letting users browse a fully-visible-at-the-start dataset rather than having to formulate an explicit database query that might return nothing or everything. **Scented widgets** augment standard filter controls (sliders, checklists) with small embedded visual encodings — bar/line charts, icons, or channel-coded regions of the widget itself — so that filtering controls carry information scent (a term borrowed from information-foraging theory) about what's behind each choice, using minimal extra screen space. **Attribute filtering** eliminates attributes rather than items, and is often combined with **attribute ordering** (a.k.a. dimensional ordering): if attributes can be ranked by a **similarity measure** (a.k.a. similarity metric) — often based on computing the **variance** of an attribute — then filtering can drop the least/most similar/important attributes, as shown in the DOSFA idiom applied to star plots of a 215-dimension medical text dataset, where raw dense star plots show no structure but ordering-plus-filtering reveals clear patterns.

**Aggregate** (13.4) merges elements into a derived attribute rather than eliminating them; filtering and aggregation can be combined. **Item aggregation** examples include: histograms (binning a quantitative attribute into a derived ordered key attribute plus a derived count, where bin-size choice is "crucial and tricky" since it can radically change the apparent shape of the distribution); continuous scatterplots (solving overplotting/occlusion by computing per-pixel overplot density as a derived attribute and color-encoding it, rather than drawing every item — conceptually distinct from discrete scatterplots despite the similar name); boxplots (a highly scalable aggregation idiom using five derived numbers — median, upper/lower quartiles, upper/lower fences — to summarize an arbitrarily large distribution in a compact glyph; variants like the vase plot add a variable-width dimension to reveal multimodality); SolarPlot (a radial histogram with interactively controllable aggregation level, tied to circle radius); and **hierarchical aggregation** generally, where a clustering hierarchy is computed as derived data and the user interactively controls level of detail (illustrated with hierarchical parallel coordinates, which can scale a normally cluttered idiom to hundreds of thousands of items by drawing proximity-colored cluster bands instead of individual polylines). **Spatial aggregation** gets its own subsection because of the **Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP)**: changing the boundaries of aggregation regions can produce dramatically different visual patterns and analysis results even with the same underlying data and region count — with **gerrymandering** cited as the MAUP instance the general public knows best. The **geowigs** (geographically weighted interactive graphics) example shows a sophisticated response, letting users interactively pick a spatial scale and see geographically-weighted boxplots (gw-boxplots) and means (gw-mean) compared against the global distribution via superimposed layers.

**Attribute aggregation**, covered as **dimensionality reduction (DR)** (synonyms: attribute synthesis, dimensionality reduction), aims to preserve a dataset's meaningful structure while representing items with fewer synthetic attributes, exploiting the assumption that there's hidden structure/redundancy that latent variables could explain. Nonlinear methods are needed when new dimensions can't be expressed as simple combinations of the old ones; **multidimensional scaling (MDS)** is the family of approaches (linear and nonlinear) that minimizes distortion between high-dimensional and low-dimensional distances. A worked example walks a document collection through a **bag of words** transformation (ignoring word order, counting occurrences) into a huge **sparse** **feature vector** table, then down via MDS (the Glimmer algorithm) to a 2D table shown as a color-coded scatterplot, chained across three what-why-how analysis stages (derive the low-D data; explore/verify clusters; annotate with labels) — Figure 13.13 is her worked example of chaining task abstractions. The chapter closes with strong caveats on reading DR scatterplots: only relative distances matter, not absolute position (layouts are typically **affine invariant** — rotation, mirror-reflection, and rescaling preserve equivalent meaning); only large-scale cluster structure should be trusted, not fine-grained distances, since information is necessarily lost in reduction; and empirical studies favor 2D scatterplots or SPLOMs over 3D scatterplots or "3D landscapes" for DR data, since floating point clouds in 3D are "the worst possible case for accurate depth perception" (referencing ch. 6.3).

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- Filter vs. aggregate is a clean, generalizable design fork with a real cognitive trade-off (out-of-sight-out-of-mind vs. inevitable loss of detail) — useful vocabulary for critiquing any dashboard or chart that hides data.
- Dynamic queries (tightly coupled slider-plus-immediate-redraw) are the foundational interaction pattern behind virtually all modern filterable dashboards — worth recognizing the FilmFinder lineage.
- Aggregation is not just "compute an average" — the choice of derived summary (histogram bins, boxplot's five numbers, per-pixel density, cluster hierarchies) determines what signal survives and what gets erased; bin size/threshold choices are analytic decisions, not implementation details.
- The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem is a critical-thinking tool: the same underlying data, aggregated into different spatial regions, can show opposite patterns (directly relevant to any choropleth map or gerrymandering discussion).
- Dimensionality reduction scatterplots are widely used but widely misread: relative position/cluster structure is meaningful, absolute position and precise distances are not, and this is a common misreading to correct explicitly.
- This chapter is the natural anchor for the course's "select subset / summarize somehow" scalability strategies: filter directly maps to "select a subset," and aggregate directly maps to "summarize somehow" — with her explicit maxim that you are always discarding information and must do so with an eye to task.

## Munzner, "Embed: Focus+Context" (*Visualization Analysis and Design*, ch. 14)

**What it is:** A design-choice taxonomy for the **focus+context** idiom family, which embeds detailed focus information within a surrounding context inside a single view — via elision, superimposed local layers, or geometric distortion — as one of Munzner's five strategies for managing visual complexity.

**AI summary:** Focus+context idioms embed detailed information about a selected set (the **focus**) within a single view that also shows overview information about more of the data (the **context**), reducing the amount of data shown through sophisticated combinations of filtering and aggregation. Munzner notes this design choice has an enormous number of synonyms in the literature — bifocal displays, degree-of-interest models, detail in context, distortion-oriented presentations, elastic presentation spaces, fisheye lens, generalized fisheye views, hyperbolic geometry, multifocal displays, nonlinear magnification fields, pliable surfaces, polyfocal projections, rubber sheet navigation, stretch and squish navigation — which she unifies under one framework. The goal is to mitigate the disorientation risk of standard navigation (especially geometric zooming), where only a small part of world space is visible at a time and users must rely on internal memory to maintain orientation; focus+context instead provides contextual landmarks as external memory, in the same nonliteral-navigation spirit as semantic zooming. These idioms can't be understood purely as visual encoding or purely as interaction — they are a synthesis of both, since the focus set changes dynamically as the user interacts, often via indirect control (navigation choices combined with dataset structure).

Three embedding design choices structure the chapter. **Elide** (14.3) omits some items completely (dynamic filtering) while summarizing others via dynamic aggregation for context, showing only focus items in detail. The general reasoning framework is the **degree of interest (DOI)** function, DOI = I(x) − D(x,y), where I is an interest function, D is semantic or spatial distance, x is an item's location, and y is the current focus point (there can be one focus or multiple independent foci). Interest functions typically exploit dataset structure, especially hierarchy — illustrated by DOITrees Revisited, which elides a 600,000-node tree using multiple foci and topological (hop-based) distance, showing elided subtrees as size-coded aggregate triangles. **Superimpose** (14.4) integrates a *local* focus layer (rather than a global one spanning the whole view, as in ch. 12) atop a background context layer — illustrated by Toolglass and Magic Lenses, where a see-through lens shows color-coded Gaussian curvature atop an unmodified 3D scene, revealing that an apparently-smooth sphere is actually a faceted patchwork.

**Distort** (14.5) — the largest section — compresses context regions geometrically to make room for magnified focus regions, all within one view. Four cross-cutting design choices apply to every distortion idiom: number of foci (single vs. multiple), shape of focus region (radial, rectangular, or arbitrary), extent of focus (global across the whole image vs. local), and interaction metaphor (constrained geometric navigation, moveable lenses, rubber-sheet stretching/squishing, or direct manipulation of vector fields). Five examples walk through these choices: **3D perspective** (Cone Trees — single global focus, constrained navigation, intuitive on first encounter but fell from favor as 3D's costs for abstract data became understood); **fisheye lens** (single focus, local radial extent, moveable-lens metaphor, replacing what's beneath it rather than superimposing — mathematically straightforward, GPU-accelerated via vertex shaders); **hyperbolic geometry** (single global radial focus, hyperbolic-translation metaphor — exploits non-Euclidean math to map an infinite plane/volume that grows exponentially, e.g. trees, into a finite Euclidean circle/sphere, achieving higher information density at the cost of partial occlusion in any single frame, as in the H3 3D hyperbolic tree browser); **stretch and squish navigation** (multiple rectangular global foci, rubber-sheet metaphor where stretching one region squishes the rest, item borders stay fixed so all items remain visible within the viewport — illustrated by TreeJuxtaposer for phylogenetic trees and PRISequenceJuxtaposer for gene sequences); and **nonlinear magnification fields** (multiple arbitrary-shape local foci via a general computational framework calculating implicit magnification fields for a desired transformation, supporting lens, stretch, or data-driven magnification-trail metaphors).

A key idiom introduced alongside stretch-and-squish is **guaranteed visibility**: aggregation operating at the subpixel level to ensure important objects stay visible however small they'd otherwise be squished, using an **importance** attribute (original or derived) rather than the poor proxy of camera distance used in realistic 3D rendering — demonstrated in PRISequenceJuxtaposer, where diagnostic red marks in a 16,000-nucleotide-wide gene sequence view stay visible even in heavily compressed regions. The chapter closes (14.6) with an unusually frank cost/benefit assessment: distortion severely impairs distance and length judgments, so it's a poor match for tasks needing precise metric comparison, but works well for node-link network/tree exploration where topology (connection, containment) rather than precise geometry carries the meaning. Costs include users misunderstanding the underlying structure (mitigated by explicit distortion indicators like hyperbolic views showing the enclosing circle/sphere, or magnification fields showing a superimposed grid) and the overhead of maintaining **object constancy** — recognizing that an item seen before and after a transformation is the same object — which gets more costly as distortion severity increases, though constrained/predictable distortion is better tolerated than drastic distortion. Notably, Furnas (the originator of generalized fisheye views) is quoted as surprised at how enthusiastically others embraced *geometric* distortion specifically, arguing that the *what* (selective filtering) matters more than the *how* (any specific distortion idiom) — the fisheye metaphor itself isn't limited to spatial geometry and can apply directly to structured data (e.g., a collapsible/expandable hierarchical document). A four-way comparison (Figure 14.10) of fisheye lens vs. magnifying lens vs. neighborhood-highlighting-via-layering vs. layering-plus-Bring-and-Go interaction on the same graph shows that non-distorting alternatives (layering, selective highlighting) can sometimes outperform geometric distortion for specific tasks like path tracing, since **Bring and Go** moves only the one-hop neighbors of a target node rather than warping the whole layout, minimizing disorientation.

**What a student/VisSnacks reader should get out of it:**
- Focus+context has a huge and confusing vocabulary in the literature (bifocal, fisheye, hyperbolic, rubber-sheet, etc.) — Munzner's embed/elide/superimpose/distort framework is valuable precisely because it unifies these under one set of design axes (number of foci, shape, extent, interaction metaphor).
- The degree-of-interest (DOI) function DOI = I(x) − D(x,y) is a compact, reusable formalism for reasoning about what to show in detail vs. summarize vs. hide — worth knowing by name and structure.
- Distortion is not a free lunch: it measurably impairs distance/length judgment, so match it to tasks about topology/connectivity (trees, networks) rather than tasks needing precise quantitative comparison — a clear design guideline students can apply.
- Object constancy is a real cognitive cost of any transformation-based technique (distortion, but also animation) — students should recognize this as a general principle, not unique to focus+context.
- Furnas's point (what matters more than how) is an important corrective: geometric distortion is only one way to achieve focus+context, and simpler non-distorting alternatives (opacity-based layering, selective highlighting, Bring and Go) sometimes beat it — worth internalizing before reaching reflexively for a fisheye lens.
- This chapter completes the five-way "manage complexity" toolkit (derive, change, facet, reduce, embed) — for the course's challenges/strategies framing, embed is a more sophisticated hybrid of filter+aggregate specifically packaged to preserve context and support orientation, rather than a wholly separate strategy.

- DOI formula: "DOI = I(x) − D(x,y)... I is an interest function; D is the distance, either semantic or spatial; x is the location of an item; y is the current focus point."
- "Focus+context idioms attempt to support orientation by providing contextual information intended to act as recognizable landmarks, using external memory to reduce internal cognitive load."
- On distortion's core weakness: "the problem is that distance or length judgements are severely impaired, so distortion is a poor match with any tasks that require such comparisons."
- On what matters more: Furnas "suggests that the question what is being shown in terms of selective filtering is more central than that of how it is shown with any specific distortion idiom."
- Object constancy defined: "the understanding that an item seen in two different frames represents the same object, just seen from a different viewpoint."
- Canonical examples: DOITrees Revisited (600,000-node tree, multiple foci, topological distance), Toolglass and Magic Lenses (curvature lens revealing a faceted "sphere"), Cone Trees (3D perspective focus+context, fell out of favor), fisheye lens on poker-player dataset, H3 hyperbolic 3D tree browser (file system), TreeJuxtaposer / PRISequenceJuxtaposer (stretch-and-squish + guaranteed visibility on phylogenetic trees and 16,000-nucleotide gene sequences), nonlinear magnification fields (Keahey), Bring and Go network navigation.

- Figure 14.5 or 14.10 (fisheye lens examples, pp.329, 336) — Figure 14.10 in particular is the strongest pedagogical figure in the chapter: four side-by-side approaches (fisheye, magnifying lens, neighborhood-highlight-via-layering, layering+Bring-and-Go) on the identical graph, making the cost/benefit comparison concrete and visual rather than abstract.
- Figure 14.7 (TreeJuxtaposer stretch-and-squish, p.332) — a clean illustration of rubber-sheet distortion applied to real phylogenetic trees, showing both single-region and multiple-region stretching; good for teaching the "borders stay fixed" guarantee.

- Figure 14.9 (nonlinear magnification fields, p.333) — abstract diagrams of magnification fields (grid warping and 3D "landscape" surfaces) that generalize the concept beyond any single tool; useful as a conceptual bridge figure explaining "what a magnification field actually is" independent of any specific interface.

**Caveats:** Nearly all systems examples in this chapter are 1990s-2000s research prototypes (Cone Trees, Toolglass/Magic Lenses, H3, TreeJuxtaposer, DOITrees) with dated screenshots and interaction paradigms (draggable lenses, custom navigation) not common in today's web-based tools — the underlying design axes transfer, the specific systems mostly don't. The chapter's own conclusion (14.6) is notably candid that "the trade-offs of cost and benefits between these five approaches are still not fully understood" as of the book's writing — this is an area of ongoing empirical uncertainty, not settled science, and instructors should present the cost/benefit claims (e.g., about 3D perspective, hyperbolic distortion) as reasonably well-supported but not definitively closed questions. The heavy synonym list in the margin (bifocal, polyfocal, etc.) is useful for literature-searching but can overwhelm students on first pass — worth flagging that the DOI/elide/superimpose/distort framework is Munzner's synthesis, and other authors will use different terms for the same ideas.
